# EXPANSION 161 — The Receipt on the Dock

## A cold-store story about a sealed promise, contested inventory, and records that cannot make the ice safe.

### Wave 30: What a Record Cannot Settle

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 33 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (132 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_cold_store_atlantic` — Atlantic Cold Store.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Atlantic Cold Store is a huge freezer whose flooded floor has frozen beneath a grey sheet of ice. Its location record says the contents are unknown and in principle intact; a separate standing-record memory says forty crates of freeze-dried penicillin sit beneath the ice, while a world-evolution seed assigns ownership to the Cutters and the codex describes a stalemate in which neither faction has moved. This is not a gap to patch with confident prose. The expansion treats the receipt, memory, codex, and live ownership as competing records, and follows the people who must distinguish a label from an inventory. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

When a place has kept a promise longer than its records agree, what can anyone honestly claim is still inside?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The location describes 12,000 cubic metres of freezer, four metres of water frozen over, an ice surface that reads lower, dead roof lights, an underwater loading dock, a hatch on the ice, a 50-rad/hour door reading, and a receipt on the dock. The codex calls the contents unknown and describes both factions knowing about the store while neither has moved. A standing-record memory names forty crates of freeze-dried penicillin in Sub-level 2. A world-evolution seed lists the Cutters as owner. These statements are not reconciled here; any selected text must keep attribution and state context visible.

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The location and codex call present contents unknown; the standing memory records a specific claim; the world-evolution seed lists an owner; the codex describes a stalemate. Do not flatten these into one fact. Preserve the source name, stratum, and condition. The receipt is present in the location description but its contents are not supplied. The underwater sump/copper quest owns its choices and mutations. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The human center is the person who left a receipt on a dock that would later be underwater, and the reader who has to decide what the paper proves. People can protect a store, claim it, or keep away from it for reasons the catalog does not resolve. Let a blank quantity field carry more weight than a false inventory. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Use dock notes, shipping language, and the restrained words of someone who cannot see through the ice. Keep the codex’s eyewitness claim separate from the standing-record stratum. Do not invent the author, shipper, or destination on the receipt. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

A prose variant may recognize that outcome if it is available through the existing state. Reading the receipt does not change ownership or unlock the store. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

### 3. Interpretation — four records

The codex, memory, world-evolution owner, and receipt make different claims. The plan marks each source instead of combining them.

The existing quest concerns flooded sump work and copper coils, not a verified food cache. Keep its purpose separate.

### 5. Choice — the existing salvage branch

The player’s current quest branch may select a callback about the damaged or intact structure if its owner exposes the flag.

A later reader sees the paper’s survival and the same unresolved inventory without receiving a false answer.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
