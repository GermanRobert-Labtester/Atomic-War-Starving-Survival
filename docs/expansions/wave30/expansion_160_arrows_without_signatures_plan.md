# EXPANSION 160 — Arrows Without Signatures

## A subterranean exploration story about contradictory marks, municipal drawings, and the difference between a route and a claim.

### Wave 30: What a Record Cannot Settle

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 33 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (132 candidate passages).

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Utility Tunnel Network has been used as an evacuation route, a hidey-hole, and a shortcut. Its unsigned chalk arrows disagree, its lower sections hold knee-deep mud and chemically tinted water, and cold-blue mold provides just enough light for eyes that have adjusted. A current excavation catalog divides work into three depth bands; a standing-record quest compares drawings from different years and already owns a choice between lighting the tunnel and keeping it dark. This prose expansion follows the people who marked, misread, erased, or left the arrows alone. It does not identify the large sound in the far runs. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

When several generations mark the same corridor for different reasons, which line can a traveler trust—and who gets to say?

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The arrows are unsigned and sometimes contradictory. The location gives a faint chemical tint and says the Hydro Barons call the water harmless while no one believes them; neither claim is confirmed by this plan. Mold is described as bioluminescent. The sound from the far runs stays unidentified. Excavation depth bands and the Chamber 7 choice belong to existing catalogs and are not replaced by these prose drafts. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The human center is the person who adds a mark because someone else may arrive in the dark, and the next person who refuses to trust it without evidence. No one needs to be a villain for an arrow to become wrong: a route changes, the wall is wet, a hand copies an old direction. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Use short field notes, map margins, and two-person exchanges. Avoid a cartographer who knows the entire tunnel. Let the 1982 schematic and 1991 overlay retain their own dates; do not turn either into a perfect map. The unknown sound receives no voice and no name. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 7. Placement and current reachability

Candidate text may accompany an existing inspection, excavation journal, or standing-record quest only after current ownership and state consumers are checked. None is evidence that the location is currently player-reachable. Before selecting any text, verify the current map/location owner, content schema, actual consumer, and the source condition under which the text can appear.

## 8. Player agency

The player may compare the existing drawings, inspect a mark, keep the current lights/dark branch, or turn back under existing game rules. A text passage cannot make the water safe, prove the Barons right, unlock a hidden path, or name what lives in the far runs. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not give instructions for touching live power or gas lines, traversing flooded tunnels, shoring unstable structures, or handling dangerous equipment. Do not identify the distant sound, confirm chemical safety, or make mold a supernatural light. No map in this plan supersedes the current route owner. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Disturbance — the unsigned arrows

Start where two chalk lines point away from each other. The player has to notice that neither carries a name or date.

### 2. Discovery — three uses of the same corridor

The tunnels’ evacuation, hiding, and shortcut histories remain separate periods, not one continuous community.

### 3. Interpretation — incompatible drawings

The existing 1982 schematic and 1991 overlay offer a documented comparison; neither is promoted to universal truth.

### 4. Complication — surface access stripped

The day-15 evolution text changes what can be found at the surface, while deeper conduits remain a separate source.

### 5. Choice — current light/dark branch

The existing standing-record quest owns the selected state. Text can reflect it only after verifying that owner.

### 6. Callback — a line left unsigned

The return leaves one arrow incomplete rather than declaring the tunnel solved.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Small map-margin notes and conversations, no new route discovery.

### Interlinked

Existing excavation, depletion, and light/dark states select text when their current owners expose them.

### Wild card

A reading-order chapter places the 1982 drawing, 1991 overlay, and unsigned chalk side by side without narrating a winner.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
