## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Bunker Is a Body is a local children’s folklore record in bunker_children_folklore_batch_2.json. The Teacher’s analogy says the bunker is a body: air ducts are lungs, water pipes are veins, the generator is a heart, walls are skin, and the hatch is a mouth. The children are called good bacteria, and they extend the metaphor by naming the compost toilet the stomach, the graveyard where the body remembers, and the radio antenna the hair that feels the wind. This plan expands the analogy as shared language, keeping metaphor distinct from anatomy, maintenance authority, or a literal living bunker.

## 2. Story question

When a Teacher’s analogy makes walls into skin, ducts into lungs, and residents into good bacteria, what does a shelter gain and lose when children tell its metaphor as a living story?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the folklore ID and exact prose. It does not establish the Teacher’s name, the hymn or lesson that preceded the analogy, the children who extended it, the bunker’s current systems, or whether every resident accepts the comparison. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing folklore, child, teacher, bunker, dormitory, generator, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local prose: 'The Teacher's analogy, repeated so many times the children have turned it into a story they tell each other: 'The bunker is a body. The air ducts are its lungs, breathing in and out. The water pipes are its veins, carrying blood to every room. The generator is its heart — when it stops, everything stops. The walls are its skin, keeping the cold out. The hatch is its mouth — it opens to eat and speak, and closes to keep the poison out. We are the bacteria that live inside the body. But we are the good bacteria. The kind that keeps the body alive.' The children have extended the metaphor on their own: the compost toilet is 'the stomach,' the graveyard is 'where the body remembers,' and the radio antenna is 'the hair that feels the wind.'' The fuller reading keeps the analogy alive without making it a technical diagram. A child can call a duct a lung and still know that a person breathes differently from a room. The metaphor’s value is relational: it lets residents imagine separate kinds of work as one shared body. Its danger is also relational: if the generator is a heart, a failure can sound like a death, and if residents are bacteria, people can be reduced to a function. A candidate passage may show children arguing whether the body has a fever, an adult reminding them that the metaphor is a story, or a worker accepting one image and refusing another. The record supports shared symbolism, care, and disagreement. It does not establish a body simulation, health state, power rule, or medical reading of the bunker. Keep the children’s extensions attributed, let the compost toilet, graveyard, and antenna remain imaginative additions, and allow the shelter to stay a place made and maintained by people. The body story can return in a room where a worker is repairing one system and a child is listening to another. The child may say the bunker has a stomach because compost is where yesterday becomes soil, while an adult may say the comparison helps only until someone needs a real maintenance decision. That tension is the useful part of the folklore. It lets residents feel connected without pretending that a machine can be comforted by praise or that a person exists to keep the shelter alive. The graveyard as where the body remembers can sit beside the cemetery drawing and open a quiet question about whether memory belongs to walls, people, or both. The radio antenna as hair that feels the wind can remain a child’s image of an outside the bunker cannot touch. A return passage may let the children add a scar, a pulse, or a cough, then let a Teacher decline to approve the new part. The metaphor should grow because children tell it, not because a system requires a new state. Keep the analogy warm, strange, and bounded. The bunker remains built from concrete, water, air, labor, and people who can disagree about what it means.
