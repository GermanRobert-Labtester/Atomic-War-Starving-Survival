## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Bunker Cross-Section is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age ten, makes a technical cross-section of the bunker showing all levels, including the dormitory, kitchen, medical bay, workshop, greenhouse, generator room, water cistern, ventilation system, and blast door. The child uses a ruler, labels the sections in neat handwriting, and produces a drawing the Architect calls remarkably accurate and uses to explain the layout to new residents. This plan expands the page as orientation, inherited structure, and the difference between a clear explanation and a current authority.

## 2. Story question

When a ten-year-old draws every bunker level with a ruler and the Architect uses the page to explain the shelter to newcomers, what does a child’s order reveal about a place adults think they already know?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the child’s name, the exact number of levels, the current condition of any room, the Architect’s teaching method, or whether the drawing remains accurate after later changes. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, bunker, architect, shelter, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "A technical cross-section drawing of the bunker, showing all levels. The drawing includes the dormitory, kitchen, medical bay, workshop, greenhouse, generator room, water cistern, ventilation system, and blast door. The child has used a ruler for the straight lines. Labels are in neat handwriting. The Architect says the drawing is remarkably accurate and has used it to explain the bunker layout to new residents." The fuller reading keeps the drawing useful without making it a replacement map. A cross-section lets a child show relationships adults often experience as separate rooms. The ruler makes straight lines visible as effort, while the labels make the shelter readable to someone who has not yet learned its sounds and turns. The Architect’s use gives the page a public function, but the source does not establish a navigation tool, emergency route, or infrastructure update. A candidate passage may show a newcomer asking where the water cistern belongs in relation to the dormitory, or the Architect pausing at a blank to admit the child’s page does not answer every question. A return can place the drawing beside an official schematic and let each document retain its authority. The bunker is a lived home and a technical structure at once. The artwork can hold both views without turning the child into an engineer of record. Preserve the named rooms and the neat labels, keep future changes outside the page, and let the child’s order help people meet the shelter without pretending the shelter is simple.
