# EXPANSION CW66-01 — A Very Good Worm

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 66: Children’s First Marks and Working Rooms

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_013 — First Drawing.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.
**Scope:** game content only; no production code, JSON data, route, quest, flag, simulation, or save changes.

## 1. Expansion thesis

First Drawing is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A bunker-born child, age four, draws a single wavy red-crayon line across the paper and says, Worm. The Teacher says, That is a very good worm. The child is satisfied. The record identifies this as the child first representational drawing; the Teacher dates and files it, and the child mother has been told and cried. This plan expands the page as first recognition, family witness, and the quiet labor of keeping a beginning.

## 2. Story question

When a four-year-old makes one red wavy line and calls it a worm, what does an adult preserve when praise gives a first picture permission to be real?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the child’s name, the mother’s circumstances, the Teacher’s filing system, the reason for the tears, or what the child draws next. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, teacher, mother, schoolroom, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "A single wavy line across the paper, in red crayon. The child says: Worm. The Teacher says: That is a very good worm. The child is satisfied. This is the child first representational drawing. The Teacher has dated it and filed it. The child mother has been told. She cried." The fuller reading keeps the line small. A first drawing does not need to become a developmental test, a lesson, or a collectible reward. The Teacher’s praise gives the line a social life while the child’s satisfaction gives it an inward ending. The mother’s tears belong to the record and should remain open in meaning: pride, grief, relief, or recognition can coexist without a diagnosis. Dating and filing are acts of care that preserve a moment without claiming to own the child’s future. A candidate passage may show the paper being placed beside other records, a parent receiving the news, or the Teacher refusing to explain why a line is good beyond saying that it is the child’s worm. The red crayon is enough color for the page. The wave is enough anatomy for the claim. The source supports tenderness, witness, and the dignity of a child’s first made meaning. It does not establish a child-development system, family profile, drawing progression, or emotional-care route. Keep the worm exactly as a single line and let the adults respond around it. The first drawing can be placed beside a dated page without making the archive a ladder the child must climb. A future reader may notice that the line is red, that it crosses rather than sits in a corner, or that the word Worm arrives before any adult explanation. The Teacher can keep the page at the child’s height and let the file remain a quiet record rather than a public verdict. The mother’s tears may be remembered by another adult as pride, by the child as weather, or by nobody at all. The prose should not select one answer. A scene may follow the mother receiving the news and choosing to hold the paper without asking the child to draw again. Another may show the Teacher opening the file years later and still hearing the child’s satisfied silence at the edge of the line. The point is not to forecast talent. It is to preserve the first moment when a mark, a word, and an adult’s trust meet. Keep the worm a worm because the child made it one, and keep the page small enough that its importance remains human. The archive can preserve this first image without turning it into a prediction. It may be read by the Teacher, the mother, or a later child, and each reader may find a different kind of beginning in the same red wave. The page does not need a lesson attached to it. The child’s satisfaction already supplies the consequence. If an adult asks what the worm eats, where it lives, or whether it has eyes, the most faithful answer can be that the drawing does not say. The line is not an incomplete scientific diagram; it is a complete first act of representation. A return passage may let the mother place the file beside a family keepsake, or let the Teacher notice that the date has become more important to the adults than to the child. Neither response should take the drawing away from the child. Keep the praise audible, the tears unresolved, and the red line singular. The record is powerful because it does not need more shapes to prove that a life has begun speaking through art. A final quiet reading may leave the file closed after the Teacher says the worm is good. The child remains satisfied, the mother remains moved, and the single red line remains exactly enough.
