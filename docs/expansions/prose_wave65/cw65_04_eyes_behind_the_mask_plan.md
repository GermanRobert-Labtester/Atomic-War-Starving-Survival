## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

## 1. Expansion thesis

Self-Portrait with Gas Mask is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age twelve, draws themselves wearing a gas mask, with only the eyes visible and rendered large and detailed. The child says this is what they look like when they go outside. The Therapist notes that the child has drawn themselves as anonymous because the mask erases identity. The drawing is technically accomplished but emotionally disturbing. This plan expands the portrait as protection, self-recognition, and the pressure of being seen through equipment, without turning the Therapist’s note into a diagnosis or adding safety instruction.

## 2. Story question

When a twelve-year-old draws a self-portrait with a gas mask covering everything except large eyes and says this is what they look like outside, what part of a person survives an assigned face?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the child’s name, the mask model, the outside location, the reason for the mask, the child’s later feelings, or the Therapist’s full assessment. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, gas mask, therapist, outside, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "A self-portrait of the child wearing a gas mask. The face is mostly obscured by the mask — only the eyes are visible. The eyes are drawn large and detailed. The child says: This is what I look like when I go outside. The Therapist notes that the child has drawn themselves as anonymous — the mask erases identity. The drawing is technically accomplished but emotionally disturbing." The fuller reading keeps the portrait close to the child’s sentence. The mask is equipment in the record, but the artwork is about a face deciding how much of itself can remain visible. Large eyes can signal fear, vigilance, concentration, or simply the part the child chose to draw. The Therapist’s language should be treated as an observation with limits. It can invite a quiet conversation about anonymity without labeling the child. A candidate scene may let the portrait sit beside an ordinary name card and make the mismatch visible. The child need not remove the mask in prose for the self-portrait to become personal. A return can show the eyes redrawn, the mask line softened, or the paper folded so that the face is hidden from a passing crowd. These are editorial possibilities, not mental-health outcomes. The source supports dignity, ambiguity, and the human cost of protective identity. It does not provide gas-handling procedures, exposure rules, or a trauma mechanic. Keep the outside unnamed, the eyes specific, and the child larger than the equipment.
