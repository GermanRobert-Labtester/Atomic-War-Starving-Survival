# EXPANSION CW65-06 — The Sentry Who Watches

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 65: Children’s Instrumented Witnesses

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_012 — The Sentry.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Sentry is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age nine, draws a sentry at the periscope from behind, hunched over the eyepiece with one hand on the focus wheel. The room is dark except for a small circle of light around the periscope. The child says he watches so we can sleep. The General says the drawing has been pinned to the sentry station, and the sentries say it makes them feel seen. This plan expands the artwork as anonymous labor recognized from behind, without adding a surveillance procedure or military system.

## 2. Story question

When a nine-year-old draws a sentry from behind at a periscope and says he watches so everyone can sleep, what does a child notice about duty when the person on watch cannot see the people who thank him?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the sentry’s name, the view through the periscope, the General’s reason for pinning the page, the length of a watch, or what the sentries mean by feeling seen. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, sentry, periscope, general, sleep, journal, or narrative consumers. The room is dark except for a small circle of light around the periscope. The child says: He watches so we can sleep. The General says the drawing has been pinned to the sentry station. The sentries say it makes them feel seen." The fuller reading stays behind the sentry because the child’s perspective is the point. The face is absent, but the hunched back, hand, and circle of light make labor visible. The child’s sentence turns an institutional duty into a gift received by sleeping people. A candidate passage may let a sentry read the line and feel gratitude, discomfort, or the distance between a duty and the story told about it. The General’s pinning can be an ordinary act of respect rather than a new recognition system. A return may show the paper under a lamp, another sentry correcting a small detail, or a watcher leaving the drawing untouched because it says enough. The periscope remains an existing object and the outside view stays undescribed. The source supports attention to invisible work, not instructions for observation, targeting, or security. Keep the child’s sleep-centered interpretation beside the sentry’s own silence. The drawing makes a person seen without requiring the face to be shown. The sentry station can hold the page without turning recognition into a command. A watcher may read the child’s sentence during a quiet handover and feel the oddness of being thanked by someone whose face is not in the drawing. Another sentry may say the child has missed the tiredness in the shoulders, while still keeping the page pinned. Those responses give the artwork a life among workers without adding a morale score or a watch schedule. The small circle of light can remain the only visible opening into the outside. The drawing does not need to show what the sentry sees for the child’s idea to matter. A return can let the paper acquire a crease or a thumbprint while the station continues its ordinary duty. The human consequence is recognition that does not reduce the watcher to a heroic emblem. A final reading may leave the page at the station after the watch changes, so the next person receives the child’s sentence before receiving any explanation. Recognition begins as a page left within reach.
