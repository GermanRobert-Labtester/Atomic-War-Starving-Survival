# EXPANSION CW65-05 — When Is the Garden?

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 65: Children’s Instrumented Witnesses

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_011 — The Garden I Want.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Garden I Want is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age seven, draws flowers in red, yellow, and blue, a fruit tree, a vegetable patch, a pond with fish, butterflies, and a blue sky copied from the blue sky drawing. The child says, I want to live here. The Teacher says, This is where we are going. Someday. When the child asks when, the Teacher does not answer. This plan expands the garden as borrowed color, future language, and the ethical weight of an unanswered promise.

## 2. Story question

When a seven-year-old copies the blue from another child’s sky drawing into a garden they want to live in, and the Teacher promises someday without answering when, how does desire keep time?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the garden’s location, whether the Teacher believes the promise, the child’s family, the copied drawing’s maker, or any timetable for going anywhere. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, teacher, garden, sky, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "A drawing of a garden with flowers (red, yellow, blue), a tree with fruit, a vegetable patch, and a pond with fish. The sky is blue (the child has seen the blue sky drawing and copied the colour). There are butterflies. The child says: I want to live here. The Teacher says: This is where we are going. Someday. The child says: When? The Teacher does not answer." The fuller reading keeps the garden as a child’s assembled future rather than a destination the prose can guarantee. The copied blue matters because the child has learned a color through another page and carried it into a place of wanting. The flowers, fruit, fish, and butterflies make the future sensorial without specifying a farm, a route, or a settlement. The Teacher’s someday is a promise-shaped word with no calendar attached. A candidate scene may let the child ask again, let the Teacher answer with care, or let silence reveal that the adult does not know. No answer should manufacture a migration schedule. A return can show the garden drawing gaining a crease, a new color, or a second viewer who recognizes the copied sky. The child’s desire remains valid even if the future remains uncertain. The source supports longing, borrowed knowledge, and honest silence. It does not establish that the bunker will relocate, that the garden is safe, or that the Teacher can deliver it. Keep the question When? alive at the center of the page. The garden page can be read beside the blue sky artwork as a chain of borrowed knowledge. One child has supplied a color; another has supplied a place where that color might someday belong. The Teacher’s silence can be shown in a pause over the paper, a hand that does not turn the page, or a sentence that admits the future has no date. A later reader may ask whether the pond is real, whether the fruit tree has a name, or whether the copied blue looks the same in both drawings. The record does not settle those questions. What it does preserve is a child’s ability to make a home from details gathered elsewhere. Keep the flowers, fish, and butterflies as drawn signs of wanting. Let the absence of a timetable remain a truthful part of the garden rather than a defect in the plan. A final reading may leave the page on the schoolroom wall while the child asks again and the Teacher answers only by naming the colors already present. That restraint keeps the future human and the artwork open.
