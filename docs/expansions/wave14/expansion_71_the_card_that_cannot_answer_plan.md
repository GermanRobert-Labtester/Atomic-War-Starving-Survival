# EXPANSION 71 — THE CARD THAT CANNOT ANSWER
## A Collapsed Command Vault Story Plan
### Wave 14: Authority Without a Witness

**Document type:** prose-first game-content expansion plan  
**Primary location anchor:** Collapsed Command Vault  
**Zone anchor:** Crater Core Ground Zero  
**Status:** proposed narrative content, subject to current source and canon review  
**Wave theme:** records made to speak with authority after the people who could explain them are gone  
**Scope:** authored dialogue, scenes, documents, player choices, and narrative outcomes through current content owners  
**Non-goal:** no new signal, access, combat, hazard, inventory, or save system

## 1. The story in one sentence

A frequency card promises a route into an emergency command vault, but the people reading it cannot ask the card what “entry” was meant to authorize; the player helps them preserve both its usefulness and its limits.

## 2. Creative promise

The player will sit with a group trying to decide what an old emergency instruction can responsibly mean to people who live after its issuing authority is gone. They will hear a radio operator resist turning a signal into a human voice, a planner resist turning the word “emergency” into permission, and a caretaker admit that an open door is not the same as a safe place.

The story is about people and language. It is not a mission to penetrate a blast door. The player’s visible outcome is a careful archive note and a difficult, honest conversation about what the group is prepared to infer.

The player may hope the command vault can help. The story does not punish that hope or reward certainty. It lets the player decide what the group can repeat without claiming a frequency value, a working interface, a current occupant, or a guaranteed recovery.

## 3. Verified local source anchors

The damaged-map zone is Crater Core Ground Zero. It names the hidden installation as Collapsed Command Vault and describes a sub-surface tactical command center beneath pulverized basalt and ionizing fallout, with heavy lead-shielded blast doors.

Its three map fragments are:
- Hypocenter Thermal Survey: an infrared overlay shows reinforced sub-structures surviving under the ground-zero vitrification ring.
- Conduit Routing Diagram: armored high-voltage cable conduits trace from the substation directly into the core blast basin.
- Dead-Hand Frequency Card: a laminated card gives the primary beacon interrogation signal for emergency automated vault entry.

The location record calls it a reinforced military command installation buried beneath blasted granite, with three distinct strata leading to a hardened communications vault. The expedition record has danger 7, distance 14 ticks, encounter chance 0.185 per tick, stamina drain 3.05 per hour, and a dead-hand-core loot table. These are separate authorities and are not guarantees of discovery, access, safety, or contents.

## 4. Current map and reveal boundary

The world-map node for loc_excavation_command_vault is currently locked, not discoverable at start, and not starting-unlocked. Current cartography records describe its reveal through the existing damaged-map chain. This story must not pre-reveal the node, draw a route, bypass the fragment gate, or state that the player has entered the vault.

The plan may be delivered after an existing source fragment or reveal event has made the story available. If no supported narrative route exists at that point, use a current journal or dialogue owner and report the gap. Do not create a parallel quest system to force the scenes into the game.

The content can be understood entirely from the artifacts and conversations. It does not require physical access to the location or a successful response from the beacon.

## 5. The evidence boundary around “Dead-Hand”

The exact local fragment label is “Dead-Hand Frequency Card.” Its description says it gives the primary beacon interrogation signal for emergency automated vault entry. The description does not provide a numeric frequency, a spoken message, a response, a set of code digits, a current power status, or the condition of an entry mechanism.

The phrase “Dead-Hand” also appears in other project content with different orbital, command, radio, and emergency meanings. This plan refers only to the Crater Core map fragment. It does not connect the card to an orbital strike protocol, missile directive, satellite ping, nuclear response, or unrelated radio transcript.

Any dialogue that speculates about those meanings must label the idea as a character’s guess and leave the archive record unchanged.

## 6. What the card can and cannot establish

The card establishes that a record labels itself as an emergency beacon interrogation signal for automated vault entry. It is evidence that someone prepared or cataloged an instruction with that stated purpose.

By itself, the card does not establish who authored it, when the instruction was valid, which beacon it addressed, whether the beacon remains powered, whether the door can respond, who was authorized to use it, what the response would mean, or what lies beyond the door.

Those limits are not a verdict against the card. They are the terms under which the group can use it without turning a historical description into a current command.

## 7. The human conflict

The immediate dispute begins when one person says, “The card gives us a way in,” and another answers, “It gives us a signal to ask.” A third person hears the word “emergency” and asks whose emergency counts.

No one in the present group knows the original operators. No one can identify a person behind the system. The conflict is not between a trusting character and a foolish skeptic; everyone is trying to protect someone from a different kind of loss.

One character fears the group will waste a rare chance by refusing to try. Another fears a dead machine could be treated as a promise. The player helps them record what the card says and what the group cannot yet ask.

## 8. The story’s emotional center

Mara Venn has lived through two evacuation notices that arrived after the people they were meant to protect had already moved. She does not tell that story to discredit the card. She tells it because an instruction can remain legible after the conditions that made it humane have disappeared.

Tovin Hask keeps the group’s radio notes. He knows the difference between a carrier, a signal identity, and a sentence. He worries that a frequency-shaped clue will invite people to invent a voice in the static.

Ilse Renn prepares expeditions. She wants the card to be useful because the group needs to plan. She is also the person most likely to be blamed if people hear “emergency entry” as “safe entry.”

Ren Tal keeps source records and asks the player to preserve the card’s wording without completing the gaps. The supporting cast names are new proposals and require canon collision review.

## 9. Character: Mara Venn

Mara is a practical caretaker who believes emergency language should name the people it protects. She asks questions in the order that a tired person might ask them: “Who is this for?”, “What happens if no one answers?”, “Does the signal open the door, or only tell the system to listen?”

Mara never claims that the card was written for her family, district, or community. Her past evacuations are present-day authored background and must not be woven into an existing faction’s official history without approval.

Her voice is plain and emotionally direct. When others use “protocol,” she asks for the sentence they would say to a frightened person standing by the gate.

## 10. Character: Tovin Hask

Tovin maintains ordinary radio equipment and writes down observations for the shelter. He is not a cryptographer and does not decode the card. He can explain that “interrogation signal” sounds like a signal that addresses a system, but he cannot infer the response, authorization rules, or a spoken operator.

Tovin’s own rule is:
> “A receiver can hear something without understanding it.”
>
> “And a person?”
>
> “A person can understand the wrong thing very well.”

He does not handle a working command-vault beacon in this story. His expertise is used to keep the language honest, not to create a new radio mechanic.

## 11. Character: Ilse Renn

Ilse coordinates the group’s existing expedition planning. She understands that any future trip has real cost and danger. She is tired of being asked to translate uncertainty into a yes or no before the evidence supports either answer.

Ilse does not start an expedition through this expansion. Her role is to keep logistical questions visible while refusing to treat the card as a route authorization or reward table.

She says:
> “If we go, we go because we chose to take the risk.”
>
> “And the card?”
>
> “The card can tell us what somebody once prepared. It cannot make the choice for us.”

## 12. Character: Ren Tal

Ren keeps the group’s archive and has learned that annotations can become the next generation’s source if they are not labeled. Ren writes “as cataloged” beside the old description and “our reading” beside the new note.

Ren is not neutral about emergency systems. They believe a system made for crisis still deserves scrutiny when its original stewards are absent. Their care is visible in method: keep the old words, name the current question, do not let one page impersonate both.

Ren’s signature is attached only to present-day notes. They are never made the author of the original frequency card.

## 13. Relationship map

Mara and Ilse agree that people need to prepare but differ on how soon a possibility should shape a plan. Tovin and Mara agree that a signal is not a voice, but Mara keeps asking what a person could reasonably hear in an emergency. Ren respects Tovin’s restraint and challenges him when caution becomes silence.

The player is not asked to choose a winner. Their choices affect the wording of the archive note and which person receives the last word in the closing scene. The underlying map state, route state, expedition availability, and signal system do not change.

## 14. Narrative arc

**Act I — The card arrives.** The group hears the card described as a way in. Ren lays out the exact source wording and asks the player to read it.

**Act II — The words are tested.** Characters distinguish a beacon from a person, emergency from permission, and a stated purpose from a current capability.

**Act III — The note travels.** The player helps decide what the group can repeat to people who were not at the table.

**Act IV — The question remains.** The group preserves the instruction as evidence and writes the unanswered questions beside it. No door opens in the story.

## 15. Opening scene: the phrase “way in”

The player enters while Ilse is asking how the card affects an existing planning conversation. Mara says it is a way in. Tovin replies that the description says it gives an interrogation signal, not that it operates a door.

Mara answers:
> “If the system expects a signal before entry, what else would you call it?”
>
> “A question to the system.”
>
> “And if it answers?”
>
> “Then we would have an answer.”
>
> “You say that like the answer would be a person.”
>
> “No. I say it like we do not have one yet.”

Ren asks the player to sit with them before the phrase settles into the group’s notes as a fact.

## 16. Opening player choice: what did the player hear?

The player can ask Mara why she called it a way in, ask Tovin what “interrogation” means to him, or ask Ilse what decision she needs to make. Each route adds a different human stake; none reveals hidden functionality.

Mara says she heard a possibility and wanted the group to take it seriously. Tovin says he heard a signal to a machine and does not know what the machine does. Ilse says she needs accurate information but has no current expedition decision to report.

Ren records none of these statements as source facts. If the player asks Ren directly, they say:
> “First we should know which words were on the card and which ones we brought with us.”

## 17. Artifact reading: the survey

Ren places the Hypocenter Thermal Survey summary first. It describes reinforced sub-structures that survived beneath the ground-zero vitrification ring. The group reads “survived” as a statement about structures in the overlay, not as proof of occupants, working power, clean air, or accessible rooms.

Mara asks whether anything surviving can still help someone. Ren answers:
> “It can. The map does not say it will.”
>
> “That sounds like the whole story.”
>
> “It is the first line of it.”

The scene shows the survey as one source among several. The player may ask to keep “surviving sub-structures” in the note, but not replace it with “intact command vault.”

## 18. Artifact reading: the conduit diagram

The second artifact traces high-voltage armored cable conduits from a substation directly into the core blast basin. Tovin says a line drawn on a diagram can show intended routing without proving that the cable still carries power.

Mara asks what it would take to know. Tovin says inspection by the proper current system and a safe route would answer different parts of the question. He does not propose a test or describe how to enter.

> “You could still have a cable.”
>
> “Yes.”
>
> “And it could still carry power.”
>
> “Yes.”
>
> “Then why not say so?”
>
> “Because this line only tells us where somebody drew the cable.”

The player may preserve that sentence as a present-day annotation.

## 19. Artifact reading: the frequency card

Ren reads the card’s current catalog description aloud:
> “A laminated card giving the primary beacon interrogation signal for emergency automated vault entry.”

The exact card face and frequency value are not reproduced in this plan because the verified local description does not provide them. If an approved game asset later reveals a value, the narrative owner must check its current content authority and context before adding it to dialogue.

Mara asks:

>
>Tovin replies:

>
> “It says emergency.”
>
> “Yes. It does not say whose.”

## 20. First annotation choice

Ren asks the player how to summarize the card. Options:
- “A signal for entry.”
- “An emergency signal for an automated vault.”
- “A card described as giving the beacon interrogation signal.”
- “A possible way in, purpose and current operation unverified.”

Ren explains that the third is the narrowest source-based wording. The fourth can work as a present-day summary if the phrase “possible way in” is explicitly a character’s reading. The first is too strong, and the second loses the distinction between a signal and a result.

The preferred archive line is:
> The card is described as giving a primary beacon interrogation signal for emergency automated vault entry. Current operation and authorization are not established by this fragment.

## 21. The card is not a command

Mara says a person hearing “emergency entry” could reasonably read it as permission. Ren asks who would be granting that permission now. The table goes quiet.

Ilse says:
> “An old procedure might have had an authority.”
>
> “Then name it,” Mara says.
>
> “We cannot.”
>
> “Then the procedure is written down, but the authority is not here.”
>
> “That is what I am trying to say.”
>
> “Say that, then.”

The conversation does not establish that the procedure has expired or that it was invalid. It makes the current absence of an identified authority visible.

## 22. Tovin explains the word “interrogation”

Tovin says the word can describe a signal sent to a system to request a status or response. In the card description, the exact exchange is not supplied. He refuses to fill it in with a familiar radio pattern or a guessed handshake.

Mara asks whether the system might recognize whoever sends it. Tovin says the card description does not say. Ilse asks if it might open the vault. Tovin says the words “for entry” identify the stated purpose, but still do not show whether it works now.

Tovin’s line:
> “Purpose is a reason somebody wrote. Operation is something happening. We have the first in this description, not the second.”

## 23. Branch: the player calls it a key

If the player calls the card a key, Ren asks whether they mean a physical credential, a signal, or an instruction. The player may clarify:
- “A source of instructions.”
- “A signal credential.”
- “A key in the broad sense.”
- “I am guessing.”

Ren records the clarification, not the first shorthand. If the player insists that the card is a literal key, Tovin points out that the description identifies a signal and does not describe a physical door key.

No physical key item appears, no interaction prompt is created, and the card is never inserted into a new system.

## 24. Branch: the player says the card is useless

Mara objects to “useless.” The card has already told them that someone documented a signal and its stated purpose. The player can accept that limited value, say the information is incomplete, or insist the group should discard it.

Ren does not destroy the source. If the player argues for discarding it, Ren offers to mark it “not currently actionable from this description” while keeping it in the archive.

Mara’s response is:
> “I do not need it to open the door before it can matter.”
>
> “What does it give you?”
>
> “A question we can ask without pretending we know the answer.”

## 25. Branch: the player says emergency settles the matter

Ilse asks whether the word “emergency” itself authorizes anyone to act. If the player says yes, she asks whose emergency and who decides. If the player says no, Mara asks whether a life-saving procedure should be ignored because its author is gone.

The scene never resolves the question with a legal rule. The player may choose:
- “Emergency explains urgency, not authority.”
- “If someone is in danger, the present people must decide.”
- “The card does not identify who may use it.”
- “I do not know.”

Ren keeps the archive note constant. These choices shape the closing dialogue and the player journal’s personal margin.

## 26. Ilse's planning pressure

Ilse tells the player that the site is a dangerous destination in current expedition data. She will not turn those figures into a sermon, but she asks people not to hear an old emergency instruction as a safety guarantee.

> “A route can be possible and still be a bad choice.”
>
> “Is this route possible?”
>
> “The map has its own answer. This card does not answer that.”
>
> “Then what are we deciding?”
>
> “How much uncertainty we carry when we plan.”

No trip begins here. If a later authorized route is available, the existing expedition system remains the sole owner of travel and risk.

## 27. Scene: a remembered alarm

Mara recalls an evacuation bell that continued after the building had emptied. She does not say it was this system, this vault, or this time. The memory belongs to her own life and is clearly presented as a personal comparison.

>

Tovin says a machine can keep speaking after its audience has moved, or stay silent while people still wait. The memory makes the present question felt without creating a history for the command vault.

## 28. Scene: no voice is heard

The scene includes a short pause in which the group listens to the camp radio. There is ordinary static, but no intercepted message, voice, beacon response, or recognizable pattern. The audio should use the existing ambient or radio presentation only if one already fits.

Mara asks:
> “Did you hear someone?”
>
> Tovin replies:
> “I heard the receiver.”
>

The silence does not confirm that the vault is unpowered or unoccupied. It simply gives the characters a moment to feel the difference between listening and receiving an answer.

## 29. Scene: Ren's margin

Ren drafts a margin note:
> No response is included in the reviewed fragment. A described signal purpose is not evidence of current operation.

Mara says it sounds colder than it is. Ren asks for a warmer version that does not overclaim. The player may propose:
> We do not know whether the beacon still answers.
or:
> The card tells us what someone hoped the signal would do, not what it does now.

Ren accepts either as dialogue or personal margin, but retains the narrow source note in the archive.

## 30. End of Act I

The group now has a shared transcription and a clearer disagreement. They know what the card is described as doing. They do not know the signal value, the response, the present operation, who had permission, or whether anyone is behind the door.

Mara has made the human need visible. Tovin has refused to invent an exchange. Ilse has separated planning from promises. Ren has made the source and annotation legible.

The next act explores how the word “automated” changes what the characters imagine: a helpful steward, a locked procedure, a machine waiting for a voice, or a device that may no longer know why it was built.


## 31. Act II: what “automated” makes the group imagine

The group writes four possible readings of “automated vault entry” on separate slips:
- A machine may control part of an entry procedure.
- A beacon may respond to a signal.
- The card may belong to a larger protocol.
- The phrase may be a catalog description whose operational details are elsewhere.

Ren keeps every slip attributed as an interpretation. The player may remove one they believe is too speculative, but no interpretation becomes a source fact.

Tovin says:
> “We can ask what the words suggest. We cannot say the machine is waiting because the words feel like waiting.”

## 32. Mara imagines a caretaker

Mara says she pictured the system as a caretaker that could recognize a distress signal and decide to open the vault. She knows that is an image, not evidence, but she is reluctant to let it go.

Ren asks why the image matters. Mara replies:
> “Because a caretaker is someone you can ask.”
>
> “And a machine?”
>
> “A machine is someone people put the asking into.”
>
> “Does that mean it answers?”
>
> “No. It means somebody once hoped it would.”

Her image is not taken as a fact about the installation. The scene lets hope have texture while keeping it distinct from a system description.

## 33. Tovin names the difference

Tovin does not tell Mara that her image is foolish. He asks whether a person wrote the card to describe a machine or to tell a person what to do. The group cannot know from the short catalog line.

> “It says beacon.”
>
> “Yes.”
>
> “It says interrogation.”
>
> “Yes.”

>

The question is preserved as a question. No human operator is invented behind the beacon.

## 34. Ilse questions the word “primary”

Ilse asks if “primary” means there were secondary signals. Ren says the word could imply a hierarchy, but the record does not list a secondary signal or explain the system’s design.

The player may say:
- “Primary means first.”
- “Primary means preferred.”
- “Primary may be a label.”
- “We do not know what it contrasts with.”

Ren records the last as the archive’s safest annotation. The first three remain possible interpretations in dialogue.

## 35. The group does not invent a backup

Mara wonders if a backup signal existed. Tovin says it is a sensible question and not an answer. Ilse says they should not write “backup unavailable” because no backup is named in the reviewed material.

Ren writes:
> Other signal paths: not described in this fragment.

That sentence does not mean no other path existed. If new source evidence later supplies one, it should be added with provenance. The group’s current note remains bounded by what has been reviewed.

## 36. The group does not invent a recipient

Ilse asks who was meant to carry the card. The artifact description says the card gives a signal, but no user or role is named in the verified record.

Mara says maybe it was for a duty officer. Tovin says maybe it was kept at a nearby station. Ren labels both as possibilities. The player can choose to keep the question “Who was meant to carry this?” on the follow-up card.

No character is identified as the old card’s original owner, holder, or operator.

## 37. Proposed present-day source card

This is a newly authored archive card, not an artifact recovered from the command vault:

> SOURCE: DAMAGED MAP FRAGMENT — DEAD-HAND FREQUENCY CARD
>
> Catalog description: laminated card giving the primary beacon interrogation signal for emergency automated vault entry.
> Signal value: not reproduced in the reviewed description.
> Author, date, intended operator, receiving system, and current status: not established here.
> “Dead-Hand” is retained as the fragment label only.

The source card must remain visibly a present-day note. Do not style it as a pre-war instruction or give it an invented stamp, authority seal, or security classification.

## 43. Branch: the player distrusts the automation

Ren keeps “occupancy not established by the reviewed fragment” as the archive wording. The personal conversation may differ. If the player chooses “reason enough to care,” Mara thanks them without claiming that anyone is present.

## 48. Scene: the conduit line

Tovin traces the conduit on the diagram with a finger and stops before the cable reaches the vault. The source says the armored line runs from the substation into the core blast basin; it does not certify the final connection, power, or function.

## 49. Scene: the survey carries a shadow

Mara asks what “surviving” means on an infrared overlay. Ren says the catalog description says reinforced sub-structures survive beneath a ring. It does not say whether a particular room, a person, or any equipment survives.

Ilse notes that the word may mean a structure remained visible after the event represented by the survey. That is a character interpretation, not an official timestamp. The player can choose “surviving according to the overlay” for the archive summary.

No thermal level, isotope reading, blast date, or safe approach is added.

## 50. Scene: the emergency belongs to the present

Mara asks whether a present emergency can inherit an old word. Ilse says the group can decide what it must do now, but it cannot make the old card speak for every future person.

> “If someone needs help, we cannot wait for a perfect record.”

>
> “Then why keep asking what the word meant?”

>

This exchange keeps urgency and consent in tension. It does not conclude that an emergency procedure is morally invalid.

## 51. The player’s question to Mara

The player may ask whether Mara would use the signal if a person were known to be in danger. She says she cannot answer without knowing what sending it would do, who would receive it, or what response might follow.

>
> “You would?”

This is a powerful personal distinction: refusing to precommit is not the same as refusing to help.

## 52. The player’s question to Tovin

The player may ask if Tovin would trust a response from an automated system. He says he would listen to it, record what it said, and still ask what authority it had.
