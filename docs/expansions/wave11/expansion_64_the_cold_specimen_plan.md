# EXPANSION 64 — THE COLD SPECIMEN
## A Materials Research Sublevel Story Plan
### Wave 11: The Work Between Storms

**Format:** prose and game-content expansion proposal  
**Primary site:** loc_materials_research_sublevel, University Quarter  
**Primary question:** What does a single test prove, and what does it leave unknown?  
**Proposed scale:** approximately 120,000 characters of authored implementation-ready narrative  
**Scope:** one self-contained expedition story, with records, dialogue, encounters, optional testimony, and two truthful resolutions  
**Status:** draft content plan; not an implementation claim or a new feature authority

---

## 1. One-sentence premise

At a sealed materials lab, the player finds winter-test records for a composite sleeve that held one cable joint through a controlled cold cycle and failed under a different bend; the expedition team must decide how to describe the result to people who need useful repairs but cannot afford a confident lie.

The story does not ask the player to discover a miracle material, restore a research program, or operate a new crafting system. It asks the player to distinguish a sample from a product, a passed test from a field guarantee, and an engineer’s caution from cowardice.

## 2. Short pitch

The University Quarter’s Materials Research Sublevel was built to make small claims repeatable. Specimens had numbered faces. Instruments carried calibration labels. A technician’s hand could hold a sample in a clamp for a measured time, then file both the result and the uncertainty. The facility is now cold, flooded in one corridor, and missing most of its staff. Some storage drawers remain dry behind hydraulic security doors. Others have warped enough to trap their labels.

An expedition reaches the site seeking useful stock. They find a test book whose headline says “PASS — 6 HOUR COLD SOAK.” The next page documents a failure at the cable entry under a repeated bend. A carbon copy suggests someone later shortened the summary to “COLD PASS” before it went to a field crew. The test engineer did not call the material useless. The technician did not call it safe. The maintenance crew needed to decide what they could repair before weather closed the route.

The player finds two ways to help the people still living with that decision. They can preserve the narrow result and the failed condition together, making the archive slower to use but harder to misuse. Or they can make an abbreviated, plain-language note that says exactly where the specimen held and where it did not. A third temptation—calling the sleeve a proven winter fix—can be challenged by the surviving witnesses and corrected before the expedition leaves.

The story’s human center is not an argument about who was right. It is the exhausted work of keeping a useful observation from turning into a promise nobody made.

## 3. What this plan is and is not

This is a narrative expansion built from the current world’s existing materials research location, expedition route, specimen-focused setting, and established industrial item vocabulary. It proposes authored prose and encounters for a future content review. Its output is a record set, several voices, and a player-facing question about evidence.

It does not establish a material catalog, add recipes, create durability statistics, rebalance equipment, add a research bench, or make the player manufacture composite stock. Existing item data remains the sole authority for any items already present. The test result is a story fact, not a reusable crafting rule.

This plan also does not imply that every readable result needs a feature. The route may remain a normal expedition. The lab can remain a location with its present travel, danger, radiation, loot, and access state. The content should attach through the current narrative and encounter contracts identified during promotion.

## 4. Evidence and site fit

The current location catalog describes the Materials Research Sublevel as an advanced metallurgy and composite test facility behind hydraulic security doors, with centrifuges, autoclaves, specimen lockers, specialist reagents, and composite stock. The active data names the node loc_materials_research_sublevel. Its current danger, travel, and radiation values are already authored in the location record. The expedition profile routes through existing map and encounter authority, and the map identifies the site in the University Quarter’s damaged zone.

The plan uses these facts as physical atmosphere: old pressure doors, test equipment, specimen cabinets, reagent labels, and composite offcuts. It does not convert them into new gameplay capabilities. The present expedition data references an existing loot table whose name points to an observatory. That table is a content source reference, not evidence that this laboratory is an observatory; the story never describes observatory rooms or observatory staff.

The location has limited unique authored narrative compared with its detailed industrial description. That gap makes a contained records story a good fit. The proposed test is fictional and local. It does not claim a real-world material property, copy real research, or borrow a recognizable institution or product.

## 5. The theme: confidence has a shape

The story’s recurring image is a sleeve around a joint. The sample had a length, a wall thickness, two layers, a seam, and a direction in which its edge faced the clamp. “It passed” loses those details. “It failed” loses them too. The question is never whether the sample passed or failed in the abstract. The question is what exact shape of evidence can be carried to somebody who has to decide what to do next.

Every major scene should return to one concrete distinction:

- material stock is not a finished replacement;
- a coupon specimen is not a cable assembly;
- a cold soak is not a winter route;
- an unbroken sleeve is not a live joint;
- a handwritten copy is not an acknowledgment;
- caution is useful only when it leaves enough information to act.

The motif must stay physical. Characters touch a split seam, hold a chart against a lamp, or turn a sample so the failed edge faces the light. The narrative should not become an essay in which everyone repeats the theme directly.

## 6. Main cast

### 6.1 Leena Oris — expedition materials conservator

Leena served as a storekeeper and sample preparer before the collapse. Her title came later, after most of the formal staff were gone. She is skilled at identifying adhesives by odor and handling, recognizes the difference between a storage coupon and an installed part, and refuses to claim she can reconstruct a missing test from a label. Her first response to the player is practical: “Keep the seam away from the wet strap.”

Leena’s flaw is that she treats uncertainty as something she can carry alone. She often leaves out the human reason for a decision because she thinks the measurement will be enough. Her arc is not to become more optimistic. It is to tell the group why the uncertain sample mattered to her personally.

### 6.2 Daro Fen — cable-maintenance survivor

Daro repaired short runs between shelter rooms and the old tram substation. He was not a member of the lab staff; he arrived with a field crew and helped install a temporary sleeve around a damaged joint. The report says the sleeve was “not authorized for route use.” Daro says the crew used it for one night because the alternative was leaving a pump disconnected.

He is not ashamed of the repair, but he knows the distinction between keeping one local connection alive and certifying a route. His anger comes from hearing the one-night decision repeated later as proof that the sleeve was safe everywhere. Daro’s dialogue must convey skill without making him an infallible mechanic.

### 6.3 Asha Neri — archive keeper at the relay shelter

Asha keeps paper records at the relay shelter and tries to ensure future crews can find them. She wants a short summary because the shelter archive is crowded and the next crew is usually cold, tired, and in a hurry. She has seen long caution notes ignored when they begin with an apology.

Her conflict is useful: brevity is not necessarily dishonesty. She believes a good label should tell a reader what to do first. She will accept a longer appendix if its first line gives a safe, useful boundary.

### 6.4 Oren Vale — former test-room operator, absent

Oren’s voice survives through signed test sheets, margin notes, and a short voice cylinder. He was not the person who designed the specimen. He operated one cold chamber and maintained its clamp. His notes are precise but not theatrical. He wrote about condensation on the clamp, a missing washer, and the time a chart recorder stopped. He never claims to know why the sleeve split beyond the failure surface he could see.

Oren’s absence must not turn him into a hidden villain or a saint. He was one careful worker in a damaged institution.

### 6.5 Nima Sol — former batch clerk, on the evacuation route

Nima handled specimen labels and carbon copies. She wrote the phrase “COLD PASS” on a transport slip to fit a small field, then regrets that the short phrase traveled farther than the full report. She did not falsify the test. She shortened it. That distinction matters but does not erase the effect.

Nima’s voice can be recovered through records even if the player never meets her. If the encounter route includes her in person, she is encountered on a later stretch of the University Quarter route, not magically waiting in the laboratory. Her line is: “I wrote the small words because the box was small. I should have asked what else would be cut away.”

### 6.6 Kest Arno — route runner and practical skeptic

Kest carries messages between the relay shelter and several small settlements. They want labels that fit on a strip tied to a part. They distrust archive language because they have watched crews spend an hour arguing about a note while a door leaked. They are not anti-record. Their test for any wording is: can a tired person use it without guessing?

Kest challenges both the engineer’s long form and the clerk’s short form. Their pressure makes the resolution concrete.

## 7. Supporting voices and witnesses

These voices are optional encounters, notes, or recalled testimony. They should be distinct enough that an attentive player can identify the person speaking without a nameplate.

- **Ivo Marek, chamber electrician:** speaks in clipped observations and always says which dial he watched.
- **Sela Venn, night cleaner:** remembers where water pooled, not what the apparatus was called.
- **Bren Tal, stores assistant:** talks in shelf positions and quantities, then corrects himself when a box count is uncertain.
- **Tavi Ren, field medic:** recalls the pump noise changing pitch during the one-night repair.
- **Kira Sol, courier:** repeats the phrase she was given and admits she never saw the full report.
- **Merrit Oss, shelter cook:** remembers who came back wet, who ate, and who would not sit down.
- **Nima Sol, batch clerk:** distinguishes “shortened” from “changed,” and is willing to say the shorter wording still caused harm.
- **Leena Oris:** names a physical detail before giving an interpretation.

No optional witness should be treated as a perfect narrator. Each person knows a different slice: room conditions, a sound, a label, a route, or an effect on the crew.

## 8. Story spine

The expedition begins with a practical request: recover a dry sample sleeve and any intact installation note from the sublevel. A local shelter has a cable joint that must be inspected before the next cold front. The player is asked to bring back evidence, not to make a repair in the lab.

Inside the facility, the party finds a sample card labeled “CS-14 / cold chamber / six hours.” The card is clean enough to read. It is not the full result. A nearby test book records that the sleeve remained intact during the cold soak, then split near its seam under a repeated bend. A second note says the chamber clamp did not match the geometry of the field joint. The report concludes that the sample’s low-temperature behavior is promising for further testing, but the assembled joint is not qualified for route use.

The short transport slip says only “COLD PASS.” The carbon impression shows that it was written after the test report, on a line reserved for shipment description. The paper traveled to the relay shelter with an offcut. Daro’s field crew used that offcut once to seal a local joint. It held long enough for the pump to run through one night. The full account, including the failed bend, never reached the crew in time.

The player retrieves the remaining evidence and meets the survivors’ disagreement about how to present it. Leena wants all pages kept together. Asha wants a clear label at the front. Kest wants a practical boundary. Daro wants the one-night repair remembered without being turned into a guarantee. The final scene lets the player write a short field card and choose which supporting records remain attached.

The ending makes the archive usable. The sample remains a sample. The one-night repair remains a real act that helped people. The failure remains part of the record.

## 9. Scene sequence overview

1. **Request at the relay shelter:** retrieve the sample sleeve and the full test note; a party member says the next crew needs a concise answer.
2. **University Quarter approach:** weather and damaged streets complicate travel; a small encounter introduces the danger of wet paper and improvised wrapping.
3. **Hydraulic threshold:** access is slow; Leena identifies the door’s stored pressure and refuses to pry against the specimen cabinet.
4. **Specimen corridor:** shelves carry tactile descriptions, labels, and old room sounds.
5. **Test room:** the “PASS” sheet is visible before the failure page, creating a deliberate reading order.
6. **Clamp bench:** a broken washer shows why the apparatus condition belongs in the report.
7. **Records alcove:** the short transport slip and carbon copy connect to the relay shelter.
8. **Optional evidence rooms:** witnesses and context broaden the account without withholding the basic truth.
9. **Return route:** another survivor reports the one-night pump repair in plain language.
10. **Archive table:** the player composes a short label from verified statements.
11. **Reading scene:** Leena, Daro, Asha, and Kest each respond to the wording.
12. **Closeout:** the archive records the limits of the test and leaves all present equipment authority unchanged.

## 10. Opening scene: the relay shelter

A metal table has been dragged away from the wall. On it lie a length of cable, three sleeves made from different scraps, and a folded map weighted with a cup. Asha Neri has pushed the cups close to the table’s center so no one can knock them over. One contains boiled water. The other contains dry sand.

Asha asks the player to recover a particular sample from the Materials Research Sublevel. The shelter has not asked for a new recipe or an automatic repair. The cable joint near the west pump has begun to seep when it cools. The team wants to know whether the lab’s composite sleeve was ever tested on a joint shaped like that one.

Asha’s first explanation is interrupted by Kest, who taps the cable end with one fingernail.

> Kest: If the label says it passed, I need the next line. Passed what?
>
> Asha: That is why we need the whole report.
>
> Kest: Then ask for the whole report. Don’t call it a repair part before we know.

Daro Fen sits at the far end of the bench, drying a glove by turning it inside out. He recognizes the location but not the specimen number. He says a field crew carried an offcut from the university site years ago. It helped for one night. It was never fitted to a permanent line.

Leena Oris offers to lead the expedition. She says she can identify the sample by its seam and edge, but she cannot promise that an old label still matches the piece in the drawer. The request is specific: bring back the intact specimen if it can be removed safely, photograph or transcribe the full test conditions using the existing journal interaction, and record any field note that travels with it.

If the player asks whether the sleeve could fix the current pump, Asha says she does not know. Daro says one of them held overnight. Kest says an overnight repair is still not a winter route. The conversation establishes three valid kinds of knowledge before the expedition begins.

### 10.1 Optional dialogue at the request table

**Player:** “If one held, why not try it again?”

> Daro: We did. One night, one joint, one wrap we could inspect by touch before dawn. It held enough to run the pump. I will stand by that much.
>
> Leena: We cannot lift that sentence out of its conditions.
>
> Kest: We can use it. We just cannot stretch it.

**Player:** “Could the lab have approved it?”

> Asha: Approval is a word for a document. We have a page that says what was measured.
>
> Daro: I was not waiting for a stamp when the line broke.
>
> Asha: I know. I am not asking the paper to judge you.

**Player:** “What should I bring back?”

> Leena: The sample, if it is intact. The pages that say what happened to it. If the pages disagree, bring the disagreement too.

**Player:** “Who shortened the label?”

> Asha: We do not know yet.
>
> Kest: We know the label is short. That is enough to ask what did not fit.

The request scene ends with the expedition marker using the existing location route. It does not add a special materials objective type or a crafting promise.

## 11. Approach encounter: water under the wrapping

On the damaged University Quarter route, a small group shelters under a fallen tram canopy. A canvas bundle has been tied with copper wire. Water runs down the canopy’s support and into the bundle’s lower corner. One traveler, Rell, is trying to keep a packet of medicine dry. Another, Onya, is arguing that the packet should be opened and separated before the ink runs.

This encounter has no magical connection to the lab. It is a human echo of the main theme: something protected is still changing in its environment. Rell asks the player to help lift the bundle to a dry shelf. Onya asks whether the player has a clean piece of cloth. The player can use existing inventory items through ordinary encounter checks, or decline.

If the player helps, Rell admits the cloth around the medicine is not a sterile wrap and should not be treated as one. The encounter gives a small, optional supply or reputation consequence only if the existing encounter system already supports it. The prose remains complete without reward. If the player refuses, Onya still moves the packet and says, “We can save the record and lose the wrapping. We have to choose what matters first.”

The scene foreshadows that a sample’s packaging and a sample’s test result are different evidence. It must not imply that the player has found the missing composite sleeve.

## 12. At the hydraulic threshold

The sublevel entrance is quieter than its age should permit. The hydraulic assembly has trapped a pocket of pressure. Condensation has darkened the cement beneath the return pipe. The outer handle moves a few millimeters and stops.

Leena asks everyone to wait while she listens. She places two fingers on the casing, then checks a fixed bolt and the seam. The player can ask what she learned. She answers with observations, not a diagnosis: a slow pulse in the line, a hiss with each return, and water moving somewhere behind the frame. She does not claim the door is safe.

The existing route should use its ordinary gate, hazard, or encounter treatment. If the player carries a suitable tool, that can satisfy existing access rules. If no such option exists, the entrance is handled in prose and through the location’s already-defined access. This proposal does not add hydraulic pressure simulation.

When the door yields, it does so in stages. The first opening releases damp air smelling of hot rubber and old detergent. The party waits for the sound to stop, then passes one at a time. Leena places a chalk mark on the side the team can see from inside.

> Leena: A mark says where we are. It does not say what the door will do next.
>
> Kest: Good. I was about to ask the mark for a forecast.

The exchange is dry, not comic relief. It shows how practical skepticism can make a tense scene easier to bear.

## 13. Specimen corridor: room writing

The first corridor opens into a line of shallow drawers. Most have pale rectangles where labels were protected from dust. Some drawers are swollen shut. A cracked observation window gives the room a greenish color in the morning. The air is still enough that the party hears their clothing brush the doorframe.

Use short descriptions as the party advances. They are written to be read in movement and to support players who do not inspect every prop.

- **Drawer A:** Fine black fibers have collected along the rail. A paper label says “cloth tensile set”; its last line is washed out.
- **Drawer B:** A row of empty specimen envelopes remains, each numbered in red. One envelope has a torn corner folded under itself.
- **Cabinet C:** The latch carries a handwritten arrow and the words “pull toward hinge.” Pulling in that direction makes it open without scraping the glass.
- **Floor grate:** Pale water marks reach a line of chalk. The water stopped there, or the chalk was drawn later; the room does not say.
- **Window:** Beyond the glass, a centrifuge rotor rests in its cradle. A faded card asks the operator to check that the retaining ring is visible before startup.
- **Bench end:** A bent ruler is tied to the leg with string. One note says “zero edge damaged—do not use for cut length.” It has been crossed out once and written again.
- **Wall clock:** The hands have stopped at 04:12. A note beside it says “clock unpowered,” not “test stopped.”
- **Sample trolley:** The upper shelf is empty. The lower has two empty foam cutouts and a damp stain shaped like a narrow rectangle.
- **Vent grill:** A strip of paper trembles when someone passes. It does not indicate airflow direction; there is a draft from another room.
- **Emergency shelf:** A tin cup, a cotton rag, and a packet of chalk are still inside a clean box. The label says “room supplies,” not “test materials.”

An inspection interaction can reveal that the lab labeled storage conditions separately from test conditions. If the player never inspects it, Leena points this out when the party reaches the sample drawer. The story does not hinge on a missable interaction.

## 14. First evidence: the specimen card

The target drawer is half-open. A padded card inside holds a dark gray sleeve segment no longer than a hand. It has two layers, a pale inner strip, and a seam that runs diagonally rather than straight. A narrow red pencil mark crosses one end. One edge is ragged, but the seam itself has not opened.

The attached specimen card reads:

> CS-14 / chamber set C
>
> Outer lay: cross weave, shallow overlap
>
> Inner liner: retained, no visible lift on receipt
>
> Prepared by: L. Oris
>
> Orientation mark: red end to fixed jaw
>
> Remnant length: 116 mm at storage check
>
> Do not trim for field fit
>
> Return to dry cabinet after observation

The date is partially rubbed. The player can identify the year only from a second document. The card uses no real institution name. Leena recognizes her initials but is careful about the “prepared by” line. She cut the sample from a supplied roll; she did not make the material.

> Leena: I remember trimming these. I don’t remember this piece.
>
> Player: Your initials are here.
>
> Leena: That tells you who measured the edge. It does not tell you I watched the test.

The specimen can be placed in an existing quest or inventory container if the current content pipeline requires it. It should not become an equipable item or a new player material. If inventory capacity is relevant, the sample rides in a padded evidence sleeve and uses an existing quest-item convention after promotion review.

A player may rotate the specimen under the portable lamp. One side shows a tiny compression mark from the clamp. The opposite side has a pale notch in the inner liner. Nothing indicates that it was installed in a live cable. Leena says so plainly: “The sample tells us it sat in a test fixture. It does not tell us a pump ran through it.”

## 15. The test room: arranged reading order

The test room contains a cold chamber, a paper chart recorder, a clamp bench, and a stack of clipboards. The chamber’s door seal has cracked. A dial sits at its lowest reading. The chamber must remain inactive. The expedition does not restart equipment or run a fresh test.

The headline sheet is clipped at eye level:

> **CS-14 / COLD SOAK / 6 HOUR**
>
> No visible split at completion.
>
> Condition: sample fixed across flat jaws.
>
> Result: pass for visual inspection.
>
> Next: repeat with edge transition under flex.

The word “pass” has been circled. There is an arrow to “visual inspection” in the margin, written in a different pencil. A second sheet below records the next condition:

> **CS-14 / REPEATED BEND / EDGE TRANSITION**
>
> Sixteen full movements recorded.
>
> First whitening at the outer seam on movement nine.
>
> Open separation at movement sixteen.
>
> Fixture stopped; specimen removed.
>
> Further work: compare with a rounded entry and a longer overlap.

The papers are in correct order, but the top sheet hides the bottom. This is deliberate. The player first sees a limited positive result, then discovers the failure condition that limits it. No skill check is needed to find the second page. An inspect action simply adds details about the paper clip and the separate test date.

The room description reminds the player that the six-hour sheet is not a product certification. The line “pass for visual inspection” describes only the first procedure. Leena refuses to translate it into “the sleeve works in cold.” Daro recognizes the second sheet’s sketch of a joint entry, but does not say it matches his field repair exactly.

## 16. The clamp bench

A bench occupies the room’s central aisle. The clamp is bolted to a steel plate. Its lower jaw is flat; a rounded entry insert lies beside it in a waxed envelope. The insert’s paper tag says it was considered for a repeat, not used in the recorded run. A small washer has been pressed into an oval under the clamp foot.

The test note contains a line that might be easy to miss:

> Clamp washer absent on first cold-soak setup. Fixture checked and washer replaced before repeat bend. Results not pooled.

Oren Vale’s initials sit beside the note, followed by the time “02:10.” Someone has written “late shift” on the reverse. Asha reads it and asks if the missing washer invalidates the cold-soak result. Leena says the page does not say that. The report separates the runs, so the player should too.

A careful physical description: the cold-soak run and the bend run were separate procedures. The failed result does not retroactively prove the first observation false. The first observation does not cancel the later failure. Oren’s note is not an admission of incompetence. It is evidence that the operator recorded a changed fixture rather than hiding it.

The washer can be recovered as a small object only if existing interaction rules support ordinary evidence props. It has no item stat, no recipe use, and no mechanical consequence. If it remains bolted, the player can still read the note through the clamp card.

**Leena:** “This part moved. He wrote it down.”

**Player:** “Does that mean the first run was wrong?”

**Leena:** “It means the first run used a clamp with a missing washer. The report says that. We can decide what the report supports. We cannot add a different test because this one is inconvenient.”

**Daro:** “A loose clamp would change what he saw?”

**Leena:** “Maybe. The page does not measure that. The washer was replaced before the bend. There is no matched comparison here.”

**Kest:** “Then put the washer in the sentence.”

**Leena:** “If the sentence needs it. It does not need a guess.”

This exchange establishes that responsible uncertainty is not the same as endless caveating. The field card can state the result and separately note that the cold-soak setup had a missing washer, without guessing what effect the washer had.

## 17. Chart recorder and the stopped line

A paper roll runs from the recorder to the floor. It is not a temperature curve with usable calibrated values. The chart’s time line fades, and a short section is blank. A pencil note says “pen dry; observation continued by visual check.” A label on the instrument indicates it had been marked for service after the test date.

The player can ask Ivo Marek, through a short memory fragment or recovered deposition, what he watched.

> Ivo: I watched the chamber display. I did not watch the paper trace after the pen failed. The chamber display held steady while I was there.
>
> Player: For the full six hours?
>
> Ivo: No. For the time I was at the panel. I wrote that down too.
>
> Player: Did the sample stay cold?
>
> Ivo: I can tell you what the display said. I cannot tell you the temperature at the sample face. There was no sensor there.

The recorded result remains “six-hour cold soak,” because the signed test summary states that duration and no source contradicts it. The missing chart trace narrows what a careful reader should claim about the exact temperature at the sample. It does not become a detective twist that invalidates every record.

A player can add one of three field-card notes:
- “Recorder trace incomplete; duration recorded in test summary.”
- “Local display reading recorded; sample-face temperature not separately measured.”
- “The archive retains the instrument note; summary does not reproduce detail.”

All are accurate. The choice affects the final label and reading dialogue. No option creates a hidden “perfect investigation” score.

The paper roll’s final clean section bears a later mark in different ink: “DO NOT REWIND.” It has been folded with the blank face outward. Sela Venn recognizes the fold pattern as one used to keep the page from picking up floor water. She does not know who folded it. Her observation adds preservation context, not authorship.

## 18. Room-to-room descriptive sequence

This section provides longer room prose for the expedition’s reading pace. Each scene is complete if the player enters it once. Optional inspection adds texture and may provide a source reference, but never gates the core finding.

### 18.1 The weighing room

The weighing room is small, warmer than the corridor by a few degrees, and crowded with paper packets. A brass balance sits under a glass hood. One pan has been removed and set on a folded cloth. A paper tag reads “repair request / do not return to use.” The tag has faded enough that the last word might be “case” rather than “use.”

Bren Tal is remembered here through an inventory sheet. He wrote the packet count as “seven, maybe eight,” then added, “One is the pan cloth; not a specimen.” The second hand corrected the original total. The detail sounds minor until the player sees how often an object changes category as it moves through a room.

If inspected, the balance’s calibration card is visible. It expired months before the CS-14 test. The test book does not claim that this balance measured CS-14, so the card should not be used to discredit the cold chamber. Leena points this out only if the player suggests it. Her restraint helps keep the investigation bounded.

### 18.2 The wash room

A shallow basin contains fine gray sediment. A thin tide line circles the porcelain. The tap is open by a fraction but no water comes through. Someone has wedged a strip of folded paper under the handle to keep it from rattling.

The lab log says the basin was used to rinse sample-preparation tools. It does not say CS-14 was washed. Daro sees a familiar gray stain on the basin edge, but cannot identify its source. He does not pretend otherwise. A player may ask if the sleeve could have been cleaned before testing. Leena says that the card records the liner as retained on receipt, not that it was cleaned.

The room gives an environmental pause. The water line is old enough to have lost its sharp edge. A drip echoes from the next room without a visible drop. The expedition moves on when Kest says the sound has been repeating from the same direction for two minutes.

### 18.3 The records mezzanine

A narrow stair rises to a mezzanine of indexed boxes. The lowest shelf is dry; the top shelf bears a damp shadow. The index is arranged by quarter, material family, and batch number. Several divider cards have been pulled and placed in a metal tray.

One card for “CS-14” lists three related samples. Only the first and third have intact records. The middle card says “not prepared; roll short.” That detail prevents an invented full series from being implied. It also gives the team a practical reason why no later test compares the longer overlap: the roll was not available in that batch.

The mezzanine’s view looks down on the test bench. It makes the room feel less like a laboratory and more like a place where people watched each other work. An old pencil cup remains on the rail. The pencils are all worn to different lengths. There is one full pencil, unsharpened, laid across the open index book.

### 18.4 The autoclave bay

The autoclave doors are closed. A laminated instruction card says the pressure indicator must read zero before the latch is touched. A red tag across the panel says “service hold.” The panel is dark. No one proposes starting it.

Sela Venn’s recollection is tied to a bucket she used to catch condensation from a pipe, not to any test result. She says a later crew used the bay as a dry place to sort papers because the upper wall did not leak. She remembers the papers, then the people who came to collect them. She cannot say which papers were in which box.

This keeps the room humane without turning every physical object into a clue. The player can rest here for a moment. If the existing expedition cadence allows it, a short camp line can play. If it does not, the scene is just text.

### 18.5 The sample wash window

A square pane opens onto the service corridor. Its lower edge is scratched in several places. The scratch marks are too far apart to be a ruler and too irregular to count as calibration. A pencilled note on the ledge reads: “light from west after 08:00.”

At the hour implied by the expedition’s current world clock, the light may not match. The note is old. The player can notice that it was written for a different season. There is no quest to recover a correct clock. Leena says the light line helped technicians see pale lift at the sample seam, but she cannot tell whether this pane was used for CS-14.

The scene provides one more tactile way to inspect the specimen: turning it under the player’s lamp shows a pale area on one side, but the area could be a reflection from the liner. After the expedition returns, Asha clears the table at the relay shelter. The cable remains at one end. The sample sleeve, still inside its padded card, lies at the other. The group has the test sheets, transport label, field note, and witness statements.

Asha asks the player to choose a field card. The player is not asked to write free text. Instead, the existing dialogue or journal choice presents three compositions from verified language. The final card can fit into a small sleeve and point to the full archive packet.

### Card B: practical summary

### Card C: abbreviated but bounded
