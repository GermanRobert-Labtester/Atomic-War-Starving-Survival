# CONTENT EXPANSION CW31-01 — The Axle Keeps a Place

## A Scavenger Guild story about a camp that moves and a rule that travels with it.

### Prose Wave 31: Six Places, Six Kinds of Work

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 32 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (128 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_scavenger_camp` — Scavenger Guild Camp.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Guild camp shifts with salvage fronts, but its marker remains an axle stood upright at the gatehouse. Rika Dorn works alone, sells silence as dearly as her finds, and knows which cellar floors are solid and which are only rumors. The Guild’s rule against stripping a site below the point where it can be worked again is economic, not sentimental. This expansion follows the friction between a mobile workplace and a reputation that persists after the canvas is gone. Rika’s existing arc states, choices, benefits, rivalry, and death outcome remain exactly as authored. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

What can a fixed marker promise when the people who set it are expected to leave?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The location row describes a rotating canvas-and-corrugated-steel encampment, a shifting position, salvage-front movement, and a constant gatehouse marker made from a stripped vehicle axle. Rika Dorn is explicitly placed at this location. Her character record names her work, trade terms, preferred rope and lights, and refusals; the Scavenger Guild codex explains its durable-work rule and blacklist. The NPC arc owns her existing states and must remain the only authority for them.

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

Rika’s character and arc records control her location, words, wants, refusal, conditions, and outcomes. The Guild’s site-preservation rule is economic rather than sentimental. The camp moves; the axle marker is the recurring sign. The codex does not grant the player a cellar list. Keep Rika’s cellars, maps, recruitment effects, and guild standing under the existing arc and faction owners. A proposed second voice remains an unnamed editorial role. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The human center is the person deciding whether another scavenger is a partner, a witness, or simply someone with whom to settle a bill. The axle helps strangers find a worksite; it does not make them welcome. Let the camp’s exact terms, a refusal, or an unfinished receipt create pressure instead of a speech explaining the Guild. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Rika is fast, transactional, dry, and observant. She does not explain a refusal after giving it, and she does not volunteer cellar names to a stranger. The Guild’s codex is an eyewitness account, not a neutral charter. Use unnamed apprentices and buyers only as proposed voices; do not add a canonical Guild officer or give a new person authority to overrule Rika. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 7. Placement and current reachability

Candidate prose belongs beside existing Rika arc content or a verified location-inspection surface. A passage may reflect only an already exposed arc state. The plan adds no camp relocation schedule, map, salvage bonus, rope budget, blacklist rule, recruit action, or new refusal flag. If the current consumer cannot expose a named state, use the state-neutral passage or omit it. All fragments are candidates. None is evidence that the location is currently player-reachable. Before selecting any text, verify the current map/location owner, content schema, actual consumer, and the source condition under which the text can appear.

## 8. Player agency

The player can ask what the axle marks, make an offer using the current interaction, read the codex, or leave. They cannot force Rika to name a cellar through a prose choice. Existing Rika quest choices retain their meaning and consequences. No additional vote, trust meter, apprenticeship feature, or camp address is introduced. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not expose a usable cellar route, describe how to bypass a flood rumor, or turn salvage into a real-world search procedure. Do not romanticize stripping sites or make the Guild’s rule a moral absolution. Do not rewrite Rika’s death, recruitment, late-ally, or rival outcomes. Do not claim that a location is safe because it is marked, mapped, or listed. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Disturbance — the axle in the mud

The marker stands while the tents are being folded. Establish that movement is part of the camp’s work, not evidence of flight.

### 2. Discovery — Rika’s terms

A rope request, a light preference, and a withheld cellar name show the difference between a useful trade and a claimed right.

### 3. Interpretation — the Guild rule

The codex’s preservation rule is practical economics. Let buyers disagree about its fairness without converting it into a moral system.

### 4. Complication — a state already chosen

Rika’s arc changes the meaning of the site. Any callback must use the actual existing state and not forecast a branch.

### 5. Choice — ask or accept the limit

The player may ask a narrow question, negotiate through an existing interaction, or leave. Rika’s refusal remains complete.

### 6. Callback — the next camp

Return to the same axle as a marker whose location may move. Nothing in the prose updates a route or the Guild registry.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Use the marker, one receipt, and two short exchanges. Keep the Guild rule visible only through work.

### Interlinked

Select state-aware Rika callbacks only if the NPC arc owner exposes the exact late ally, rival, recruited, or dead branch.

### Wild card

Tell one beat through an apprentice trying to copy the axle’s position onto paper, then leaving the map blank when the camp moves.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
