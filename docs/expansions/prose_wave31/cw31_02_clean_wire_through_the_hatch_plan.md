# CONTENT EXPANSION CW31-02 — Clean Wire Through the Hatch

## A material-culture story about a transaction that returns what it promises.

### Prose Wave 31: Six Places, Six Kinds of Work

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 31 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (124 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_automated_abattoir` — Automated Abattoir.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Automated Abattoir is a working industrial remnant used by the Osteophages to process large irradiated game and recycle bone stock. The codex names the faction “Rust-Eaters,” records their illness without making them villains, and describes a trade in which toxic technological waste enters through an airlock and cleaned copper wire and scrap metal come back. The location also has a chimney benchmark whose visibility is excellent except for smoke-plume obstruction. This plan writes about the people and records around a difficult exchange; it adds no process instructions, new products, or claims about a present animal or inventory. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

What makes an exchange fair when one side must take the risk everyone else refuses?

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The location and codex are separate sources with their own emphasis. The Osteophages are ill and organized; the codex explicitly rejects a villain reading. Metal is returned clean according to the eyewitness account, while the location describes a bone-stock trade. The bone and horn files are authored records, not safety manuals. Do not add operating instructions, disease explanations, animal suffering, product yields, or a new barter rate. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The human center is the worker who waits for the hatch to open from the other side, with an object everyone else calls waste on one side and usable wire on the other. Their expertise should read as work, not magic. The story does not ask the player to cure, judge, or rescue the Osteophages; it lets the transaction show how dependence and dignity can occupy the same counter. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Use the codex as a sourced eyewitness account, never as a first-person confession by an invented faction member. Proposed shop-floor voices remain unnamed editorial roles. Keep technical records exact in attribution and sparse in quotation. Do not make a person speak for the whole Osteophage community, and do not write illness as a mask, curse, or moral punishment. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 7. Placement and current reachability

Potential passages sit beside the existing location, codex, and discovery content only after their current consumers are verified. A sound or text callback may refer to the existing site inspection; no new processing station, recipe, contamination check, or trade window is proposed. Before selecting any text, verify the current map/location owner, content schema, actual consumer, and the source condition under which the text can appear.

## 8. Player agency

The player can observe the hatch, read the codex, inspect a record, or leave. Any trade remains with current faction and economy owners. The prose does not add a new decision about which people deserve clean metal, and does not make the player a medical authority over another community. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not provide instructions for slaughter equipment, industrial power, chemical treatment, bone processing, or handling contaminated materials. Do not describe graphic injury. Do not stigmatize pica or poisoning, or infer an individual’s condition from faction membership. Do not invent a named worker, a bargain rate, a shipment, or a claim that every returned item has the same provenance. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Disturbance — the closed hatch

Begin with a transaction waiting on both sides of an airlock. The scene does not reveal the machinery inside.

### 2. Discovery — the clean return

The codex’s eyewitness account makes a specific claim about copper and scrap. Keep it attributed rather than universal.

### 3. Interpretation — two kinds of work

The location’s bone-stock practice and the codex’s metal exchange remain related to one place but not one process.

### 4. Complication — records travel

Discovery rows link the abattoir and workshop surfaces. Provenance matters more than convenient staging.

### 5. Choice — accept the boundary

The player may read, wait, trade only through current systems, or leave. No new process choice is written.

### 6. Callback — the chimney sightline

End at the benchmark where smoke can interrupt an otherwise excellent view. Observation remains partial.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Use the hatch, one receipt, and a brief exchange that names the material without explaining its treatment.

### Interlinked

A discovery callback can refer to the existing record ID and its producer provenance, never inventing a shared author.

### Wild card

Set a scene at the survey point: the chimney blocks part of the view, while the transaction below continues outside the observer’s line of sight.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
