# EXPANSION 74 — PRESS SIDE STAYS CLEAR

## A prose-first content expansion plan for the Cartwright Sisters

### Wave 15: The Ordinary Measure

## 1. Creative promise

This expansion turns one line in an existing character record into a patient, playable relationship: two press operators do not owe the visitor a confession in exchange for work. Ada negotiates in spoken terms; Ruth has not spoken since Hour Zero and performs the work. The press is useful because it can make materials for several urgent purposes, and the sign above it says the output is not for the mouth. A late four-word note is part of their relationship with the player, but its subject is not Ruth. The story protects that separation.

The player's progress comes from learning to stand where the work can use them. They do not cure silence, solve the sisters' private history, or receive an intimate disclosure as a prize. Their most consequential success is to help a batch leave the barn with its intended use still legible.

## 2. One-sentence story

When competing needs reach the press on the same day, the player helps Ada and Ruth keep a safe, honest distinction between oil, sterilizing spirits, and tractor fuel, while learning that trust can include a subject one is asked not to approach.

## 3. Story mode

This is a low-volume, relationship-led location expansion for a current expedition site. It is designed as several visits rather than a single long conversation. The barn's sound, queue of containers, sign, and work sequence carry more story than a hidden room or combat reveal. The proposed scenes can be presented in a quest or recurring-encounter path only after a current owner is checked; this document does not create that route or claim that one already exists.

The plan uses existing character facts and location description as fixed canon. New dialogue, labels, visitors, and the exact four-word note below are authored proposals. The note wording is not present in source data and must be approved as new content before production. Keeping the note's subject outside Ruth is the fixed constraint.

## 4. Verified anchor: the place

The Cider Press is described as a stone barn containing a working screw press, with oiled threads and a sound beam. The location description gives twenty-four rads per hour and calls the danger moderate. Its products are explicitly not drink: oil, sterilizing spirits for wounds and instruments, and a fuel blend for tractors on the far side of the valley. The sign over the press is plain and kept legible. This story treats each of those details as authored evidence, not a prop that can be overwritten by an exciting reveal.

The expedition catalog also lists the site, with four distance ticks, danger four, an encounter chance per tick, stamina drain, and a farm scavenging table. That is proof of an authored expedition row, not proof of a new map connection, a guaranteed encounter, or a safe route. The prose never promises the party a particular resource quantity.

## 5. Verified anchor: the operators

The character entry is npc_the_cartwright_sisters, named in lore as Ada and Ruth. Ada speaks and negotiates. Ruth has not spoken since Hour Zero and does the work. Their wants are apples, boiler fuel, and no questions about Ruth; their offer is ethanol. The bio says that late in a long partnership Ruth hands the player a written note rather than speaking. The note is four words long and is not about herself.

This expansion does not add a diagnosis, a presumed cause, a date for the silence, or a promise that the silence will end. It treats written communication, gesture, work, and refusal as complete ways of participating in a scene. No line in this plan asks another character to translate Ruth's interior life.

## 6. Evidence boundary and uncertainty

The record names Ada and Ruth but supplies one shared character identifier. A proposed production version must preserve that shared identity unless the data owner approves a specific split. The story may distinguish the sisters by their authored dialogue and observable actions without silently inventing two recruitable NPCs or two inventory vendors.

The record does not tell us which words Ruth writes, why she has not spoken, how long the partnership takes, whether every visit occurs at the press, or whether the sisters have family beyond the barn. Those remain open. A scene may offer a clearly labeled candidate detail, but it must not recast a candidate as already established canon. The exact note is therefore marked as proposed in each section where it appears.

## 7. The central human conflict

The conflict is practical but not a delivery puzzle. A small cluster of containers must be prepared for more than one destination, and the people waiting for them have incompatible schedules. A ward wants material for cleaning instruments; a tractor crew wants the blend before the next field crossing; Ada has enough experience to know that a hurried label can become an unsafe assumption weeks later. These visitors are proposed additions, not named current characters.

The player is not asked to calculate a hidden best use or diagnose contamination. They help decide how to make the current work understandable to its recipients: distinguish containers, preserve the original sign's wording, tell a waiting party that the press cannot serve them first, or leave the explanation with Ada. Scarcity stays human and visible without inventing an economic simulation.

## 8. The story's emotional center

Ada can answer questions about the press, its schedule, and what the sisters will trade. She does not need to answer a question about Ruth merely because a visitor asks it gently. Ruth does not become a locked narrative door whose reward is unlocked by the player's patience. The player shows respect by taking the stated boundary literally.

The emotional turn arrives when Ruth hands over a note that concerns a shared task. The player may feel invited to treat any written message as a personal revelation; the scene resists that assumption. The note does not explain the silence or ask to be read aloud. It makes a specific production request. Ada hears the player read it only if the player chooses; Ruth's own action remains sufficient to make the request known.

## 9. The premise's point of view

The scenes stay close to what a visiting survivor can see and hear: a screw thread being cleaned, a beam settling under load, a drum rim wiped before a mark is painted, one sister setting the order and the other changing a tool's position. The narrator does not state what Ruth thinks. The player can notice a hand pause, a look toward the sign, or the sequence of work, but the text does not turn observation into access to her private memory.

Ada can state her view directly, and the player may disagree. Her speech is negotiation, not a universal explanation of the sisters. Their working rhythm can communicate familiarity without the prose claiming that the player now understands everything about their relationship.

## 10. Tone and texture

The barn is warm only near the boiler and cold where the stone meets the floor. The press sounds in repeated intervals, never like a machine waking from a supernatural sleep. Its beam has a creak the sisters recognize; they stop when the sound changes. Rags carry the smell of oil. A chalk mark is erased with the side of a thumb because a damp sleeve would spread the color into a label nobody can read.

The tone is workmanlike and unromantic. The story may be tender, but it does not use a sister's silence as a shortcut to tragedy. The institution around the press can be strained without becoming cruel caricature. The most dramatic moment can be a visitor waiting at the wrong side of the rope while Ada decides whether to stop the press.

## 11. Named and unnamed cast

Ada and Ruth are the only returning named people required by the source anchor. Their proposed voices should remain distinguishable: Ada asks for the terms and states the boundary; Ruth communicates through the task, timing, and a written note. Do not invent surnames. Do not add a speaking companion who claims to know what happened to Ruth.

Three functional visitors can carry the scheduling conflict: a clinic runner with clean hands and a case for instrument spirits; a tractor mechanic with a patched funnel and a return schedule; and a courier who needs only to know whether a container is safe to carry. They have no faction authority and no new biography beyond the scene. Their sparse characterization keeps attention on the operators and avoids a second story taking over.

## 12. Arrival: the sign before the conversation

The player arrives while a traveler has stopped in front of the sign and is trying to decide whether “not for the mouth” means no one may handle the containers. Ada is inside the barn, measuring a drum's shoulder with a strip of wood. Ruth clears a rag from the press bed, folds it twice, and lays it where no one will step on it. The traveler asks the player whether the sign is a joke.

The player can answer with the source plainly: the output is intended for other uses, and the sisters can explain their current stock. They can instead call Ada over, or say they do not know. If they guess that it is drink and mock the sign, Ada's first scene becomes colder. She does not withhold a required clue or stage a public humiliation; she simply makes clear that a person who cannot read a working sign should not advise a stranger what to drink.

## 13. Opening dialogue: Ada sets the boundary

Proposed exchange:

> “You came to trade?”
>
> “I came to see what was going on.”
>
> “Those aren't the same answer.”
>
> “The sign brought me in.”
>
> “Then read it before you ask for the press.”
>
> “What if I was asking about your sister?”
>
> “Then the answer is already there. No questions about Ruth.”
>
> “I didn't know if it meant no personal questions.”
>
> “You know now.”

The player's response can be respectful, embarrassed, defensive, or silent. Ada does not punish an apology by accepting it as payment. She returns to the order of work. Ruth does not react as a reward cue. The scene's job is to make the boundary easy to understand before the player can violate it.

## 14. Opening dialogue: choosing to stop talking

The player can ask Ada what the press can make, ask what they need, or ask about the sign. If the player pauses instead, Ada offers the same work-facing invitation: “You can stand inside the chalk or outside it. Inside, keep clear of the beam.” The player is not granted private space because they were quiet. They are offered an ordinary safe position, with the same rule as any helper.

A production pass should let the player walk away from this conversation without losing access to the location's ordinary expedition outcome. The content is an expansion layer, not a compulsory confession scene. The player can decline trade, decline the task, and return later if the current route permits.

## 15. Scene: what the press does

Ada names the three uses in an order that reflects current need, not an eternal ranking: oil, instrument spirits, tractor blend. A player may ask which is most important. Ada says, “This week? The thing whose receiver has a date and a clean container.” That is proposed dialogue, not a new rule about the sisters' priorities.

Ruth demonstrates how the press binds under a bad angle. She turns the beam back half a hand's width, removes the fruit from the thread, and wipes a surface before applying pressure again. Ada narrates only what a visitor needs to know. A player who has crafting knowledge may identify the load; one who does not can still help by carrying an empty drum or holding a label flat. The scene makes participation possible without making the player the expert.

## 16. Scene: keeping the sign legible

A corner of the sign has lifted from its board. The player may offer to hold it while Ada resets a nail, fetch a dry cloth, or leave it alone. Ruth does not ask for restoration, and the sign is not a collectible. If the player handles it, the original sentence remains the most prominent text. No additional slogan replaces it.

A note about the sign's history is not available because the source does not provide one. Ada can say that they keep it legible because people read what they need to read and skip what they don't. She does not claim the sign saved a named person or caused the press to survive. The location's current description already gives the sign its force; the story amplifies that force through use rather than manufacturing provenance.

## 17. Scene: safe working distance

The press floor has a chalked arc around the beam, an authored visual cue proposed for this plan. Its purpose is practical: visitors should know where to wait while the equipment is under load. Ada tells the player to keep a sleeve clear of the screw. If they step too close, she stops work with a sharp “Back.” The stoppage is brief and concrete. No injury animation or hazard system is invented.

A repeated visit can change who reminds the player, but not the boundary itself. Ruth can shift the player's basket outside the arc before Ada speaks. If the player notices and moves without a line, the game can acknowledge that with a short nod rather than a trust score. This is not a new hidden relationship meter; it is a scene variant controlled by ordinary dialogue state if approved.

## 18. Scene: the container question

The clinic runner arrives carrying a lidded case and asks if the liquid will sterilize a needle. Ada refuses to answer from a container's appearance. She checks the mark, the seal, and the current order, then tells the runner which statement the sisters can support. The exact production details must be checked against item catalog and chemistry authority before implementation; this plan does not claim that any unlabeled item has a particular strength.

The player can repeat Ada's cautious answer or overstate what the flask can do. If they overstate, Ada corrects the words, not the visitor's need: “We made this for instruments. We didn't test it on a body.” That wording is authored proposal. The scene's content target is a plain line that distinguishes intended use from clinical guarantee.

## 19. Scene: the tractor crew has a clock

A mechanic reaches the barn late with a funnel and says the crew is already waiting at the road. Ada asks when they need to leave. The mechanic gives a time but cannot say how much fuel the far-side tractor still has. Ada does not make that missing fact up. The player can ask the mechanic to return with a count, help write a departure note, or ask Ada to decide with the information in hand.

This is not a branching resource-allocation calculator. The choice changes how the dialogue frames the delay. If the player urges Ada to ship an unmarked container, she refuses. If the player accepts the delay, the mechanic can complain and still take the labeled load when it is ready. The objective is to make the cost visible without granting the player a magic way to make the scarcity disappear.

## 20. The first small task

Ada asks the player to sort three clean empty containers by the marks already painted on their necks. Each mark is introduced as a proposed visual convention and must be validated for accessibility. The task asks no recipe question; it tests whether the player will read before acting. One container belongs near the oil cloth, one by the spirit case, and one remains empty for the next batch.

The player can take time, ask Ada to repeat the destination, or set one down and admit uncertainty. The best response is not fastest; it is the response that leaves the containers in the right place. Ruth sees the result and begins the next step. She does not clap, smile, or grant an item. The work proceeds because the player did a small task correctly.

## 21. Ada's account of a good day

If asked what a good day looks like, Ada answers with counts that do not imply current inventory: “The beam stays true. The labels stay where they belong. Nobody puts a cup under the drain and decides a useful smell means supper.” The last phrase is authored proposal and must not be used to suggest the product was ever beverage stock.

She says a good day also means the mechanic comes back with the container rather than leaving it at the road, and the clinic runner gives a clear receipt. The player may ask whether a good day is the same as a profitable one. Ada answers, “Only if the buyer knows what they're buying.” This turns trade into informed consent without inventing new faction law.

## 22. Ruth's working vocabulary

Ruth's actions are presented as communication only when the scene supports the reading. She places a lid beside one drum, not another; she taps a mark on the sign; she leaves a rag on the beam when it should not be used. A player can notice those facts, but a generic dialogue caption should not translate each gesture into interior thought. The writing may show what the player can infer and let a character ask Ada whether they have read it correctly.

When the player gets a gesture wrong, Ada can say, “No. She means this one,” and point to the object, not to Ruth's face. The correction is about a task and does not imply that Ada is a permanent interpreter for her sister. When the player asks Ruth directly through an approved response option, the game should leave room for her to decline, write, point, or continue working without a forced answer.

## 23. Boundary rehearsal

A visitor says, “Has she always been like this?” Ada turns away from the press. The player has three lines: “That isn't ours to ask”; “She can decide whether to answer”; or a silence with a visible step back from the workbench. Ada acknowledges the first two in distinct ways. The player who redirects the conversation does not receive a special friendship reward. The visitor is told what the barn needs and the scene returns to the batch.

A fourth option—pressing for a medical or psychological explanation—is included only as a refusal path, not a hidden route to new lore. Ada ends the exchange. Ruth does not become angry or break down. The story represents privacy as an ordinary boundary that the operator can enforce, not a dramatic mechanism that extracts information from the player.

## 24. Act I close: an order written correctly

At the end of the first visit, the player can choose whether the day's container note is written as “oil,” “instrument spirits,” or “fuel blend,” depending on what was actually designated by Ada in the scene. The implementation must not let a player select a destination inconsistent with current authored inventory. If the game cannot represent those categories as separate items, the prose option should remain a label on a narrative record, not a new stock transaction.

Ada checks the handwriting and makes one correction: a destination is not a property of a smell. The player leaves with the original sign still present and no special revelation. If the player has asked about Ruth, the conversation remembers the boundary in its tone, not through a new morality score.

## 25. Return visit: the door is not a test

The next visit does not begin with a pop quiz about the player's last choice. Ada may be in the yard counting sacks or inside the barn checking a thread; Ruth may be already at work. If the player previously respected the no-questions boundary, the sisters do not congratulate them for ordinary courtesy. They simply make room for the kind of help the player has already demonstrated they can do.

If the player previously pushed, Ada can keep the conversation practical and let the player try again without a speech about forgiveness. “If you have a load to move, say where it goes. If you came to ask about my sister, you heard me the first time.” This preserves the relationship's forward motion without pretending a single apology erased the first encounter.

## 26. Repetition as relationship

The expansion should use repeated work with small changes. The first day teaches the player to read the sign. The second teaches that a clean container is not the same as a known container. A later visit shows that Ada now leaves the label board where the player can reach it. Ruth still determines what happens at the press through her action. The change is not that the player receives access to more personal facts; the change is that the sisters have a reason to trust the player's hands near their work.

This matters for the rhythm of a long game. A recurring relationship can feel meaningful even if its state is represented by an existing quest completion and no new friendship meter. The production team should choose the smallest current narrative state that can distinguish visits, or keep the scenes sequential within one authored encounter. It must not create a parallel relationship ledger.

## 27. The blank label

A new batch waits in three containers. One has a label; two are blank because the decision about destination has not been made. A visitor reads the one mark, points at the others, and asks which is safe to carry. Ada says they are clean containers, not yet identified output. She does not let the player invent a label simply to make the scene move.

The player can say that they will wait for the mark, carry the containers back empty, or ask which person is waiting for which use. If the player writes “oil” on the wrong container, Ruth takes the chalk and crosses it once, clearly enough that no one can mistake it for a correction to the sign. The visual is modest and legible. It communicates a mistake without making Ruth a scold or making the player a fool.

## 28. The clinic runner's second visit

The clinic runner returns with the same case, this time empty. The player sees the case stay on the outside bench until Ada has confirmed the flask and its destination. The runner says the ward used the material as intended and asks for another. The plan does not give a treatment result, healing multiplier, or medical guarantee. The only returned evidence is that the case came back empty and the runner asks to repeat an order.

A player may ask how many instruments were cleaned. The runner may answer that the case holds the clinic's current tray, not a count of every instrument. That answer is intentionally small. It does not turn the runner into a medical report or the sisters into a supply authority that sets the ward's survival rate. The press has supplied something useful; it cannot certify everything downstream.

## 29. The mechanic's receipt

The tractor mechanic brings the empty container back with a folded piece of paper under its handle. The paper carries a simple acknowledgment: received at the far-side shed, container returned, cap intact. This is an authored prop proposal, not proof that a specific facility already exists. Ada asks the player to hold it flat while she checks the container rim.

If the player wants to keep the paper as a quest item, the plan refuses that reward. It belongs in the local record or can be read and left with the operators. No new unique item is required. The point is not that a paper receipt has special authority; it is that a round trip confirms the container did not become an excuse to claim an unknown result.

## 30. Conversation: “Could you do more?”

A visitor asks whether the sisters can run longer hours. Ada asks whether the visitor brought more boiler fuel. When the answer is no, she declines. The player may sympathize with the visitor, ask Ada to explain the machine's limit, or remain quiet while the sisters close the discussion. No choice makes Ada's refusal a hidden moral failure.

Proposed dialogue:

> “If you run another hour, we get the blend before dark.”
>
> “If we run another hour without fuel, the next batch gets no heat.”
>
> “It's only one hour.”
>
> “That's the size of the request. Not the size of its cost.”
>
> “Would you do it if the ward asked?”
>
> “The ward did not ask. You did.”

The exchange is about whose need is being represented. It does not claim the equipment has a novel failure mode or expose a mechanical weakness absent from the catalog.

## 31. A player who wants to mediate

The player can offer to carry an explanation between the clinic and the mechanic. Ada asks what exactly the player will say. A good summary names the current schedule and admits that the batch is not ready; it does not promise that either destination will be served first. A bad summary tells one visitor that the other has agreed to wait when no such agreement exists.

If the player misstates the schedule, the next scene offers a correction in plain language. The sisters do not humiliate the player or cancel their trade offer. They do stop treating the player as a reliable messenger until the player repeats the corrected version. This is a dialogue consequence, not a new score. It can be stored in the local narrative path only if a current owner already has an appropriate choice state.

## 32. What Ada will say about Ruth

The story permits Ada to talk about practical communication: “If Ruth needs me, she knows how to get my attention.” It does not permit her to turn that sentence into a full biography. If the player asks whether Ruth might speak someday, Ada says, “That isn't a question I can answer for her.” The line marks both the limit of Ada's knowledge and the limit of what the player should demand.

Other answer variants may include “You can ask me about the press,” or “You have enough to decide whether to work here.” All variants should avoid making an eventual spoken line the expected climax. If Ruth chooses to communicate in writing, that choice is already an affirmative action. The story's outcome does not depend on converting one kind of expression into another.

## 33. Quiet scene: Ada covers the ledger

A courier is standing at the table while Ada writes a quantity that is needed to settle a trade. When the courier leaves, Ada closes the book before the player can read it. If the player asks whether the entry was about them, she says, “It was about the order.” The ledger is not a secret diary waiting for the protagonist. It is a working record, and its privacy can be respected without a confrontation.

A player can ask Ada to repeat the part relevant to their own delivery. She turns the book back and points only to the line for that container. The staging should make visible that she has chosen what to show. The page does not contain unexplained names, veiled prophecies, or a hidden cause of Ruth's silence.

## 34. The unsafe shortcut

A proposed visitor suggests using one container for two different products if it is rinsed between them. Ada asks who will be responsible for the mark afterward. The player can support the shortcut, ask for a fresh container, or refuse to speak for the sisters. Ada makes the final safety call. If she declines, the scene continues without a debate about whether she is overcautious.

The story should not invent a chemistry procedure or validate a cleaning method. The author's task is to dramatize the difference between a container that looks clean and a process the operators can stand behind. Before any interactive version is implemented, a technical content reviewer must compare product language with the actual item and crafting catalogs. Until then, the dialogue uses no numbers, concentrations, or clinical claims.

## 35. Act II close: who owns the last word

At the end of a workday, the player can choose to repeat Ada's explanation to the waiting visitors, ask her to speak for herself, or tell the group only that the sisters have closed the press. Each option leaves the boundary intact. The player does not decide which sibling explains Ruth to the public.

If asked why Ada talks so directly, she says, “The work is easier when the terms are visible.” She does not say that Ruth cannot speak, because the visitors can see the work and because the story should not make that fact a public announcement every time the player arrives. The close rests on practical limits, not on a monologue that claims to settle the sisters' history.

## 36. Proposed note: exact candidate text

The existing character record establishes a four-word written note from Ruth, late in a long partnership, and explicitly says it is not about herself. It does not provide the exact words. This plan proposes one candidate for author review:

> Press side stays clear.

The candidate has four words and concerns a shared working space. It can be read as a practical reminder, a correction to the way visitors wait, or a safety instruction without explaining who Ruth is. It must remain labeled as authored proposed dialogue until the canon owner approves it. If reviewers prefer a different four-word instruction, replace the exact sentence without changing the scene's underlying boundary.

## 37. Why the note is not a cure

The note is not an answer to a question about silence. It does not say how long Ruth has been silent, how she feels about Ada, what happened at Hour Zero, or whether the player has earned the right to hear anything private. It is a contribution to the work. Ada reads it in her own voice only if Ruth has directed her to; the default is that the player reads it silently.

The player may ask why Ruth wrote those words. Ada replies, “Because she wanted that side clear.” The answer is intentionally literal. The player may still wonder whether there is a larger meaning, but the game gives no hidden confirmation and no extra lore flag for interpretive cleverness.

## 38. The late partnership threshold

“Late in a long partnership” comes from the character bio, but the data does not specify a visit count. The content owner should map the note to a current narrative condition that already represents a sustained relationship, or keep it in a later authored encounter whose placement does not claim a particular number of weeks. The writing must not say “after thirty days” unless the existing day clock and quest state actually support that condition.

The note should not appear at the first visit, nor should it be withheld behind intrusive dialogue. The appropriate threshold is demonstrated by repeated, ordinary collaboration: the player has worked near the press, respected the sign, followed a label, and accepted a closed boundary. This is a content criterion for placing the scene, not a proposal to add a new hidden trust statistic.

## 39. How the note arrives

The player comes to the workbench and finds a scrap held flat under an empty jar. Ruth places it there while Ada is checking an order outside. No soundtrack tells the player that a major reveal has arrived. The note is not hidden in a locked drawer or slipped into the player's pocket; the scene lets Ruth put it in a place where it can be read and returned.

The player can read, ask whether Ada should read it aloud, or hand it back without reading. If the player chooses not to read it, Ruth points to the text, then to the empty place on the bench. The story can make clear that the message is intended for the shared task, while preserving the player's option not to treat writing as automatically public.

## 40. Four-word note dialogue routes

If the player reads the candidate text, Ada can say, “We'll keep the press side clear.” If the player asks whether it was a warning, Ada replies, “It is an instruction.” If the player asks whether it is meant for them, Ada says, “It is where you stand.” If the player asks whether there is something else behind it, Ada answers, “You have the words she wrote.”

A response such as “I understand now” is allowed, but the narrative does not endorse it as complete understanding. The more careful option is “I'll move the basket.” Ruth can see the player move it and begin work. The response is visible and reversible; it does not convert the note into a permanent title or perk.

## 41. A note that is misread

The player can misread the phrase, or imagine it refers to an emotional distance. Ada corrects only what the page can support: “It says the press side.” She does not resolve the metaphor, because it is not a metaphor in the proposed version. The correction should not set a shame flag. It gives the player a chance to return to concrete details.

If the player insists on treating it as a coded confession, Ada puts the paper on the table and asks which work instruction they are going to follow. Ruth does not perform a second gesture to satisfy the player's interpretation. The scene demonstrates that ambiguity can be tolerated without being converted into a puzzle whose solution is another person's private life.

## 42. The note and the sign

After the note, the old sign remains unchanged. The player may say that the note should be added beneath it. Ada asks whether the note was written for visitors or for the press crew. If the player suggests displaying it publicly, Ada says the sisters will decide where the note belongs. The character record says Ruth hands it to the player, not that she authorizes its publication.

A respectful player returns the note to the workbench or asks Ada where it should be kept. The scene avoids making the private message a quest collectible. If the final game needs a journal record, that record should paraphrase the player's encounter and never reproduce the note as a public inscription unless the source owner approves.

## 43. The visitor at the rope

A new visitor arrives after the note and steps into the marked work area. Ada starts to tell them back; the player can do so first, point to the chalk arc, or let Ada handle it. The scene is not a test of whether the player can protect Ruth. It is a routine situation in which the player has learned the barn's safe distance and can choose to act on that knowledge.

If the player redirects the visitor calmly, Ada continues the order. If the player shouts or threatens, the visitor leaves and Ada says that fear is not the same as understanding. The consequence is tonal: the player has made the space less welcoming without making it safer. No combat encounter, faction hostility, or reputation penalty is required.

## 44. Small humor without ridicule

Ada occasionally uses dry humor about customers who mistake an unpleasant smell for a product label. The humor is directed at bad assumptions, never at Ruth's silence. Example: “If it smells like a reason to drink it, you have found the wrong end of the argument.” Another line: “The sign is older than your theory and has done more work.” These are authored proposals and can be softened during voice review.

Ruth can make the scene funny through timing: she may slide the correct empty jar across the bench just as the visitor reaches for the wrong one. The humor belongs to competence and shared work. It should not treat non-speaking communication as a gag or turn Ada into the only person allowed to be witty.

## 45. End of the late scene

The last press turn is stopped before the day's batch is forced. Ada wipes the beam while Ruth checks the empty space beside the marked containers. The player has no prompt to ask what Ruth meant “really.” The note remains a four-word instruction, with the proposed wording clearly attributed in implementation notes.

The story's immediate reward is the continuation of ordinary work: Ada tells the player what the next order is, and Ruth makes room for the player's basket at the correct side of the table. If the player leaves, the sisters close the barn in their usual sequence. Nothing in this scene declares them fixed, healed, reconciled, or fully known.

## 46. A separate conversation with the mechanic

On a later visit, the tractor mechanic asks the player why the press crew is so particular about labels. The player may explain that the sign is literal, say that Ada insists on it, or admit that they do not know more than the written instruction. The mechanic can answer, “I don't need more than that. I need not to put the wrong thing in the wrong machine.”

The scene gives the player a way to transmit information without sharing Ruth's note. It distinguishes operational trust from social access. If the player says “Ruth asked me to keep clear,” Ada corrects them: the instruction belongs to the work area, not to a personal message that the player may use to establish status.

## 47. A separate conversation with the clinic runner

The clinic runner asks whether the sisters can make another case. The player can repeat only what the operators actually said, ask the runner to request it directly, or decline to speak on the sisters' behalf. The runner is allowed to be frustrated by the extra trip. The story does not make the clinic's need trivial simply because Ada has a boundary.

If the runner asks about Ruth, the player can say, “That isn't part of the order,” or “Ask Ada about the press.” The runner accepts the redirection and asks about the container instead. This allows the same boundary to be held in another conversation without making Ada attend every external exchange.
