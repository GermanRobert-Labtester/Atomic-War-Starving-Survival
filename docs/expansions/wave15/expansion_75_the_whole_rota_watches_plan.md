# EXPANSION 75 — THE WHOLE ROTA WATCHES

## A prose-first content expansion plan for Arvo Tamm

### Wave 15: The Ordinary Measure

## 1. Creative promise

This expansion gives the existing Arvo Tamm arc a full human scale. It takes the moment already present in the quest catalog—a fair mess, a private crate of good oil, and a cook weighing what kind of person he is—and develops the conversations before and after the player's choice. It does not add a new moral branch. The player still chooses between offering amnesty so the crate goes to the kettle, or exposing it and letting the rota decide.

The story's subject is not whether Arvo is secretly generous or secretly corrupt. He has kept the portions exact and has kept one private reserve. Both facts matter. The player is asked to decide what to do with that contradiction while people who eat at the mess are in the room.

## 2. One-sentence story

In Vansen's stripped department store, the player helps a cook face the one good ingredient he kept for himself, then lives with the different kind of meal and different kind of trust produced by amnesty or exposure.

## 3. Story mode

This is a prose expansion for the existing recurring NPC quest, not a new economy feature. Its main play is dialogue, staging, a workday at the mess, and the existing two-choice resolution. The choice identifiers remain arvo_amnesty and arvo_expose. The current consequences remain authoritative.

The story can be delivered through one encounter with a strong before-and-after scene, or through the existing quest's state variants. This plan does not require additional save data, portion simulation, food quality stats, or a new cook assignment. The content's scale is in its scene writing and the way the store feels when a meal is served.

## 4. Verified anchor: the department store

Vansen's Department Store has six floors. Its description says the first year stripped the useful stock, later years picked through what remained, and now the fittings, counters, and displays are what salvage visitors find. Window mannequins still wear clothes for a season that did not arrive. The service floors hold fixtures, wiring, brass, and copper. The store is quiet above, with thirty-six rads per hour and a moderate danger label.

The expedition catalog lists five distance ticks, danger six, an encounter chance, stamina drain, a shopping-center scavenging table, and categories including cloth, canned food, batteries, and medical kits. These data prove an expedition row, not a safe route or a guarantee that the mess stores any of those items. The story uses the store's scale and left-behind displays, not an invented retail inventory.

## 5. Verified anchor: Arvo

Arvo's character entry calls him a mess cook. He fed an institution for twenty years and never stopped cooking as if a delivery might be late. He runs the department-store mess honestly, keeps one private crate of good oil, and has moved it twice since autumn. The crate is not for sale; the record calls it an argument he is having with himself.

His wants are herbal tea, dry textiles, and not to be counted while counting. His offers include exact institutional portions, a seat at the mess, and kitchen stewardship. He will not short the rota or admit to the crate unless it matters. His signature line says institutions fail when the cook starts eating differently from those being served.

## 6. Verified anchor: the existing quest

The current recurring-NPC quest is titled “Which Kind of Cook,” available in its listed day range and attached to the Provisioned faction. Its synopsis describes the exact crate and Arvo's internal argument. Its two choices are:
- offer amnesty: the crate goes to the mess kettle, the secret stays at the door;
- expose the crate and let the rota decide.

The amnesty consequence says he pours the oil into the kettle with the whole rota watching and cooks differently afterward. The exposure consequence says the rota votes, the mess continues under a cook who does not know why deliveries were exactly late, and Arvo is expelled. This plan treats those outcomes as fixed canon and expands the scene around them.

## 7. Verified character arc outcomes

The NPC arc already has a state for the amnesty path: the crate is made speakable, the oil goes into the kettle, and Arvo later cooks differently. A late quartermaster state can follow that choice. The exposure path has an expelled-cook state: the mess keeps the old standard under a successor, while Arvo trades oil by the flask on the road. A late unmet state remains if the player does not resolve the quest.

This plan does not rewrite the state conditions, day gates, role names, recruitment availability, faction label, or choice requirements. Any prose adaptation must use those existing branch outcomes. If a code or data owner changes the state model later, re-audit the story before using these lines.

## 8. The central contradiction

The portions are exact. The oil is private. The mess is fair. The reserve is not. The story does not tell the player that one fact erases the other. Arvo has fed an institution for two decades, and his experience appears in the work he does every day. He has also chosen to set aside something good, move it twice, and keep it beyond the rota's count.

The player may see the crate as a betrayal, a boundary, a reserve for a future he has not admitted, or a burden he can no longer carry alone. The story need not endorse one interpretation. Its job is to make the cost of each response legible before the player chooses.

## 9. The store as a kitchen

The store's mess is proposed on a service floor, a place with workable drains, enough floor area for a communal table, and access to the back service corridor. This exact layout is a staging proposal, not a fact in current location data. The plan does not claim Vansen's had a restaurant, food court, or surviving stove.

Use existing scene assets or generic interior staging if available. The store's identity can come from the broad counters, mannequins visible through a distant display window, and the difference between public floors and service space. Do not require a new dedicated wing, shopping UI, or hidden archive. The mess is simply the working kitchen where the current character record already places Arvo.

## 10. The crate

The current source says the crate holds good oil and has been moved twice since autumn. It does not give the oil's origin, quantity, exact storage floor, or intended use. The quest consequence establishes that it goes to the mess kettle on the amnesty branch. Before that outcome, the crate should appear closed or partly screened, not opened by the player without Arvo's consent.

A proposed prop can have a rubbed place where a label used to be. This detail should be used only if the writer is comfortable treating it as new authored content. It must not become a hidden delivery manifest or family keepsake. The crate's importance is its known material value and Arvo's decision, not a mystery item inside it.

## 11. Point of view

The story stays close to the meal, the exact portions, the voices around the rota, and Arvo's handling of the crate. It does not enter his private thoughts. The player can notice the way he counts, changes the subject, looks toward the upper floor once, or sets a ladle down before answering.

Arvo's own lines can describe his argument with himself. His biography already names it as an argument; the plan lets him articulate that pressure in scene dialogue. The text should not narrate that he stole, because no source defines ownership or how the reserve came to exist. It should not absolve him as a secret benefactor either.

## 12. Tone

The mess is disciplined, not militarized. It can have a rota, a wall chalkboard, portions measured by a scoop, and a line that forms before the pot is ready. No one speaks like a policy document. People are allowed to dislike the same meal for different reasons.

The writing should avoid sentimental “one drop makes everything better” imagery. The good oil changes how the food tastes, but it cannot make a shortage disappear or reconcile a vote. The meal is still a meal. Arvo remains a cook, not a symbol who exists to teach the player about fairness.

## 13. Supporting cast

The existing source does not name mess workers or diners. Use role labels until current character data provides names: rota caller, dish washer, older regular, young runner, late-shift worker. They can speak one or two lines each and need not become recruitable characters.

The rota should have mixed opinions. One person values the exact portions because they stop favoritism. Another is tired of eating food that is measured correctly but tastes like the container. A third will not speak in public because the cook controls no one's ration alone. None represents the entire settlement. The player's decision should not be mistaken for a unanimous moral verdict.

## 14. Opening image: portion line

The player enters while Arvo sets the serving scoop on a clean cloth. There are bowls on one side of the counter and a chalk line on the other. A mess worker calls names by rota order. Arvo does not watch who looks disappointed; he watches the portion land level.

The player can sit and be served, stand aside, or ask if the line has room for one more. Arvo answers only about the current place in the rota. The room does not greet the player with the crate. The first view shows a working kitchen, giving the player a concrete baseline before any allegation or confession.

## 15. Opening meal text

A proposed first bowl is warm and sufficient. It is not described as good. The steam carries a plain starch smell; the surface is even, not oily. Arvo asks whether the amount is right. If the player says yes, he asks whether the flavor is right. The separation matters: a correct quantity does not imply a satisfying meal.

If the player says the portion is too small, Arvo shows the scoop and the line of bowls. He does not argue that hunger is solved by arithmetic. If the portion is too large, he asks the player to leave the excess on the return tray rather than make a performance of refusing it. The opening serves as a living demonstration of his discipline.

## 16. First conversation: “institutional-exact”

Proposed dialogue:

> “You serve every bowl the same?”
>
> “I serve the same portion on the same rota.”
>
> “That isn't what I asked.”
>
> “It's the answer I can stand behind.”
>
> “And if somebody needs more?”
>
> “Then they need more. The scoop doesn't decide what they need. It decides what I served.”

The exchange should not make Arvo sound proud of indifference. He knows that fairness and sufficiency are different. The player can ask who handles exceptions; Arvo says the current kitchen has a process or, if the data has none, that the person needs to speak to the rota caller. No new ration authority is invented.

## 17. The delivery arrives late

A runner carries an empty sack to the service door and says the usual delivery did not arrive at the expected time. Arvo checks the day's entry. The player's line can ask what was expected, whether the kitchen has enough for today's rota, or if the runner wants help carrying the sack away.

The word “late” matters because Arvo's biography says he cooks as if deliveries may be late. The story does not claim a repeated conspiracy or make an unidentified supplier responsible. The delay is a present fact in this scene; its cause is unknown unless current game data establishes it.

## 18. His eyes go to the upper floor

The current encounter description says Arvo's eyes go once, exactly once, to the upper floor. This is the reveal's seed. The scene should honor the singular glance: he does not stare at it every time the player speaks, and no camera arrow points toward a glowing crate.

The player can notice and ask about the upper floor. Arvo does not deny that there is something there. He asks whether the player came to ask about the meal or the room above it. If the player says the meal, the conversation can return to the rota. If they say the crate, the current quest's story opens.

## 19. No inventory search

The crate is not discovered by the player breaking into a floor, looting Arvo's private storage, or searching a room without invitation. Its reveal belongs to his existing NPC quest and its social interaction. The player receives the option to address the matter after the encounter points them toward it.

If the player's expedition system can ordinarily scavenge the store, that mechanic remains separate. No new prop should be exposed as a loot container. The story explicitly avoids making Arvo's private reserve a collectible whose ownership becomes a gameplay prompt.

## 20. The question that opens the quest

When the player asks about the crate, Arvo answers: “It has oil.” If the player asks why it is stored upstairs, he says, “Because I moved it twice.” If they ask whose it is, he says, “Mine, until I decide what that means.” These are proposed lines, not current canon. They invite the existing moral question without asserting how the oil was acquired.

The player can ask about the meal instead, ask what is in the crate, or say that he does not have to show them. The third response leaves space for Arvo to choose to continue. The quest must not force the player to interrogate him before they can understand why the crate matters.

## 21. Arvo's experience

Arvo's source bio says he fed an institution for twenty years. A proposed monologue can refer to the habit of setting bowls in the same order, checking the pot before the delivery, and starting the meal before the last cart had arrived. Those are authored details that extend his background, not verified history of a named institution.

He can say that the work teaches you to recognize a missing delivery by the way people ask whether there is enough, not by the empty place in the storeroom. He has not stopped cooking as if the truck might be late. This voice makes the habit concrete while avoiding a flashback to a specific war, employer, or named kitchen not present in the source.

## 22. The private crate as a sentence

Arvo says the crate is the only thing in the store that does not have a portion beside it. He has moved it twice since autumn. The phrase does not disclose the route of those moves, and the prose should not set dates. He has kept it apart because the oil is good, because he wants to know what it would be like to cook with it, and because he has not decided what keeping it says about him.

This is proposed dialogue that expands the biography's stated inner argument. It is not a claim that the crate was meant for a feast, a celebration, or a private meal. The oil's value is established by his reluctance to use it and by the way he watches the serving line, not by a new catalog price.

## 23. The rota is not a jury

The people on the rota have authority to decide what happens in the exposure branch, as the existing choice states. They are not a single voice and should not be staged like a courtroom. No prosecutor stands at the door. The player can tell the room what they saw, ask Arvo to say it himself, or present the choice without embellishment.

The scene should make public exposure feel materially different from an amnesty conversation. More people hear the crate named. Their first reactions are mixed. A vote may be quick because the consequences are already familiar to them, not because nobody cares. Avoid a theatrical unanimous shout unless the current quest outcome text is adapted by an author who confirms that detail.

## 24. The player is not the rota

In both paths, the player can propose but cannot appoint themselves judge of the mess. If they expose the crate, they let the rota decide; they do not dictate the vote. If they offer amnesty, they ask Arvo to put the oil into the kettle and leave the secret at the door; they do not erase every feeling the room may have about it.

The player's dialogue should not say, “I forgive you on everyone's behalf,” or “The settlement has voted,” unless the player has actually completed the respective branch. This distinction keeps the two source choices intact and prevents a heroic protagonist from displacing the people who eat at the mess.

## 25. The question Arvo asks himself

Arvo may ask: “If I pour it in, do I become the cook who kept it too long or the cook who finally used it?” The player can answer that he has to decide, that it can be both, or that the rota should hear the facts. Each line gives a distinct point of view without replacing the existing choice.

The sentence captures his existing argument. It should not be the only question in the story; the diners' experience, the kitchen's exact portions, and the way a decision is carried out also matter. If a writer adds a reflection, it should stay anchored to this kitchen rather than expanding into an abstract lecture about institutions.

## 26. The cook has a private reserve

Arvo does not pretend that the crate is public property. He also does not tell the player that he has already decided what to do with it. He says it is good oil and the mess has not used it. The source does not say when he acquired it, who paid for it, or whether it was owed to the rota. The plan leaves those questions open unless a later authoring review finds current evidence.

A player can ask whether the crate was paid for from the mess stores. Arvo can answer, “I have no record that says it was.” This is not a confession of theft or proof that the oil belongs to him. It is a statement that he cannot establish its origin in the way the rota would require. The scene must not promote an unknown chain of custody into a finding.

## 27. The first response options

The current encounter has two choices, and the expansion should preview their character without adding a third:

1. Offer amnesty: the crate goes to the mess kettle and the secret stays at the door.
2. Expose the crate and let the rota decide.

A player who is not ready can close the conversation if the existing quest framework permits deferral. That is not a third resolution. It is an unresolved state already reflected in the NPC arc. The player can return within the quest's current day range. No new “hide the crate” choice should be added.

## 28. Choosing amnesty: the private offer

The player offers amnesty privately, without announcing a verdict to the room. They say that the crate should go into the kettle, and the secret should stay at the door. Arvo asks if the player is promising that no one will hear about it. The player may answer that the choice is to keep the private fact private, or ask Arvo to decide whether he can accept that condition.

The content should not make amnesty sound like a transaction that guarantees Arvo's gratitude. He can hesitate, ask for the offer to be repeated, and check whether the oil goes to the mess rather than to the player's inventory. The existing quest says the crate goes to the kettle. That destination is the center of the offer.

## 29. Choosing exposure: the public account

The player can tell Arvo they will expose the crate and let the rota decide. Before the room is gathered, the player can ask whether he wants to state the facts himself or have the player repeat what they saw. The player must not embellish. Arvo is entitled to answer in his own words.

The exposure scene should name only the supported facts: the mess portions have been kept exact; a private crate of good oil exists; it has been moved twice since autumn; the player found out; the rota will decide. It should not accuse him of stealing the oil, hiding a shortage, or feeding himself better. The existing choice uses “expose the crate,” not “convict the cook.”

## 30. A late delivery on the same day

A runner appears with a small package for the store. Arvo stops preparing the evening meal long enough to check its mark. This beat shows the habit his biography describes: he cooks as if the delivery might be late. The package may contain a mundane ingredient if current data authorizes it, but the plan does not specify an item or quantity.

The player's attention can shift to the package, and Arvo lets it. The crate story is not a trap whose clue remains frozen while everything else stops. He can finish the immediate kitchen task and return to the personal decision. This gives the scene a human rhythm and makes clear that an unresolved crate does not erase the rest of his work.

## 31. The exact portion in the player's hands

Arvo serves the player a portion using the same scoop used for the rota. He asks whether they want to compare it with the marked bowl. The player can say that it looks the same, that they do not want to inspect another person's food, or that they expected something better.

The bowl is not a proof that every distribution in the store is perfect. It is a limited experience of the mess's stated standard. Arvo's exactness can be real and still fail to make people feel well fed. A player may appreciate the fairness and dislike the flavor, or distrust the policy and value the predictable amount. The dialogue should support both reactions.

## 32. A diner who defends the scoop

One regular says that the exact portion is the first thing they trust in the building. Another says that a level scoop can still be a level scoop of thin food. These lines are authored proposals. The characters should not be given names unless existing roster data provides them; they can remain mess regulars with a distinct presence.

The first regular may ask the player not to make the crate into a public trial. The second may ask why a better ingredient is kept above the kitchen. Both questions are reasonable. The scene lets the player hear the pressure without allowing one diner to speak for the whole room.

## 33. A diner who does not vote

The room's process allows that kind of silence if the current quest data supports a vote.

## 34. The crate has moved twice

If Arvo tells the player why he moved it, he gives two concrete reasons that are proposals: a delivery needed room on one landing, and the store's upper floors were being checked for salvage. These reasons should be used only if approved. They must not imply he moved it to evade a named search or because the oil was stolen.

A more restrained line leaves the reasons unknown: “I didn't want it in one place.” When the player asks why, he says, “That is the part I can't make sound like an answer.” This honors the record's phrase that the crate is his argument. The line does not claim that the crate itself has moved due to an external threat.

## 35. What he means by good oil

Arvo can explain that “good” means he can cook differently with it, as the existing outcome says. He is not required to name a grade, source, or market price. The item catalog should define the actual oil item if the game needs one; the prose can describe the kitchen result without making a new commodity.

A diner who asks what “differently” means can answer with a small sensory observation after the amnesty path: the smell changes first, or the surface holds warmth differently. These are candidate descriptions, not technical claims. The meal does not become a feast or a morale potion. A good ingredient changes the work and the taste; it does not change how many bowls exist.

## 36. The public-floor mannequins

From the kitchen door, one mannequin is visible on the public floor, dressed in clothes that do not fit the current weather. Arvo looks at the display and says he has never served a meal to a mannequin. The line can be a moment of dry humor, not a thesis about consumerism.

Another possible line: “It has kept the same size through all the seasons.” This can contrast the mannequin's fixed display with the mess's changing needs. Use one or the other, not both in every scene. The store's visual identity should sit in the background and not steal the choice from the people eating.

## 37. The upstairs route is ordinary

The crate's placement may bring the player upstairs, but the story must not turn the upper floor into a stealth dungeon. If an authored walk-through is needed, the route is short and safe within existing location rules, using the current expedition context. The upper landing can show mannequins, empty counters, and one closed service door.

The exact crate location remains a production choice because the source only states that Arvo looks once toward the upper floor. Do not place it in a secret vault or create an unlisted storage room. A visible service stair and a plain crate are enough. If current location rendering cannot support that scene, the crate can remain offscreen and be named in dialogue.

## 38. The cook's bowl

Arvo's own bowl is the same size as the others in the initial scene. The player can ask if he eats the same portion. He says that he eats when his turn comes, or that the rota has a place for him. Do not assert that he has never eaten privately; the crate is the one private reserve named by his source, but this line can be an authored present-day fact only if approved.

His signature quote about a cook eating differently from the served is a principle, not proof about every meal in his life. A scene may let him acknowledge that he already knows the difference: “I've been asking myself which bowl I think I deserve.” The line expands his self-argument, not the inventory of private meals.

## 39. A player who asks “Why not eat it yourself?”

Arvo answers that he has not opened the crate because deciding who should receive it is different from deciding what he wants. The line is proposed. If the player says that he should be allowed to eat it, Arvo asks whether that is what the player believes or what they want him to hear. This keeps the exchange open without making him a manipulator.

If the player says the oil should belong to the rota, Arvo says that is one answer. If the player says it should be his, Arvo says that is another. He does not treat the player's view as a ruling. Only the existing quest choice moves the story to amnesty or exposure.

## 40. A player who asks “Why not give it away?”

Arvo can answer that giving it away would still choose who gets it. He has not found a way to make that choice disappear by changing the name. The player may say that the rota should choose, or that the kitchen has already chosen by serving exact portions.

This conversation speaks to the difference between generosity and process. It does not make charitable donation a third outcome. If the player tries to put the crate into their own inventory to distribute it elsewhere, the narrative refuses that action unless an existing item authority explicitly supports it. The crate's story is not a scavenging reward.

## 41. What counts as “fair”

The story gives the player several definitions to discuss: same amount, same ingredients, a need-based exception, or an open record. Arvo can say that the scoop only answers the first. A rota caller can say that a special need is handled by the current settlement's existing process, if one exists. The player can say that fairness needs a person listening.

No answer settles the term forever. A proposed dialogue tree should let each line stand as a worldview rather than labeling one “good.” The current quest's two choices remain separate decisions about secrecy and the crate; they do not become a referendum on every meal policy in the game.

## 42. Shortage without a villain

The story must not invent a saboteur who caused every late delivery. A delay can be caused by ordinary uncertainty: a route took longer, the runner does not know, or the store received no notice. If the current campaign state supplies a cause, use it; otherwise say it is unknown.

Arvo's preparation for late arrivals is a habit, not evidence of conspiracy. The player may suspect someone is taking food, but the scene does not confirm that suspicion. That restraint lets the existing crate remain the one specific contradiction rather than an opening to an unrelated crime plot.

## 43. Conversation with the rota caller

A role-labeled rota caller asks Arvo whether every bowl is still exact. He says yes. The caller asks whether the crate has touched the stores. Arvo says no, not in the way they record them. This proposed exchange makes the distinction between fair serving and private reserve explicit without claiming the caller discovered a shortage.

The player can intervene by stating only what they know, ask Arvo to answer himself, or defer to the existing exposure choice. If the player chooses amnesty, the caller does not learn the secret unless the current consequence says otherwise. If the player chooses exposure, the room hears the same facts before it decides.

## 44. Does the crate belong to the institution?

The source does not establish the crate's provenance. The story may make that uncertainty part of Arvo's burden. The player can ask if there is a mark on it. Arvo says the mark does not answer the question he is asking. If a prop label exists, it should not be treated as a legal deed or proof of ownership.

A lore review should reject any line that states the crate was a personal ration, a gift, a purchase, or a diversion unless current data supports it. The emotional point does not require a theft backstory. What the player can see is that Arvo has held good oil apart and that the mess has not used it.

## 45. How the room hears the word “amnesty”

If the player says “amnesty” aloud in front of the rota, a diner asks who is being forgiven. The player may restate the offer privately to Arvo, call it an agreement, or admit that amnesty is an imprecise word. The existing choice text includes amnesty; the scene can explore its weight without renaming the choice ID.

Arvo may say that the word makes it sound as if he asked to be pardoned. He has not. He did not ask the player for this offer. The player can answer that the offer still needs his consent. This moment prevents a merciful choice from becoming paternalistic: amnesty is proposed, not imposed.

## 46. How the room hears the word “expose”

The word “expose” can sound like a performance. A diner asks whether the player means to tell the rota or to shame Arvo in front of it. The player can say that the facts should be heard, that they are angry, or that they are not sure. Arvo does not interrupt to control the answer.

The choice remains “Expose the crate and let the rota decide.” The narrative should show what gets exposed: the existence of the crate and its separation from the mess stores. It should not stage a public accusation that Arvo falsified portions. Accuracy matters because the current source explicitly says the portions have been fair.

## 47. An honest invitation to leave

Arvo may say, “You can leave this alone if you have to.” The player can answer that the quest asks them to decide, that it is not theirs, or that they want to hear the rota first. The line offers emotional space but does not create a third resolution. If the current system does not allow deferral, omit the line rather than promising a postponement the game cannot honor.

If deferred, the crate remains in the same state: still private, still moved twice, still the unresolved argument. The player can return and find Arvo doing the work. This persistent behavior is already represented by the late-unmet arc, not a newly implemented stash simulation.

## 48. A question Arvo will not answer

A player may ask, “If I choose amnesty, will you become a different cook?” Arvo says he does not know. If asked whether he wants the rota to forgive him, he says the question is whether they should have the oil. If asked whether he is afraid, he can say, “Of the room? No. Of what I think when it is quiet? Yes.” That last line is proposed and should be reviewed for melodrama.

The answer must not funnel the player toward one branch. Arvo can be frightened in either outcome. His choice is not a puzzle to solve by identifying the correct emotional cue. The source already gives his inner argument; the player may recognize it without being asked to cure it.

## 49. Act I close: the bowl and the crate

The player finishes the first bowl and places it on the return tray. Arvo picks up the scoop, checks that it is clean, and puts it back in its place. Above, the crate remains closed. The player can ask to hear what the rota thinks, ask Arvo to speak first, or return to the current quest choice.

This image puts the two objects in the same frame without claiming they are equivalent: a bowl used by everyone and a crate set aside. The story does not answer which one is morally heavier. It gives the player enough context to make a choice while leaving the people in the room free to disagree.

## 50. Act II: the room gathers

When the player chooses to bring the rota together, Arvo does not ring a bell or make an announcement that turns the mess into a tribunal. He tells the people who are already present that there is something they need to hear before the next meal. The caller asks whether the line can wait. Arvo says it can wait until the bowls are set down.

The pause is practical, not ceremonial. People finish eating; a worker carries the pot away from the edge of the counter. The player can wait, repeat the purpose, or ask Arvo whether he wants to go on. The gathered room is made of people with shifts and hunger, not an audience assembled to watch a moral performance.

The player can ask Arvo to say the words himself. If the player repeats them, they must not add a claim about where the oil came from or what Arvo planned to do with it.

No one answers immediately. A dish washer puts a stack of bowls down with more force than necessary. The rota caller looks at Arvo and asks whether the crate is still there. He says yes. Someone asks why he told the player first. Arvo says that he did not; the player noticed the question in the room.

This exchange complicates the reveal without creating a betrayal subplot. Arvo's eyes moved toward the upper floor. The player asked. The room is entitled to wonder why it did not know. Arvo does not claim the player discovered a theft. The discussion stays on the terms already named.

## 53. A diner counts the bowls

One diner asks if the portions were short. The rota caller checks the serving marks. Arvo says the scoop was level every night. The diner says, “Then the count was right.” Another says, “The count isn't all I wanted to know.” Both lines can coexist.
