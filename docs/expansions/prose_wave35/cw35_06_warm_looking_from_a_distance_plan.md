# EXPANSION CW35-06 — Warm-Looking from a Distance

## A resort story about the misleading comfort of a preserved silhouette.

### Prose Wave 35: Places That Keep Their Questions

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 48 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (192 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `location_abandoned_ski_resort` — Abandoned Ski Resort.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Abandoned Ski Resort preserves a surface image of comfort: cable cars hang mid-swing, coats look warm at a distance, and the rooms remain as they were left. The location describes passengers still inside the cars and says the wealthy who ran for the mountains died warm and well-dressed, a comparison that has become difficult to separate from the mannequins. This plan treats the place with restraint. It keeps human remains out of spectacle, refuses to invent names, and asks what distance lets a visitor mistake for safety. Its content follows one reader deciding how to describe a scene without claiming to know the last minutes of its occupants. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

What can a visitor say about a preserved scene without turning distance into a story about the people inside it?

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The location’s visual facts are the stalled cable cars, passengers inside, luxury resort rooms, seized lifts, grey snow, and mannequins. Its description compares the dead to mannequins and says wealthy occupants died warm and dressed. No names, relationships, individual causes, or exact chronology are given. The prose does not add a rescue attempt or identify any body. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The center is the visitor who sees a coat and first thinks of warmth, then learns to correct the thought without claiming to know the person wearing it. The people in the scene are not props and do not become a puzzle. A careful narrator can choose a less dramatic word, keep distance, and let the silence belong to the place. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

A visitor and a companion speak in short, practical corrections. No source character or resort worker is named. A later copyist records what was visible and refuses to infer who paid, who left first, or what any passenger expected. Avoid giving mannequins speech or turning an appearance into a ghost story. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 8. Player agency

The player can look from a distance, choose not to approach a car, ask a narrow question, or leave. The prose does not force inspection of a body or reward a disturbing close-up. Existing expedition, hazard, inventory, and location systems retain all actual effects. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not depict remains graphically, identify passengers, or infer a final conversation. Do not provide medical, radiation, or cold-exposure advice. Do not treat the dead as mannequins in narrator voice; preserve the source’s comparison as the discomfort that the living must examine. Do not turn the location into a horror reveal. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Approach — the image from below

Begin at a distance where coats and cars are visible but individual identity is not.

### 2. Correction — warm-looking is not warmth

Let a visitor revise their first thought without detailing bodies or inventing weather behavior.

### 3. Interior — rooms kept as they were

Use the source’s unchanged rooms and resort objects without giving each one a former owner.

### 4. Comparison — mannequins are not people

Keep the source’s disturbing comparison visible as a narrator’s problem, not as a fact about the dead.

### 5. Choice — a visitor stops at the threshold

Player agency includes not approaching a cable car or chalet.

### 6. Return — a careful description survives

Close with wording that distinguishes what can be seen from what cannot be known.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Use a distant view and one correction in wording. Do not depict remains or name an occupant.

### Interlinked

If an existing location consumer exposes more exact setting details, preserve only the current source’s wording and author attribution.

### Wild card

Write a short field card with two columns: visible from here and not known from here. The second column is not a clue list.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
