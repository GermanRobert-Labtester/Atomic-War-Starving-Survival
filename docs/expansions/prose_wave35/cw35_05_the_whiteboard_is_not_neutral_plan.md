# EXPANSION CW35-05 — The Whiteboard Is Not Neutral

## A shelter-room story about shared space, old agendas, and the handwriting left visible.

### Prose Wave 35: Places That Keep Their Questions

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 48 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (192 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_shelter_meeting` — Shelter Meeting Room.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Shelter Meeting Room is a repurposed municipal conference chamber with a long salvaged table and unmatched chairs. Whiteboards still hold the last pre-war agenda; new hands have added water rations, duty roster, and radiation count in different marker colors. The location calls the room the closest thing the shelter has to neutral ground. This plan treats neutrality as a practice people keep attempting, not a magic property of a room. Its game content follows who writes, who reads, and which agenda survives erasure without turning the meeting into a new governance system. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

What makes a room neutral when every mark on its board was made by someone with a need?

The location calls it the closest thing to neutral ground. No attendees, meeting procedure, vote, outcome, or author of any board line is identified. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The people at the center are those who enter carrying separate needs and sit in chairs that do not match. A neutral room cannot remove disagreement; it can only give people a surface where the same wording stays visible long enough to be read back. Give each voice an ordinary stake and let unfinished items remain unfinished. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

A note-taker distinguishes old agenda text from current additions. A participant asks who wrote a line. Another answers only for what they saw. All are anonymous editorial roles. No speaker becomes the canonical chair or claims authority over the shelter’s policy. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

Candidate content may be placed on a verified meeting-room surface after current consumer and route checks. Board notes and exchanges are draft prose, not actual policy, roster, ration, or radiation state. No new vote, agenda manager, meeting event, or save authority is proposed. All fragments are candidates. None is evidence that the location is currently player-reachable. Before selecting any text, verify the current map/location owner, content schema, actual consumer, and the source condition under which the text can appear.

## 8. Player agency

The player can read, offer a wording correction, pass the marker, decline to speak, or leave. They need not settle the water, duty, or radiation topic just because it appears on the board. Existing owners retain real policy and resource outcomes. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not invent who attended the pre-war meeting, what its agenda decided, or who wrote current notes. Do not present colors as faction identity or severity. Do not add vote counts, attendance, policy, or a neutral-ground guarantee. The player is not forced to mediate. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Chamber — chairs that do not match

Begin with salvaged furniture and the repurposed room. Do not invent a meeting already in progress.

### 2. Old agenda — text that survived its use

A reader distinguishes remaining pre-war writing from current additions without claiming the old items remain active.

### 3. New lines — three present concerns

Water rations, duty roster, and radiation count are named exactly as board topics, not as resolved policies.

### 4. Color — authorship without faction

Different markers show that more than one hand wrote, not who belongs to which group.

### 5. Neutrality — closest is not absolute

The scene lets disagreement happen in a room people still consider useful.

### 6. Close — a marker is set down

End with wording preserved or passed along, not a vote or formal decision.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Stage one reading of the board and a short disagreement over wording; leave all topics unresolved.

### Interlinked

Only reflect a current policy if its owning system provides it; the board draft itself cannot alter state.

### Wild card

Give each marker color a different anonymous author voice, then explicitly refuse to map color to identity.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
