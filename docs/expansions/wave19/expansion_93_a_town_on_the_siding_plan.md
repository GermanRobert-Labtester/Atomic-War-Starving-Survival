# EXPANSION 93 — A Town on the Siding

## A prose-first settlement-content expansion for Iron Siding’s freight-car homes, its public repair queue, and the value of steel after the trains stopped being the whole point.

### Wave 19: Places Kept by Hand

## 1. Creative promise

Iron Siding is a town built inside the practical remains of freight service. The writing keeps the place human-sized: Korov maintains a perimeter and a community, Bess deals in parts whose sizes matter, and Milos turns railroad steel into work the settlement can use. It does not turn the guild into a combat feature pitch. This is a prose-first game-content plan. It offers candidate scenes, dialogue, notices, work records, and repeat-visit text. It is not a feature roadmap and does not claim that any proposed prose is already in the game.

## 2. One-sentence story

At Iron Siding, three kinds of work meet around freight-car steel: keeping the settlement legible, trading for the parts it can use, and remaking material without pretending its history is simple.

## 3. Verified local anchor

`loc_settlement_iron_siding` is a modular rail-car town buried beneath railroad ballast near Span 44. Its description says work gangs forge high-tensile hardware and armor plates from freight-car steel; location values are danger 3, 2.5 travel hours, and 20 rads per hour. Character data places Master Korov, Bess the Chandler, and Milos the Riveter at the site. Korov governs the armored boxcar community and defense perimeter. Bess supplies forged track bolts, leaf springs, and mechanical spares. Milos reforges railroad steel into barrier armor and vehicle suspension plates. The inspected NPC-arc, quest, and narrative-encounter catalogs contain no current arc or encounter for them. The character records point to the location, and the location records provide the setting description. The selected characters have no current NPC arc, quest, or narrative encounter entry in the inspected catalogs. Their authored presence does not prove that a player can reach the location through a particular route.

## 4. Fixed source facts and new proposal

The source does not describe an active train line, a siege, a failed perimeter, or a specific shortage. Do not invent those facts as history. The modular rail-car community, Span 44 reference, trade and craft roles, and stated outputs are the fixed anchor. Do not provide defensive layout, weapons manufacture instructions, engineering specifications, or guarantees about what any scrap can become. Do not turn a proposed scene into a claim about existing canon or a guaranteed gameplay path.

## 5. Emotional center

A town made from rolling stock is still a town. A boxcar can be somebody’s home before it becomes a wall, and the same steel can be useful without erasing what it was built to carry. Korov’s boundary, Bess’s sales terms, and Milos’s bench each answer a different question; the player does not get to speak for all three. The place is not a prop for the player’s growth. The people here keep their own reasons, rhythms, and right to refuse a conversation.

## 6. Point of view and voices

The place speaks through close work and lived boundaries. Korov does not become a war speech; Bess does not become a shop interface; Milos does not teach a recipe. Let the residents’ space and craft carry the meaning. Narration stays close to observable work: who carries a note, what is posted, when a task stops, and who is present to answer. Private motives belong in a person’s own words or a record they have agreed to share. Secondary characters keep distinct duties and do not become a chorus explaining the settlement.

## 7. Proposed content sequence

A proposed visit begins at a lived-in rail car, moves through a posted yard boundary and a parts conversation, then follows an ordinary repair request across the chandler and smith’s separate roles. Later text can revisit an object after its use has been decided. No current game route or encounter ordering is asserted. This sequence is a writing proposal only. It does not introduce a quest ID, encounter registration, branching system, route connection, or persistent state. Dialogue can contain everyday responses, but no response is a hidden test or a substitute for an authored choice owner.

## 8. Human consequence without scorekeeping

A proposed scene can show a repair delayed until a part is identified, a public boundary restated, or a household choosing to keep an object in place. Do not add armor values, defensive strength, a rail-travel system, crafting recipes, or a new trade ledger. Let consequence appear in changed wording, a task assigned to its actual owner, a limit restated, or a return that is easier or more awkward. Do not imply numeric rewards, punishments, faction reputation, or new economic rules.

## 9. Location and safety discipline

Keep forge and perimeter prose at the level of decision, responsibility, and observable outcome. Do not specify temperatures, dimensions, fabrication steps, weapon parts, defensive angles, or a route across the ballast. Environmental risk stays grounded in the current location description. Do not teach hazardous access, underwater salvage, explosive handling, boiler repair, cliff travel, or any other dangerous procedure. A character can close a work area or refuse a request without the prose explaining a workaround.

Before implementation, verify the current data schema and consumer, author any needed IDs through the owning content path, and confirm route eligibility with current map and encounter authorities. Keep public and private records distinct. This plan changes no production JSON, code, save data, map, quest catalog, or master catalog.
