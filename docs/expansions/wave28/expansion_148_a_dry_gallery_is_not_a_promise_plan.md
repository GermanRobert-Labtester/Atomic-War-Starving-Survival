# EXPANSION 148 — A Dry Gallery Is Not a Promise

## The depot divides its stories at the waterline: a beacon asks for an answer, a rumor names a people, and a map labels a place that may not be here.

### Wave 28: What Survives the Record

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first location content plan with scene, record, conversation, and conditional revisit drafts.
**Quantity:** 26 story beats, four selectable forms per beat.
**Current location anchor:** `loc_flooded_subway_depot` — Flooded Subway Depot.
**Tone:** material, restrained, observant, and careful about the source’s unknowns.
**Canon sensitivity:** cited location and linked content records define the available facts; any other route or content reachability remains unproven.
**Scope:** editorial text only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, encounter, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Flooded Subway Depot is described as a drowned rail-maintenance yard whose dry upper galleries hold supply caches attributed to the Undertow and whose usual movement relies on rafts. Nearby records do not form a single confirmed expedition story: one dynamic template points to an erratic beacon and an Office investigation, a faction codex repeats an unproven rumor, an infestation row is disabled, and a survey entry labels a depot-related point as a bridge abutment. This expansion treats the depot as a place where records meet imperfectly. Its tension comes from deciding which statement belongs to which source, not from inventing a hidden route or a confirmed faction plot. The player-facing material should make the location feel inhabited by the act of observing, copying, disagreeing, and deciding what a sentence can support. The prose does not need a new system to create consequence: it can change the reader’s interpretation of a record while leaving the world state untouched.

## 2. Story question

When a beacon, a rumor, and a map all name the same stretch of drowned ground differently, what should the next reader copy—and what should they leave unresolved?

## 3. Verified local anchor

The location record calls this a flooded subway maintenance yard, notes dry upper galleries and supply caches attributed to the Undertow, and describes raft travel. The codex record calls the Undertow “Wreckers,” but its provenance is rumor. The investigation template describes an erratic beacon and names the Office as target; its listed geiger-counter reward is an existing template detail, not proof that a live quest is reachable here. The infestation catalog contains a molerat-nest row with trigger chance zero. A geodetic survey row names “Flooded Subway Depot Bridge,” assigns bridge_abutment as its point type, and carries gorge_shadow and trestle_decay tags. These records do not establish that a bridge exists at the location or that the rows share an event, person, or timeline.

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The depot’s waterlogged maintenance-yard identity, dry-gallery caches, Undertow attribution, and raft movement remain the only location facts. Keep rumor provenance visible when the Wreckers label appears. Keep the beacon template, infestation row, and bridge-abutment survey entry separate. A zero trigger chance is not an active nest, and a survey label is not a proven route. No cache quantity, live beacon event, bridge crossing, hostile encounter, or return path is added. The location description and linked records cited in this plan are fixed anchors. Existing characters retain their authored identity and choices; any additional working role is editorial only.

## 5. Human center

The people at the center are readers with reasons to make a mark legible: a depot copyist who wants the next shift to know what was actually seen, a cache keeper who dislikes being turned into a faction symbol, and a survey hand who knows a map can outlive its field correction. They can disagree without anyone becoming a villain or an oracle. The emotion should come through what a person chooses to write, omit, copy, disagree with, or leave unsigned rather than through a narrator announcing what the site means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Use the clipped language of maintenance labels and the cautious language of secondhand reports. The copyist favors nouns and dates; the cache keeper speaks in terms of what was left for whom; the survey hand distinguishes a plotted point from a walked path. Keep any new role anonymous and editorial. Do not give the beacon a voice or let the water produce a supernatural answer. Keep the author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know the history of an unnamed visitor; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing the world’s facts.

## 7. Placement and current reachability

The plan is anchored to the location row and the separate codex, dynamic-quest template, infestation catalog, and geodetic survey records named above. No current content consumer or actual route is established by those records alone. Any selected fragment requires a present-day check of the content owner, actual location route, and the condition that would display it. The survey naming seam is a note for provenance review, not permission to create a crossing. All fragments are optional editorial drafts. None is evidence that the location is currently player-reachable. If an editor later chooses any text, first verify the map/location owner, current content schema, actual consumer, and the source condition under which it can be shown.

## 8. Player agency

The player may read one note, ask which source a word came from, or leave the papers in their current order. Do not turn uncertainty into a dialogue test, faction choice, route unlock, cache claim, or pest-control action. If a passage appears near an existing interaction, its owner must determine placement without changing quest or world state. Let the player decide whether to read, ask one practical question, correct a transcription, or move on. Do not create a moral-choice menu merely to make quiet prose feel interactive. The people who work at the site retain control over their words and papers.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not provide directions, depths, wading or raft instructions, flood safety advice, water-treatment advice, pest-control options, cache contents, or bridge access. Do not state that Undertow members are Wreckers. Do not make the beacon live, the zero-chance infestation active, or the survey label a traversable bridge. No new faction, encounter, route, reward, survival rule, or map marker is introduced. Any newly introduced working role remains anonymous and editorial. Existing named characters keep their source identity and boundaries. Unknowns remain unknown; no absent person is given a biography to heighten emotion. Keep technical context descriptive and non-instructional.

## 10. Existing hooks and implementation boundary

**Existing content anchors:** the current `locations.json` record and the linked catalogs named in section 3. **Unverified:** any player-facing route, encounter, text consumer, state condition, or return trigger beyond what those records explicitly establish. **Classification:** editorial content proposal; no implementation category is claimed until an owner and consumer are verified.

This plan changes no code or game data. Do not add a parallel discovery registry or a new route solely to host these drafts. The plan is review material, not authorization to create a new gameplay authority.

## 11. Variation and selection

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not a sequence of four required encounters. Scene drafts stage the observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical disagreement; revisit vignettes let a later reader recognize changed wording. Select only the forms that fit an existing content owner. Shorten freely while retaining the source facts and each fragment’s boundary.

## 12. Editorial rubric

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep the physical object specific enough to belong to this location. The line should do practical work before it carries theme. Avoid exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and duplicated record wording.
