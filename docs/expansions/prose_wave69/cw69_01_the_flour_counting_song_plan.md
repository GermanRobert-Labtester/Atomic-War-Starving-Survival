## 1. Expansion thesis

The Flour Counting Song is a local children’s folklore record in childrens_folklore_expansion.json. Children sing it at the midday meal while tapping the table with spoons: one for the baker, two for bread, three for the flour the quartermaster said, four for the number nobody knows, five for the number the book shows, and six through eight for hunger, thinness, and flour running to nothing. The children do not know what the book means, but know the rhyme is about flour. This plan expands the song as scarcity remembered through rhythm, keeping its numbers and hunger language separate from the current economy authority.

## 2. Story question

When children tap a meal table through baker, bread, flour, hunger, and thinness while a book holds the number nobody knows, what does a counting song preserve when supplies become a mystery?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the folklore ID and exact prose. It does not establish the quartermaster’s name, the book’s identity, the meal’s menu, the true flour count, or whether the children understand the rhyme as history or play. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing folklore, child, meal, flour, quartermaster, book, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local prose: 'One for the baker, two for the bread, / three for the flour that the quartermaster said. / Four is the number that nobody knows, / five is the number that the book only shows. / Six is the hungry, seven is the thin, / eight is the number when the flour runs to nothing.' Sung by children at the midday meal, tapping the table with their spoons. The spoon is the beat. The beat is the spoon. The children do not know what the 'book only shows' means. The children know the rhyme is about the flour. The flour is the rhyme. The fuller reading keeps the song’s arithmetic deliberately incomplete. Its numbers are not a ration table. They are a way for children to make the absence of certainty audible while eating together. The spoon turns a table into a shared instrument, and the line about the book gives authority a distant shape that nobody can open from the lyric. A candidate passage may show a child changing four to another number, a quartermaster hearing the song without correcting it, or a book being carried past the meal without becoming an answer. The source supports hunger, memory, and the dignity of a group finding rhythm inside scarcity. It does not establish flour reserves, meal portions, a nutrition mechanic, or an economic forecast. Keep six, seven, and eight as the song’s movement toward thinness, not a prediction. Let the children know the rhyme is about flour even when the adults know the rhyme cannot tell them how much is left.
