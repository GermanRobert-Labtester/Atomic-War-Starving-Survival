## 1. Expansion thesis

The Silent Observatory appears in several current records, each with a different voice. The location description gives a high, cold dome with instruments mostly where the astronomers left them. `wish_reporter_observatory` already carries a reporter’s wish to see the stars and its exact completion. Marcus Olejnik’s day-52 journal entry reports Bram’s compass note and the word “LISTENING.” A separate automated scientific beacon points to pre-war data. This plan deepens the content around silence and attention while refusing to merge those three accounts into one answer. The player-facing material should make the location feel inhabited by the act of observing, copying, disagreeing, and deciding what a sentence can support. The prose does not need a new system to create consequence: it can change the reader’s interpretation of a record while leaving the world state untouched.

## 2. Story question

When a place is called silent but its records keep speaking, what should a listener leave unresolved?

## 3. Verified local anchor

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

Do not combine or alter these outcomes. The location description and linked records cited in this plan are fixed anchors. Existing characters retain their authored identity and choices; any additional working role is editorial only.

## 5. Human center

The human center is the reporter’s chosen return to a story they once covered. Alongside it sit other people’s records: Bram’s reluctance to return, Marcus’s account of that reluctance, and a machine asking for data retrieval rather than rescue. Respect each voice’s distance from the facts. The emotion should come through what a person chooses to write, omit, copy, or leave unsigned rather than through a narrator announcing what the site means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Let the cold and silence shape pacing without becoming an action tutorial. Keep the reporter’s speech intimate and spare; keep the journal’s second-hand account clearly attributed; keep the beacon mechanical. Do not solve the observatory’s mystery or promote an automated message into a living character. Keep the author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know the history of an unnamed visitor; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing the world’s facts.

## 7. Placement and current reachability

## 8. Player agency

No new dome control, astronomy objective, signal-decoding reward, or mystery choice is proposed. Let the player decide whether to read, ask one practical question, correct a transcription, or move on. Do not create a moral-choice menu merely to make quiet prose feel interactive. The people who work at the site retain control over their words and papers.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Keep Bram’s report second-hand, the beacon automated, and the reporter’s star-gazing outcome intact. Any newly introduced working role remains anonymous and editorial. Existing named characters keep their source identity and boundaries. Unknowns remain unknown; no absent person is given a biography to heighten emotion. Keep technical context descriptive and non-instructional.

## 10. Existing hooks and implementation boundary

**Existing content anchors:** the current `locations.json` record and the linked catalogs named in section 3. **Unverified:** any player-facing route, encounter, text consumer, state condition, or return trigger beyond what those records explicitly establish. **Classification:** editorial content proposal; no implementation category is claimed until an owner and consumer are verified.

## 11. Variation and selection

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not a sequence of four required encounters. Scene drafts stage the observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical disagreement; revisit vignettes let a later reader recognize changed wording. Select only the forms that fit an existing content owner. Shorten freely while retaining the source facts and each fragment’s boundary.

## 12. Editorial rubric

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep the physical object specific enough to belong to this location. The line should do practical work before it carries theme. Avoid exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and duplicated record wording.
