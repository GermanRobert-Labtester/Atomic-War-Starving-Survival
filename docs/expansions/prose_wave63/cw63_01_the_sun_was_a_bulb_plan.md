# EXPANSION CW63-01 — The Sun Was a Bulb

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 63: Children’s Inherited Outside

## Batch brief

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Sun Was a Bulb is a local children’s folklore record in bunker_children_folklore.json. Its prose is a four-line song saying the sun was a bulb in the blue, a billion times bigger than Room Twenty-Two, that it burned without coal or wire, and that nobody choked when it made its warm fire. The record says the song is sung in unison by children who have never seen open sky or natural shadows. This plan expands the song as inherited knowledge, wonder, and the careful difference between a child’s image and a fact about the world.

## 2. Story question

When children who have never seen open sky sing that the sun was a bulb living in the blue, what does a room imagine when it inherits light as a story?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the folklore ID and exact prose. It does not establish the children’s names, Room Twenty-Two’s current occupants, the song’s author, the song’s age, or whether any child believes every line literally. The prose preserves those unknowns and routes candidates only through an existing folklore, child, education, journal, or narrative consumer. Verbatim local text: "'The Sun was a bulb that lived in the blue, / A billion times bigger than Room Twenty-Two. / It burned without coal, it burned without wire, / And nobody choked when it made its warm fire.' Sung in unison by children who have never seen open sky or shadows cast by natural light." The fuller reading keeps the song in the space between knowledge and inheritance. The children have not seen open sky, so the sun begins as a bulb because a bulb is one of the forms of light their hands can imagine. Room Twenty-Two supplies a scale because rooms are measurable in the bunker even when a billion is not. The song does not need correction to become valuable. Its impossible claims are evidence of a community trying to hand an outside down through language. The line about burning without coal or wire is especially tender because it frees warmth from the infrastructure that governs every other light. The line about nobody choking gives the children a bodily ending they can understand, but it must remain a lyric claim rather than a promise about air or fire. A candidate passage may show a teacher, elder, or child asking whether the sun really lived in blue and then deciding that the song is not a test. It may show the unison weaken when one child changes a word, or grow stronger when the room needs the same image. These are editorial variations, not new education mechanics. The record ID is the authority for the folklore, and the source prose should remain visible to a reviewer. A return can let the song be heard in a different room, beside a drawing, or after a child learns that natural shadows exist. The later knowledge should not erase the earlier song. It can make the song more precious, stranger, or harder to sing. Verify the current folklore and child-content owner before placement. If no consumer can preserve the distinction between inherited image and world fact, retain the song as editorial reference. The bulb is not a lie that needs punishment. It is a small lamp held up toward a sky no one has touched.

The emotional center is children building an outside from the objects they know. The song is inaccurate in ways that make it tender: a bulb can be held, a room can be measured, and warmth can be imagined without choking. The unison matters because the children borrow certainty from one another.
