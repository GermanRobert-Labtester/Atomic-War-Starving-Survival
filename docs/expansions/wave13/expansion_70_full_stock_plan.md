# EXPANSION 70 — FULL STOCK
## An Armory 7 Records Story Plan
### Wave 13: The Wording in the Margin

**Document type:** narrative expansion and implementation plan  
**Primary location anchor:** Blacksite Armory 7  
**Status:** proposed content; evidence boundaries and new character names require owner review  
**Wave theme:** the wording in a record can preserve a promise while hiding who had to carry it  
**Target:** prose-rich, content-first expansion using existing narrative delivery and journal ownership  
**Explicit scope:** authored story, choices, documents, dialogue, and outcome summaries; no new gameplay feature or mutable authority

---

## 1. Expansion premise

A surviving supply manifest calls Blacksite Armory 7 “Full Stock” and describes EMP-hardened equipment. A separate map fragment records three elevator digits, 7, 4, and 9, while the remaining digits are water damaged. A third record identifies an authorization string: CHIMERA-7-4-9.

The story does not treat those records as one working access sequence. It asks what “Full Stock” meant to the people whose labor and names disappeared behind a clean inventory heading. The player helps a small archive group preserve the distinction between a recorded claim, a damaged fragment, and a present-tense fact they cannot establish.

The tension is human. An expedition lead wants the manifest to be actionable because the group needs supplies. A former records worker wants the phrase kept because it may explain what the institution promised. A mechanic who has spent years repairing absent parts wants the inventory word challenged. None of them is a villain, and no one can settle the question with a keypad.

## 2. Player-facing promise

The player will spend time with people who must decide whether to trust a document that sounds precise but does not prove current stock. They will read a manifest, listen to disagreements, ask what a number can and cannot establish, and choose how the archive annotates the phrase “Full Stock.”

The player will not receive a guaranteed armory haul, a new weapon tier, a tactical mission, or a functional access code. The outcome is a better record and a sharper understanding of how scarcity changes the way people read promises.

The story earns weight through particular details: a clerk who remembers counting empty shelf marks, a mechanic who can name the missing spring but not the person who borrowed it, an expedition lead who must tell others whether a promise counts as a plan, and an archivist who refuses to make a sentence look complete when the source is not.

## 3. Verified local anchors

The current data names a Blacksite Armory 7 location described as an unlisted military cache under a decommissioned radar station. Its description mentions blast berms, concertina wire, hardened lockers, defensive revetments, ammunition, and tactical equipment. The location record carries danger 7, travel 3.5, and radiation 35.

A map-zone fragment says the radar-station exterior is condemned while the substructure remains active. An elevator-access fragment preserves 7, 4, and 9, with the rest water damaged. A supply manifest reads “Armory 7 — Full Stock. EMP-hardened equipment. Authorization code: CHIMERA-7-4-9.”

The expedition entry describes distanceTicks 18, danger 6, encounter chance 0.17, and stamina cost 2.9, with a military-depot loot table that includes faraday_pack, military_radio, night_vision_scope, and ammo_308. These records do not prove the location is currently reachable, discovered, safe, stocked, or connected to a particular active quest. Loot categories are data references, not narrative guarantees.

## 4. The core distinction

There are at least four different claims in the phrase cluster:
1. A record says “Full Stock.”
2. A map fragment preserves part of an elevator-related sequence.
3. A manifest prints an authorization string.
4. The current state of the place and its contents is not established by those texts alone.

The expansion keeps each claim with its own source. It never turns the full authorization string into the missing elevator digits. The plan does not say whether the two artifacts were written at the same time, by the same office, for the same audience, or for the same physical control.

The story does not retroactively declare the manifest false. It allows that the manifest may have been accurate in its original context. What it refuses is the unsupported jump from “Full Stock” in a historical record to “full stock now.”

## 5. Why this belongs in a survival story

A list of supplies is never just a list when people are cold, tired, and deciding who will travel. A phrase like “Full Stock” can become a promise repeated around a camp, then a reason someone spends strength on a dangerous route, then a source of anger when the promise fails to materialize.

The story keeps this pressure close to the characters. No resource meter changes because a character argues. The player’s decision changes how the group talks about the document and what wording they leave for the next reader. That record can shape trust without pretending to calculate trust.

A survival setting gives every noun a cost. “Inventory” asks who counted. “Available” asks to whom. “Reserved” asks for whose return. “Full” asks how much of a shelf, a catalog, a quota, or a promise the speaker meant. The characters disagree because they have each paid for a different reading.

## 6. Tone and presentation

The voice is restrained and specific. There is no heroic assault on the armory, no military briefing montage, no celebration of hardware, and no catalogue of weapon specifications. The story stays among the people interpreting records, preparing for uncertainty, and explaining what a document can support.

Tension should come from silence after a practical question, from a line crossed out and rewritten, or from someone choosing not to claim what they cannot know. Keep sound cues small: paper, pencil, a pot moved off a hot surface, a latch in a camp building. Avoid triumphant scoring when the group reaches a more honest sentence.

Any physical depiction of the armory must use assets and scenes that already exist. This expansion does not ask for a new combat space, new tactical interaction, or special breach sequence.

## 7. Canon and authorship status

The supporting characters in this proposal are new authored candidates:
- Rell Varo, a former logistics copyist who handled manifests late in the old institution’s life.
- Anik Vale, a mechanic and salvage worker who has repaired equipment without reliable inventories.
- Dema Cor, an expedition organizer who must communicate uncertainty to a waiting community.
- Pell Sorn, an archive keeper responsible for keeping claims and sources adjacent.
- Evi Tarn, a younger runner who has heard “Full Stock” as a promise from people who never saw the document.

These names, biographies, and scene roles require the usual narrative and canon review. They are not claims about existing named characters. If name review finds collisions, replace the names without changing the source boundaries or scene functions.

No real-world military, country, institution, weapon manual, insignia, or copied text is introduced. The setting remains fictional and the artifacts are short, fictional records grounded in the local data.

## 8. Character: Rell Varo

Rell Varo remembers handling a copy of the manifest as an ordinary work document. He is not the author and does not claim to have counted the armory. He remembers the way the heading was repeated and the fatigue of copying figures from a page whose columns did not line up.

Rell is precise when describing what he saw and evasive about what he inferred. He is ashamed that, years later, he cannot remember who supplied the words “Full Stock.” He once used the phrase as if it settled an argument. The story gives him room to admit that without making him a liar.

His speech has a clerk’s habit of naming the field before answering: “That is the heading,” “That is what the copy says,” “That is not the date I can give you.” When he is tired, he abandons those distinctions and speaks plainly. The player can hear both modes without being asked to diagnose him.

## 9. Character: Anik Vale

Anik Vale has spent years fitting scarce parts into damaged machines. He is not a weapons specialist and does not give the player tactical advice. His expertise is in wear, fit, and the difference between an entry in a list and an object in someone’s hand.

He is impatient with paperwork when the paperwork is used to hurry people. He becomes unexpectedly careful around the phrase “full stock” because he has been asked to make empty containers sound like a plan.

Anik’s voice is practical:
> “A shelf can be full of the wrong thing.”
>
> “A count can be right and still not tell you what works.”
>
> “Do not write that I called it empty. I said I could not inspect it.”

His objections are about evidence and consequence, not a generic distrust of institutions.

## 10. Character: Dema Cor

Dema organizes expeditions and must speak to people who are waiting for a decision. She is not a quest-giver who can promise the player a destination. The data currently marks the relevant map node locked and undiscovered; the story does not override that state.

Dema wants words that can survive being repeated aloud. If she tells the group “Full Stock,” it will become “there is stock.” If she says “the record claims full stock,” people may hear evasion. The player must help her say something honest that is still useful.

Dema’s conflict is not resolved by making her stop planning. She continues to prepare for uncertainty, but the plan distinguishes preparation from guarantee.

## 11. Character: Pell Sorn

Pell keeps the group’s paper records. They believe an annotation should travel with the claim it qualifies, not be filed elsewhere where the next reader will never find it. Pell can be gentle and exacting in the same breath.

Pell’s recurring action is to leave room on the page. When someone proposes a confident sentence, Pell asks where the source ends. Their best line is:
> “If the note has to be separated from the claim for the claim to sound true, the note is doing the work.”

Pell should not become a lecturer. They are allowed to be wrong about a person’s motives, impatient when tired, and protective of a record because they fear what someone may do with it.

## 12. Character: Evi Tarn

Evi has heard the phrase “Full Stock” passed along as a promise. No source in this plan identifies who first told her, and she does not know whether the phrase came from the manifest or from another survivor’s account.

Her family or personal history is intentionally not specified. Evi matters because she is the listener who inherited a sentence after its source was lost. The player should not be given a hidden biography to unlock.

Evi asks:
> “If the paper never reached us, why did everyone know the words?”
>
> “A sentence can travel farther than the thing it describes,” Pell says.
>
> “Then what did it promise?”
>
> “That depends on who repeated it.”

Her question returns in several scenes with different emotional force.

## 13. Relationship map

Rell and Anik disagree over the value of a record. Rell fears that erasing the heading will erase the work people did to keep counts. Anik fears that preserving the heading without qualification will turn a historical line into a current guarantee.

Dema and Evi disagree over communication. Evi wants the group to speak plainly about what it hopes to find. Dema worries that hope can be heard as certainty when people are preparing for a hard trip.

Pell works between them but is not neutral about the wording. They believe the record should retain the exact phrase and the new annotation should sit directly beneath it.

The player is never asked to choose which person is right. The choices select the archive’s emphasis and the language Dema uses with the waiting group.

## 14. Narrative arc

**Act I: The phrase arrives.** The player hears “Full Stock” in an ordinary conversation before seeing its source. People have already attached different meanings to it.

**Act II: The source is read aloud.** The manifest and fragments are separated. The group discovers that the wording feels more complete than the evidence actually is.

**Act III: The words are carried.** The player helps each person decide what they can say to others without making the record carry more than it can.

**Act IV: The annotation remains.** The phrase is preserved with attribution and an explicit limit. The group acts under uncertainty, not because the uncertainty is solved.

The arc ends in a camp discussion, not an armory assault. The map state and all existing access conditions remain authoritative.

## 15. Opening scene: a promise overheard

The player enters a routine discussion already in progress. Evi is asking Dema whether the armory is stocked. Dema says only that there is a record. Evi replies that she has heard “Full Stock” all her life.

Anik turns a small repaired casing in his hands and says:
> “Did you hear it from a person who counted, or from a person who heard it?”
>
> “I don't know.”
>
> “That is a real answer.”
>
> “It isn't a useful one.”
>
> “It is useful if someone is about to pack for a promise.”

Rell arrives carrying a sleeve of copied papers. He recognizes the phrase, but does not claim the manifest is in the sleeve until Pell checks its label. This prevents the opening from quietly treating rumor and source as the same thing.

## 16. Opening choice: what does the player ask?

The player may ask Evi:
- “Who told you?”
- “What did you think it meant?”
- “What do you need from the group now?”

Each answer reveals a different angle, not a new factual source. Evi cannot identify the first speaker. She thought the words meant the place had enough to make the danger worthwhile. Now she wants to know whether Dema is planning around a document or around a story.

If asked what she needs, she says:
> “A sentence I can repeat without having to apologize later.”

That request becomes the practical purpose of the player’s archive work.

## 17. Rell checks the paper sleeve

Pell asks Rell not to summarize the document from memory. Rell reads its label and recognizes the distribution mark, but does not give a date or explain who assembled the copy.

> “You used to carry this?”
>
> “I carried papers like it.”
>
> “This one?”
>
> “I believe I carried this one. I cannot sign that from the label alone.”
>
> “Would you rather we call it yours?”
>
> “I would rather we call it the record in front of us.”

Rell’s caution should feel earned, not artificial. He has seen how a confident attribution can outlive its source. The scene establishes why his testimony will be limited later.

## 18. The manifest is introduced

Pell opens the sleeve and reads the artifact exactly:

> ARMORY 7 — FULL STOCK.
> EMP-HARDENED EQUIPMENT.
> AUTHORIZATION CODE: CHIMERA-7-4-9.

The text is the locally verified string. Formatting in the game may follow the existing content conventions, but spelling, punctuation, and order must be checked against the source JSON when implementation begins.

Pell reads the heading twice. The second time, Evi interrupts:
> “That sounds like a promise.”
>
> “It is written as a heading,” Pell answers.
>
> “Those can be the same thing.”
>
> “They can. We have to find out which job this sentence was doing.”

No new explanatory phrase is added to the manifest itself.

## 19. First annotation choice

The player can suggest one of three margins:
- “Manifest wording: Full Stock.”
- “Historical claim; present contents unverified.”
- “A claim is not a count.”

Pell explains what each keeps visible. The first preserves the exact phrase with no interpretation. The second separates past from present. The third is memorable but does not name the source.

The preferred archive treatment combines the first two:
> Source wording: “Full Stock.”
> Current contents are not established by this manifest.

The player may prefer the third as a spoken line, but it should not replace attribution in the archive. This is a content choice, not a new record-authoring interface.

## 20. Why the exact phrase stays

Anik proposes crossing out “Full Stock.” Rell objects:
> “Then the next reader will think we never saw it.”
>
> “The next reader could still see what we wrote under it.”
>
> “Not if somebody copies only the heading.”
>
> “They already did.”
>
> “That is why it has to stay where the line came from.”

The disagreement is about copying practices and trust, not a philosophical exercise. Rell accepts that the archive must add a limit. Anik accepts that removing the phrase would hide a fact about what the document says.

The plan’s settled outcome retains the source phrase verbatim and places the annotation beside it.

## 21. The phrase reaches the camp

Evi says she first heard “Full Stock” in a conversation where no one brought a copy of the manifest. She does not know the speaker or date. She remembers adults using the phrase to end an argument about whether someone should go looking.

The player can ask whether that conversation made her feel safe. Evi says:
> “It made it feel like somebody had checked.”
>
> “Had they?”
>
> “I don't know. That's what I am trying to ask now.”

The exchange reframes the phrase as a social object. It cannot establish who repeated it, but it shows why the words acquired weight.

## 22. Rell's copyist memory

Rell describes a table under a lamp, stacks of forms, and people waiting for corrections. He remembers retyping headings when a page was damaged. He does not remember entering the specific phrase “Full Stock.”

> “You remember the task.”
>
> “I remember the pressure.”
>
> “Do you remember the count?”
>
> “No.”
>
> “Did you see the shelves?”
>
> “No.”
>
> “Then what can you tell us?”
>
> “That the words were important enough to copy.”

Rell’s memory is valid but narrow. It supports a history of circulation, not the accuracy of the stock claim.

## 23. Anik's missing-part story

Anik describes a different kind of inventory. He once spent an afternoon searching a workshop for a spring listed as available. The cabinet was empty. The ledger was not wrong in the narrow sense: someone had signed the spring out, but the entry remained under the original bin.

He pauses before giving the year or the person’s name and decides neither is necessary to this story.

> “I found the space where the part belonged. I didn't find the part.”
>
> “And the ledger?”
>
> “The ledger told me the next question. It did not answer it.”
>
> “What was the next question?”
>
> “Who was supposed to put the borrowed things back?”

This is not evidence about Armory 7. It is Anik’s lived comparison, clearly kept separate from the armory manifest.

## 24. Dema's practical problem

Dema has to tell the group what to prepare for. If she repeats “Full Stock,” someone will expect supplies. If she says “we have no idea,” people may hear that there is no reason to plan. Her question is not whether the manifest is trustworthy in the abstract; it is how to communicate responsibly while keeping options open.

The player may advise:
- Quote the record and state its limits.
- Prepare for no recovery.
- Keep the manifest in the planning file but do not promise its contents.

The story does not adjudicate route selection or resource expenditure. Dema’s line at the end should be accurate under every choice:
> “We have a record that says ‘Full Stock.’ We do not have a present count.”

## 25. Scene: the sheet is held to the light

The artifact is old in the fictional world, but the source does not describe stains, a stamp, paper color, or physical damage. The scene therefore focuses on the act of reading rather than newly invented patina.

Pell holds the page steady while Rell reads it. The player can ask them to read the authorization line aloud. Rell does so once and then asks that the string not be written on a new access card.

> “It is already printed here.”
>
> “Then keep it here, with the source.”
>
> “You think someone will try it?”
>
> “I think a copied number starts to look like an instruction.”

This is a boundary conversation, not evidence that any control exists or that the code works.

## 26. The elevator fragment is opened

A separate fragment is laid next to the manifest. It says that the exterior radar-station building is condemned and its substructure remains active. A second fragment has digits 7, 4, and 9, with the rest water damaged.

Pell reads both cards separately. The player may ask whether 7-4-9 matches the ending of CHIMERA-7-4-9. Pell answers:
> “The marks resemble each other.”
>
> “That seems meaningful.”
>
> “It may be. This page does not tell us that they belong together.”

The player can write “possible overlap, unverified” in the margin. The plan never fuses them into one code or claims they operate the same elevator.

## 27. A number is not a bridge

Rell says that people often assume a number connects two pages because it is visible on both. He remembers numbering forms by hand and seeing the same digit used for a shelf, a shift, and a copy series.

He cannot offer an example from the armory. Pell stops him before the story drifts into a claim:
> “That tells us numbers repeat. It does not tell us what these numbers do.”
>
> “Exactly.”
>
> “You sound annoyed.”
>
> “I am annoyed by how useful a guess feels when you need it to be true.”

The scene makes uncertainty emotionally legible without pretending that similarity is proof.

## 28. Evi asks whether a blank means failure

Evi asks if the group is giving up by refusing to connect the digits. Dema answers that they are keeping separate questions separate.

> “If the digits matter, we still have them.”
>
> “And if they don't?”
>
> “Then we haven't lost anything by not calling them a door.”
>
> “It feels like waiting.”
>
> “It is waiting. Waiting can be a choice if you say why.”

The player may agree or object. Evi listens either way, but the archive wording remains sourced. Her emotional response can vary; the data and location state do not.

## 29. Artifact handling scene

The story specifies three paper objects: the supply manifest, the radar exterior fragment, and the elevator-access fragment. They should appear as distinct records in text or visual delivery if the existing narrative system supports this. Do not create fake photographs or detailed archival textures solely to make them appear connected.

Each object receives an attribution line in the archive summary:
- Source artifact: supply manifest.
- Source artifact: radar-station exterior fragment.
- Source artifact: elevator-access fragment.

The provenance may remain “source attribution as cataloged,” if no fuller origin exists. There is no implication that the group personally recovered all three at the armory.

## 30. Pell's source card

Pell drafts a short card to keep beside the manifest:

> SOURCE CARD — ARMORY 7
>
> Manifest wording: “Full Stock.”
> Manifest wording also references EMP-hardened equipment and prints CHIMERA-7-4-9 as an authorization code.
> Current stock, current authorization, and present access are not established by this text.
> A separate elevator fragment preserves 7, 4, 9; its relation to the manifest is unverified.

Pell asks the player whether to use “unverified” or “not established.” “Unverified” can sound like someone has checked and failed to confirm. “Not established” says only what the group knows. The plan’s preferred wording is “not established,” while the dialogue can use “unverified” when a character is speaking informally.

## 31. Alternative source-card wording

If the existing journal has a short character limit, use:

> The Armory 7 manifest says “Full Stock.” It does not establish current contents. A separate elevator fragment shows 7, 4, 9 with remaining digits water damaged. Its relation to the printed CHIMERA-7-4-9 authorization string is not established.

This text contains the essential boundaries in one paragraph. It must not be shortened to “Code partly recovered” or “Armory stocked,” both of which add a claim the sources do not support.

## 32. Scene: Rell signs only what he knows

Pell asks Rell to sign the copyist note. Rell reads:
> “I handled a copy of a document bearing this heading.”

He crosses out “I handled this manifest.” He explains that his memory supports the class of document but cannot certify that the page in front of them is the same copy.

The player can encourage him to sign the narrower line. If they do, he does so. If they ask for the stronger wording, he refuses without anger:
> “You can think I am being difficult. I would rather be difficult than useful in the wrong way.”

The signed note is a proposed narrative artifact and should be implemented only if the scene’s authored character biography is approved.

## 33. Scene: Dema drafts a message

Dema writes three versions for the camp notice. The first says:
> Armory 7: full stock of EMP-hardened supplies.

Pell circles “full stock of.” Dema crosses the phrase out, annoyed at herself more than at Pell.

The second says:
> A manifest for Armory 7 describes full stock and EMP-hardened equipment. Present contents are unknown.

Evi asks why Dema kept the phrase if she does not trust it. Dema says the group needs to know what the record claims, because that claim is part of the reason they are discussing the place at all.

The third version, the one she posts, reads:
> The existing manifest claims “Full Stock” and names EMP-hardened equipment. It does not give us a current count.

This is the canonical outward-facing wording.

## 34. Scene: what should the group prepare for?

Anik says that any planning discussion should include the possibility that the record is old or incomplete. Dema says they already prepare for incomplete information. Evi asks whether this is just a formal way of saying “expect nothing.”

Dema answers:
> “No. It means we do not spend tomorrow's certainty today.”
>
> “That sounds like a slogan.”
>
> “Then cross it out.”
>
> “I don't want to.”
>
> “Neither do I. But slogans can be repeated without their limits.”

The player may ask Dema to rewrite it. She eventually chooses a plain sentence about uncertainty and preparation. No gameplay prep or supplies are granted.

## 35. End of Act I

By the end of the opening movement, the player has heard “Full Stock” from a person and from a manifest. The two uses are no longer interchangeable. The player has also learned that the partial elevator digits and the printed authorization string are separate artifacts.

The unresolved questions now have faces: Evi needs language she can repeat; Rell fears being used as proof he cannot provide; Anik fears a heading becoming a promise; Dema must communicate without making a guarantee; Pell wants the record and its limits kept together.

The story turns next to the meaning of a stock claim: whose count, at what time, against which list, and for whom. The armory itself stays offscreen unless an existing route and scene authority already support it.


## 36. Act II: define the noun before arguing about the adjective

Pell asks what “stock” means before anyone decides whether it was full. The group offers several possibilities: equipment assigned to a site, items physically present, items recorded as present, equipment reserved for later issue, or a list of categories approved for storage.

No source in the current data defines the term for Armory 7. The conversation must not select a meaning and present it as canonical. Its purpose is to show why the same heading can sound clear to several listeners while leaving the central question open.

Anik says:
> “If it means on paper, then we have stock.”
>
> “If it means usable?”
>
> “We have a word.”
>
> “That is not an answer.”
>
> “No. It is the problem.”

The player may ask Pell to record the competing meanings without choosing among them.

## 37. Scene: a list with no count

Evi expects the manifest to contain quantities. Pell turns the page so she can see the line that names equipment and the printed authorization text. There is no count in the quoted fragment available to this plan.

Evi asks whether the rest of the page is missing. Rell says he cannot tell from the fragment alone. Pell writes:
> Quantities: not present in the reviewed text.

This wording is carefully narrow. The story must not assert that the entire manifest has no quantities, only that the locally verified fragment does not provide them. If a new source supplies more rows, the line may be revised with provenance. Until then, the narrative uses the reviewed text as-is.

## 38. Scene: Evi wants the whole sheet

Evi asks whether a wider copy might exist. Dema says the group can keep the question open without pretending it has an answer. Rell is uncomfortable. He has seen people treat a missing page as proof that the missing words would support their favorite theory.

> “If the rest exists, I want it found.”
>
> “So do I,” Pell says.
>
> “Then write that.”
>
> “I will write that we have not reviewed it.”
>
> “That sounds like waiting again.”
>
> “It is a different kind of waiting. One asks a question. The other invents the reply.”

The player can suggest a line about searching for an unreviewed copy. It remains an archive follow-up, not a quest flag or evidence of a physical page in the world.

## 39. A proposed work note, clearly authored

The following is a proposed diegetic note, not a recovered artifact or existing local data:

> READING NOTE — ARMORY 7 MANIFEST
>
> The reviewed line uses the heading “Full Stock.”
> No quantity is included in the quoted portion.
> The phrase does not state whether items were present, serviceable, assigned, or reserved.
> The record's date and compiler are not established in this copy.
> Do not turn the heading into a current stock report.

Its function is to summarize the archive group’s reading. It may appear in the existing journal if approved; it must not be given an old date or a false author signature.

## 40. Scene: Rell remembers a column

Rell remembers forms where a stock label sat beside columns for quantity, condition, and destination. He does not remember this manifest’s layout. The player can ask which column “Full Stock” occupied.

Rell answers:
> “I cannot tell you.”
>
> “Could it have been the quantity column?”
>
> “It could have been a heading above it.”
>
> “And if it was?”
>
> “Then we would still need the entries underneath.”
>
> “Do you think there were entries?”
>
> “I think forms usually have more than one line. I am not going to make that a memory.”

The dialogue shows knowledge gained from a former job without converting a general work habit into evidence about this artifact.

## 41. Scene: Anik's definition of ready

Anik argues that “stock” can hide three different conditions: an object exists, an object can be used, and an object can be used by this group. He emphasizes that those conditions are not interchangeable.

> “A radio can be present and dead.”
>
> “The line says equipment.”
>
> “The line says a category. It does not say a battery, a working connector, or a person who can set it up.”
>
> “You cannot know what it says without opening the place.”
>
> “I did not say I knew. I said the sentence doesn't.”

The line is not an item-quality feature proposal. No condition system, degradation model, or equipment inspection interaction is added.

## 42. Evi pushes back on Anik

Evi asks if he would ever let a useful statement count as useful. Anik says he would, if he knew what job the sentence could do.

> “It tells us the place was meant to hold something.”
>
> “Does it?”
>
> “The phrase says stock.”
>
> “That is fair. It tells us the writer wanted a reader to understand that there was stock.”
>
> “And that is useful.”
>
> “It is useful. I did not say it was enough.”

This gives Anik an important concession. He is not a universal skeptic; he recognizes the record’s evidentiary value while limiting the inference.

## 43. Scene: two readers, one line

Pell asks each person to paraphrase “Full Stock” without looking at the others’ wording. Evi writes “enough to make the trip matter.” Dema writes “a supply status, date unknown.” Anik writes “a claim without a count in the excerpt.” Rell writes only the exact heading.

The player may read the cards aloud. The difference surprises the group: the same words carry need, procedure, skepticism, and memory. Pell arranges the cards beside, not on top of, the manifest.

> “The phrase did not change,” Evi says.
>
> “We changed the distance from ourselves,” Pell answers.
>
> “That sounds like you have a better phrase.”
>
> “No. It sounds like I have a better reason to keep the old one.”

This scene is the story’s interpretive center.

## 44. Optional player wording

When asked what the phrase means, the player may select:
- “A recorded status.”
- “A promise someone carried forward.”
- “A claim without a present count.”
