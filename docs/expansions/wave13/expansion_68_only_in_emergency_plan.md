# EXPANSION 68 — ONLY IN EMERGENCY
## An Underground Fuel Depot Story Plan
### Wave 13: The Wording in the Margin

**Primary location:** Underground Fuel Depot, Industrial District  
**Location ID:** underground_fuel_depot  
**Document type:** prose-first game-content expansion proposal  
**Target:** at least 120,000 characters of authored story and game content  
**Story question:** When a reserve is marked “emergency only,” who gets to say the emergency has begun?  
**Status:** content proposal; no production-system approval

---

## 1. Player-facing premise

The industrial map fragments lead toward a pre-war emergency fuel reserve sealed beneath a collapsed factory. The Northern Sector sheet shows the district north of the river, but burn damage has removed the eastern edge. A sewer map circles junction C-7. A key card carries an access code on one side and a short instruction on the other: “DEPOT — EMERGENCY ONLY.”

The player is asked to bring the recovered papers to a present-day route keeper, who wants a note that will prevent the card from being mistaken for proof of a usable fuel supply. The depot’s location description says heavy steel manholes seal underground tanks, their valve wheels are frozen, and lingering hydrocarbon vapors make open flame dangerous. Its expedition data lists diesel fuel and mechanical parts as categories. None of those records proves how much usable fuel remains.

Former depot clerk Rava Deyn remembers that “emergency only” was printed on every reserve key, not just this one. A pump mechanic, Olan Vey, remembers one night when the phrase was used to keep a generator running through a storm. Neither person can tell the player whether the fuel card’s code still works or whether anyone ever opened these particular tanks.

The story is not a fuel acquisition promise. It is an account of a label whose purpose outlived the office that used it. The player’s work is to preserve the map trail, report the condition the current location record actually gives, and leave the reserve’s unknown quantity unknown.

## 2. Short pitch

A card has traveled farther than its tank. It appears first in a family’s envelope of trade papers, later in the Industrial District map sleeve, and finally on a table at the Holdfast route room. The card’s printed heading is clean; the handwritten warning on its reverse is older and more useful.

The code looks precise. The words beside it are not. “Emergency only” could have been an access restriction, a stock category, a warning against routine use, or all three in different offices. People remember how their own crews used the phrase. No one remembers the whole depot.

The player follows three sources that do not quite overlap: a damaged regional map, a sewer grid with a circled junction, and the fuel key. At the end, the player can prepare a concise route note, a fuller archive summary, or no field annotation. Every option leaves the same factual boundary: a marked reserve is not a measured reserve.

The emotional center is Rava’s old habit of turning the key card face down when a shift ended. She says the code was for a person who had been assigned the task, not a person who had discovered the card. Olan remembers a night when he wanted the fuel and someone asked whether the emergency was real. Both remember a decision they made under pressure. Neither gets to make the current player’s decision.

## 4. Narrative scope

This proposal supplies authored material for a location visit and its record trail:
- an expedition request framed around recovering a map packet and returning a clear description;
- arrival and inspection prose for the industrial map fragments;
- a present-day route-room scene with a clerk and a mechanic;
- in-world key-card text, shift notes, a reserve form, and a copied handover;
- dialogue that distinguishes work authorization, inventory, and present need;
- one player choice about how much of the source history to preserve;

- closing dialogue that leaves the current fuel crisis to its existing owner.

It does not provide new fuel mechanics. It does not introduce a valve-operation mini-game, fuel quality score, vapor meter, fire risk simulation, tank-pumping system, stock reservation authority, or a new expedition yield. The story does not tell the player to light, strike, heat, drill, or open anything.

## 5. Non-goals and hard boundaries

The plan does not:
- declare that any tank is full, empty, sealed, safe, or reachable beyond what current records say;
- convert the item card into a functioning key without a current content and host contract;
- add a new fuel stockpile, ledger, fuel type, recipe, or reserve authority;
- alter the Holdfast fuel crisis, generator consumption, inventory, or trade owners;
- add contamination, explosion, inhalation, or fire gameplay;
- create a new route through the sewer grid;
- change map discoverability, expedition danger, or travel values;
- decide who is morally entitled to a reserve in the present day;
- turn the old shift workers into a new faction;
- make a current survivor responsible for operating the depot.

All potentially hazardous physical action remains under existing location and world authorities. The story can describe a warning printed on paper, but cannot turn that warning into a new simulation.

## 6. Theme: a rule can outlive its reader

“Emergency only” is a compact phrase written for a workplace whose readers shared a schedule, a chain of custody, and a definition of emergency. Once the workplace is gone, the words still fit on the card but no longer carry the surrounding agreement.

Rava used the phrase to close a shift: the key came back, the card turned face down, and the next worker would check the ledger. Olan used it in an argument about whether to keep a generator running. The route keeper hears it as a current promise that fuel is waiting. Each use makes sense in its own room. None establishes what the depot can provide now.

The story does not claim that the old rule was good or bad. It lets the player see the people who relied on the rule and the person who had to interpret it. The present-day route note should stop one specific mistake: treating a historical label as an inventory report.

## 7. Main cast

### 7.1 Rava Deyn — former reserve clerk

Rava checked keys in and out at an industrial supply desk. She did not work underground and never inspected the tanks. Her ledger covered task assignments, return signatures, and missing cards. She remembers “emergency only” as a standard marking on reserve items, used to distinguish stores that were not part of routine maintenance.

Rava’s flaw is trusting a phrase to carry its whole context. Her old coworkers knew what counted as an emergency because they shared a supervisor, shift calendar, and an incident report. She never expected the card to circulate as a standalone instruction decades later. She wants the player to preserve the exact phrase and identify it as handwriting on the reverse.

Rava does not claim to know who wrote the words, when they were added, or whether they applied to a single tank or the reserve program. Her role is to explain how her office used similar labels, not to certify this card.

### 7.2 Olan Vey — former pump mechanic

Olan kept pumps and transfer lines running at a different facility in the district. He remembers one storm shift when the main line lost pressure and a supervisor authorized reserve fuel for a portable generator. He did not visit this depot and does not recognize the key card.

Olan’s memory is about a decision, not proof. He remembers that the supervisor said the storm was an emergency because the clinic’s cold room was losing power. He also remembers that the fuel came from an above-ground maintenance tank, not a buried reserve. His story explains why someone might defend a flexible reading of the phrase while making clear that it does not establish a precedent for this site.

### 7.3 Tessa Noll — present-day route keeper

Tessa updates the existing expedition board. She has heard the depot might be a reliable fuel source and wants to prevent a misleading note from spreading. She is not a quartermaster and does not allocate the shelter’s fuel.

Tessa asks for language that fits a field card: the location is mapped, the sources describe a reserve, and the condition of its contents is unknown. She wants to be useful without turning the card into an authorization.

### 7.4 Jeren Pell — archive runner

Jeren carries copies between the route room and the archive shelf. They can identify the paper stock on the sewer map as a utility-office copy, but cannot tell when it was printed. Their practical skill is keeping the fragments together and returning originals to the sleeve.

Jeren’s question is whether a copied code should be filed beside the card or on the map. They believe it should be copied with its source, so the number never floats free of its label. Jeren does not decipher the code or operate it.

### 7.5 Sava Orrel — junior mechanic

Sava has learned to repair hand pumps but has never worked with petroleum tanks. She sees the “valve wheels are frozen” description and asks if that means the site cannot yield fuel. Tessa explains that the current data describes the wheels, not the state of every tank or the capacity of every pipe.

Sava is curious but not reckless. She is present to give the player a reason to say what the record does and does not establish. She is not assigned to a new apprenticeship or fuel recovery feature.

Her request is a narrative errand, not a new system contract. The implementation must attach it to an existing quest, journal, or expedition hook only after its current access path is audited. The plan does not require a fresh dynamic quest template or a new reward.

## 12. Reading the access code

The code on the Fuel Reserve Key is presented exactly as the map-fragment owner currently provides it. No character reads digits aloud in a way that turns the code into a password minigame. The player may inspect the code field and the handwritten phrase independently.

Rava explains:

> “An access code says a form expected a code. It does not tell me who could use the door. We issued cards to named workers and took them back at shift end. That was the practice at my desk.”

The player can ask whether the code belongs to this particular depot. Rava answers:

> “The card says depot. I cannot certify the address from the back of a card.”

If asked whether the code still works:

> “I do not know. It was written for equipment and a system I never saw.”

The access number remains a source detail. This plan does not introduce a keypad interface, unlock condition, or claim that the fragment itself grants access to the hidden installation.

## 13. Dialogue: “emergency only”

The conversation with Rava can use a compact menu.

**Player:** “What did emergency only mean?”

**Rava:** “It meant this stock was not part of the ordinary draw.”

**Player:** “Who decided it was an emergency?”

**Rava:** “The supervisor on the form. If there was a fire, a line failure, or a clinic loss, the request said so.”

**Player:** “Could a worker take it without a form?”

**Rava:** “A worker could do anything. The form told the next shift what had happened.”

**Player:** “Was the label a rule or a warning?”

**Rava:** “At my desk, it was a category. On this card, I cannot tell.”

If the player asks whether a category can also be a rule, Rava says:

> “Of course. Paper can carry two jobs. The trouble begins when the second reader only sees one.”

The dialogue is not a verdict on reserve policy. Rava remembers one office’s routine. The player can preserve her statement as testimony rather than generalizing it to every depot.

## 14. Dialogue: the mechanic’s storm

Olan tells the storm-generator story only if the player asks why the word “emergency” mattered to him.

> “The clinic freezer had medicine inside. The line failed after the second warning. We had a portable set on the west pad and one tank for it. The supervisor wrote ‘emergency draw’ before anyone turned the key.”

**Player:** “Did you get in trouble?”

**Olan:** “No. The work was authorized.”

**Player:** “Then what bothers you?”

**Olan:** “At the next meeting they called it proof that every reserve should open whenever the word ‘emergency’ was spoken. That was not what happened. We had a failing line, a named room, a person who signed, and enough fuel for one set.”

If asked if the depot could serve the clinic now, Olan says:

> “I did not work that depot. I am telling you what one form looked like.”

His story should not imply that medicine remains at a clinic or that the player can reproduce the event. It concerns how a specific decision was documented.

## 25. Olan’s second memory

Olan remembers a different day from the storm shift. A foreman asked the crew to reserve fuel for a transport that might arrive. The vehicle did not come. At shift end, the fuel remained in the drum, but the decision had consumed the whole conversation.

**Player:** “Was that an emergency?”

**Olan:** “The foreman thought it might become one.”

**Player:** “Did you open the reserve?”

**Olan:** “No.”

**Player:** “Why do you remember it?”

He says that a reserve can shape a day even when it is untouched. He does not say that the foreman was wrong to hold it. The player can record the anecdote as “Olan remembers a deferred draw at another site,” not as history of the Underground Fuel Depot.

The scene should resist turning an absence of use into evidence that a reserve was never needed. It is one recollection from one workplace.

## 26. Rava’s ledger practice

This is a personal account of paperwork, not a recommendation to add a new inventory authority to the game.
