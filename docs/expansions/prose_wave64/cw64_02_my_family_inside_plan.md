# EXPANSION CW64-02 — My Family Inside

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 64: Children’s Drawn Worlds

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).

## 1. Expansion thesis

My Family is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age six and bunker-born, draws four stick figures labelled MAMA, PAPA, ME, and BABY inside a rectangle representing the bunker. The baby is a smiling circle. Above the bunker are no figures outside. The child says, We are inside. It is safe inside. The Teacher says the child has never drawn the surface. This plan expands the image as belonging, protection, and an absence that should not be forced into a lesson.

## 2. Story question

When a six-year-old draws four labelled figures inside a bunker rectangle and leaves all space above it empty, what does safety look like when outside has never entered the page?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the family’s names, current household, the child’s age at later readings, the Teacher’s interpretation beyond the record, or what safety means to the child. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, family, bunker, schoolroom, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "Four stick figures inside a rectangle (the bunker). The figures are labelled in wobbly letters: MAMA, PAPA, ME, BABY. The baby is drawn as a circle with a smile. Above the bunker, there are no stick figures outside. The child says: We are inside. It is safe inside. The Teacher says the child has never drawn the surface." The rectangle in this drawing carries several kinds of shelter at once. It is a shape on paper, the outline of a bunker, and the child’s chosen answer to where a family belongs. The four labels do not need polished lettering to hold their force. MAMA and PAPA are roles named by a child; ME is the place from which the picture is seen; BABY is a smiling circle whose simplicity makes care visible without explaining it. The blank space above the rectangle must be treated with patience. It records that the child has never drawn the surface, but it does not tell a reader whether the surface is feared, desired, irrelevant, or merely unavailable to the child’s visual vocabulary. A scene can let an adult notice the blank and stop themselves from filling it. A conversation can let another child ask whether there are families outside and receive an answer that remains uncertain. The artwork can be read beside a shelter notice or a household record only if those existing materials remain separate from the child’s page. Nothing in the drawing establishes who currently sleeps together, who chose the labels, or what the smiling baby knows. The safety sentence belongs to the child and should keep its warmth. It is not a universal guarantee and does not need an adult correction. A later return may show the page pinned lower so younger children can see it, or folded carefully because someone wants to keep the labels from fading. The meaningful change is attention, not a new household mechanic. The child has drawn an interior that is enough for the moment. Let the prose honor that enoughness while leaving the unpictured world open. A quiet follow-up can let the child point to the rectangle and say that the drawing is still true because it is where the family is pictured. The adult listener can answer by looking at the paper instead of enlarging the claim. This keeps safety intimate, attributed, and unfinished.
