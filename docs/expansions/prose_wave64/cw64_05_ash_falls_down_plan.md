# EXPANSION CW64-05 — Ash Falls Down

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 64: Children’s Drawn Worlds

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_006 — Ash Falls Down.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.
**Scope:** game content only; no production code, JSON data, route, quest, flag, simulation, or save changes.

## 1. Expansion thesis

Ash Falls Down is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. It describes a sheet covered entirely in grey finger-paint strokes, with no distinguishable shapes, and records the child’s answer: Ash. When the Teacher asks what else is in the picture, the child says, Nothing. Just ash. The Teacher pins it beside the blue sky drawing, and the record calls the contrast devastating. This plan expands the artwork as refusal, witness, and the ethics of not adding a hopeful shape to a child’s page.

## 2. Story question

When an entire sheet becomes grey finger-paint with no shapes and a child answers every question with only ash, what does a teacher preserve when the picture refuses to become anything else?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the child’s name, age, the Teacher’s intention, the sky drawing’s exact identity, or whether the child’s answer changes later. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, teacher, ash, schoolroom, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "The entire paper is covered in grey finger-paint strokes. There are no distinguishable shapes — just layers of grey. The child says: Ash. The Teacher asked what else is in the picture. The child said: Nothing. Just ash. The Teacher pinned it next to the blue sky drawing. The contrast is devastating." The grey page asks the reader to resist the urge to rescue it with interpretation. Finger paint has covered the paper in layers, and the record says there are no distinguishable shapes. The child answers Ash and then Nothing, just ash when the Teacher asks for something else. Those words should be allowed to land without a second diagnosis. The Teacher’s action of pinning the page beside the blue sky drawing creates a relation, but it does not make the grey page incomplete. A scene may describe the wall, the neighboring colors, and the adult who looks from one page to the other. The child does not need to explain the contrast. A conversation can include a pause in which someone asks whether the picture contains anything besides ash and then realizes the question has already been answered. The word devastating belongs to the record’s framing voice, so a careful draft can keep that judgment outside the child’s mouth. The artwork does not establish an event, a weather condition, an exposure history, or a mental state. It establishes a material field and two concise answers. A later return may show the paint drying, a corner lifting, or another child sitting near the wall without speaking. None of those details should turn the page into a progress chart. If the blue sky drawing nearby invites comparison, let the two pages remain distinct forms of inherited outside. Blue is not required to defeat grey, and grey is not required to predict blue. The Teacher can preserve both because the classroom is large enough for incompatible images. The prose should keep its hands off the child’s interior life. The page is already an authored act. Its refusal to add a shape is part of what it says.
