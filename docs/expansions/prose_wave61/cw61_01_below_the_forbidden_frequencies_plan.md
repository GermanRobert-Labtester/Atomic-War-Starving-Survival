# EXPANSION CW61-01 — Below the Forbidden Frequencies

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 61: Radio, Labor, and What Returns

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** graffiti_radio_dial_night_watch — Below the Forbidden Frequencies.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.

## 1. Expansion thesis

Below the Forbidden Frequencies is a local graffiti posting triggered by friction_forbidden_radio_listening, available from day 28 with weight 0.9. Its lowercase voice addresses whoever keeps tuning past the mark, says the dial can be heard from two bunks over, compares the click to a tooth, and admits that some people count clicks to sleep now, which is either trust or the end of them. This plan treats the line as a night-watch record about sound, proximity, and exhausted interpretation without explaining the radio or prescribing listening behavior.

## 2. Story question

When a dial past a forbidden mark clicks like a tooth two bunks over, and people count those clicks to sleep, how does a bunker tell the difference between trust and the end of trust?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The posting record establishes the title, exact text, triggerWorldFlag, minDay, and weight. It does not establish the forbidden mark, the receiver owner, the two bunks, the number of clicks, or whether the people are trusting the dial or losing themselves to it. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing radio, night-watch, frequency, sleep, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local text: "whoever keeps tuning past the mark — we hear the dial from two bunks over. it clicks like a tooth. some of us count the clicks to sleep now, which is either trust or the end of us." The expanded reading keeps the forbidden mark deliberately undefined. It may be a line on a dial, a phrase repeated by tired people, or a boundary that exists only because someone once said it should. The posting gives us no calibrated threshold and no reliable account of what is beyond it. What it gives us is a room hearing a click through two bunks and trying to make that sound livable. The tooth comparison brings the machine close to the body without explaining either one. A person can trust the click because it is regular, or fear it because regularity has become the only thing left to trust. Counting can be a private rhythm, a shared joke, a way to wait, or the edge of an exhaustion the writer cannot name. The plan should let several people hold those interpretations without turning one into a diagnosis. The night watch context is social before it is procedural. Someone notices a light, a blanket shifting, a sentence scratched low on a wall, or a person who wakes before the sound arrives. None of those details should become instructions for listening or a new schedule. The receiver remains an existing object, and the source text remains the authority for the phrase trust or the end of us. A return passage can show the clicks stopping, continuing, or being heard by someone who was not part of the original count. The change is in audience and attention, not in radio state. A later reader may erase the line, leave it, or write one word beneath it, but the plan should not manufacture a new flag to justify that choice. The strongest ending leaves the room in the uncertain hour before dawn, with the dial still somewhere beyond an unnamed mark and the people deciding whether a repeated sound is company. Verify current radio ownership and source attribution before any placement. If the consumer cannot preserve the ambiguity, keep the text as a candidate bank entry rather than building a listening mechanic around it.

## 5. Human center

The emotional center is a sound that has become part of the room’s sleep. The dial is not frightening only because it clicks; it is frightening because people have begun to depend on the click enough to count it. Trust and exhaustion share the same sentence.
