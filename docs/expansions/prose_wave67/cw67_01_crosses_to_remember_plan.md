# EXPANSION CW67-01 — Crosses to Remember

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 67: Children’s Stories at the Threshold

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_019 — The Cemetery.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.
**Scope:** game content only; no production code, JSON data, route, quest, flag, simulation, or save changes.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Cemetery is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age seven, draws the eastern field cemetery with small crosses arranged in rows, crudely drawn flowers at their bases, and a grey ash sky. The child says the Undertaker takes them there sometimes, tells them about the people, and that they draw the crosses so they remember. The Undertaker says the child has a natural respect for the dead that most adults never develop. This plan expands the drawing as apprenticeship in remembrance, keeping the dead and the child distinct from any new memorial system.

## 2. Story question

When a seven-year-old draws the eastern field cemetery in rows of small crosses and says the Undertaker tells them about the people, what does remembering look like when the names are not on the page?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the names on the crosses, the number of graves, the Undertaker’s teaching style, the child’s family, or what the child remembers after drawing. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, cemetery, undertaker, memorial, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "A drawing of the eastern field cemetery. Small crosses are arranged in rows. Flowers (crudely drawn) are at the base of each cross. The sky is grey (ash). The child says: The Undertaker takes me there sometimes. He tells me about the people. I draw the crosses so I remember. The Undertaker says the child has a natural respect for the dead that most adults never develop." The fuller reading stays with the child’s act of drawing rather than turning the page into a record of the cemetery itself. The crosses are small, the flowers are crude, and the grey sky makes the page carry both care and ash. The Undertaker’s telling is an existing relationship, not permission to invent biographies for the dead. A candidate passage may show the child asking for one more story, the Undertaker declining to fill a silence, or the drawing being placed near a memorial notice without becoming a replacement for names. Respect can be learned through repetition and still remain personal. The source supports grief, attention, and continuity. It does not establish a memorial ledger, cemetery route, age-appropriate curriculum, or emotional diagnosis. Keep the dead as people whose lives exceed the crosses, keep the child’s flowers imperfect, and let remembering remain a practice rather than a score. The page can remain a small companion to the cemetery rather than a map of it. A child may remember the rows by shape before remembering any names, and the Undertaker can let that first memory be incomplete without calling it disrespect. The grey ash sky is part of the drawing’s weather, but it need not stand for every feeling in the room. The flowers are crude because the child is drawing to remember, not to decorate a finished monument. A candidate passage may follow the child home after the visit, where the crosses return as simple marks on the paper and one story returns as a sentence the child repeats. Another passage may let the Undertaker correct a detail in the drawing gently, then decide that correction is less important than the child’s reason for making it. If the artwork is placed beside an existing epitaph or cemetery record, the two can remain in different voices. The record can name; the drawing can hold the act of looking. A return may show the child drawing one flower again, not because the cemetery has changed but because memory has. The Undertaker’s praise should stay modest. Respect is not a trait to award and not a proof that the child understands death. It is a practice visible in the time the child gives to the page. Keep the crosses small, the people uncompleted, and the child’s remembering open to the next story.
