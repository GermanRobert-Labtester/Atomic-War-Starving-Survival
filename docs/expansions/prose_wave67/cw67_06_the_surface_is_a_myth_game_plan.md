# EXPANSION CW67-06 — The Surface Is a Myth Game

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 67: Children’s Stories at the Threshold

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** folklore_b2_the_surface_is_a_myth — The Surface Is a Myth.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Surface Is a Myth is a local children’s folklore game in bunker_children_folklore_batch_2.json. One child claims the surface is real, naming sky, wind, rain, and trees; the others argue it is an adult myth meant to keep children obedient. The believer may use only adult stories or pre-war books, while the sceptics answer that a huge sky, real trees, and rain should leave evidence in the bunker. The game always ends in silence, with the believer saying the Teacher says it is real and the sceptics replying that the Teacher says many things. No one has won. This plan expands the game as epistemic play, inherited testimony, and a silence that belongs to everyone.

## 2. Story question

When one child must argue that sky, wind, rain, and trees are real while the others answer with bunker logic and no one wins, what does a shared uncertainty become when it is played as debate?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the folklore ID and exact prose. It does not establish the children’s names, the books, the Teacher’s beliefs, the surface’s current condition, or whether the game is harmless for every participant. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing folklore, child, teacher, sky, greenhouse, book, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local prose: "A game where one child claims the surface is real — sky, wind, rain, trees — and the others must argue that it is a myth invented by the adults to keep children obedient. The 'believer' must describe the surface using only things they have heard from adults or read in pre-war books. The 'sceptics' counter with bunker reality: 'If the sky is so big, why haven't we seen it? If trees are real, why do we only have the greenhouse? If rain is water falling from above, why doesn't the ceiling leak?' The game always ends the same way: a long silence, and then the believer says, quietly, 'But the Teacher says it's real.' And the sceptics say, 'The Teacher says a lot of things.' No one has ever won." The fuller reading keeps the game from becoming a truth contest the prose must settle. The believer and sceptics both rely on inherited evidence, and both discover that absence of sight is not proof of absence. A candidate passage may show a child changing sides, a Teacher hearing the final exchange, or a greenhouse plant becoming a disputed substitute for a tree. The ending matters because silence is the only result no side can claim. The source supports curiosity, doubt, and the limits of testimony. It does not establish a surface survey, a child education mechanic, a truth meter, or a route to open air. Keep the rules attributed to the game, let the questions remain answerable in multiple voices, and protect the final silence as a shared human space. The debate can be written as a room full of children who know that both sides are using borrowed evidence. The believer has sky, wind, rain, and trees from books and adults. The sceptics have walls, a greenhouse, dry ceilings, and the absence of a view. Neither side needs to become cruel for the game to have stakes. A child can switch roles halfway through, discovering that the same sentence sounds different when spoken against the concrete. The Teacher’s phrase at the end matters because it is trusted and questioned at once. A return may show the Teacher entering after the silence and refusing to settle the debate, or may show a child bringing a new book whose picture changes one argument without proving the world. The greenhouse should remain an imperfect substitute rather than evidence for or against a surface. The game’s long silence is a form of shared knowledge: everyone has reached the edge of what they can demonstrate. No one wins because certainty is not the prize. The content should leave room for a player to respect belief, respect doubt, or remain quiet. Keep the surface unverified, the rules attributed, and the final line soft enough to make curiosity possible after the argument ends.

The emotional center is children discovering that inherited stories and lived absence can both be sincere. The game should not reward belief or scepticism. Its long silence gives every player a way to remain uncertain without surrendering curiosity or trust.
