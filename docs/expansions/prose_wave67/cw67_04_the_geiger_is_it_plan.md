## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Dosimeter Hide-and-Seek game is a local children’s folklore record in bunker_children_folklore_batch_2.json. Petr, age ten, invents a game in which one child is the Geiger, others hide, the Geiger calls click-click-click, and a pointed hiding place is declared Hot. The hidden child gives a made-up dosimeter reading; above fifty means contaminated and a slow walk to base, below fifty means clean and a run. No actual dosimeters are used, and the children memorized thresholds from a mess-hall poster. This plan expands the game as imitation, peer authority, and the risk of adult safety language becoming a rule-shaped toy, without endorsing its thresholds.

## 2. Story question

When children turn hide-and-seek into a game where one child is the Geiger and invented numbers decide whether a hidden player walks or runs, what happens when safety language becomes play?

The other children hide. The Geiger counts to twenty, then walks the corridors calling 'Click-click-click.' When the Geiger points at a hiding spot and says 'Hot!' the hidden child must come out and say their dosimeter reading (a number they make up). If the reading is above fifty, the child is 'contaminated' and must walk slowly back to base. If below fifty, they are 'clean' and can run. No actual dosimeters are used. The children have memorised the safety thresholds from the poster in the mess hall." The fuller reading keeps the game’s invented numbers visibly invented. The children borrow adult words because the bunker has made those words important, then use them to stage suspense between corridors. A candidate passage may show a caregiver interrupting the game, a child changing the rule, or Petr admitting that the number is made up even when everyone shouts Hot. The game can reveal how children rehearse danger without becoming an accurate safety lesson. The source does not authorize radiation guidance, contamination states, movement rules, or a child health mechanic. The poster remains an existing source of remembered language, not a value table to reproduce. Keep the play recognizable as play, keep the threshold fictional inside the game, and let the human consequence be a conversation about what words do when children inherit them. The game can be shown in a corridor with enough room for play while keeping its fictional boundary explicit in the prose. The children’s invented numbers matter because they imitate the authority of a poster, not because the numbers describe the air. A caregiver may hear the word contaminated and ask the children what they think it means; the answer can reveal that the game is built from sound, movement, and remembered adult seriousness. Petr may defend the fifty line because changing it would make the game less recognizable, while another child may make up a different number and start a dispute. That dispute is useful content because it exposes the game’s lack of an instrument. The Geiger role can be tiring, funny, or coveted, and the hidden children can choose to leave a hiding place before they are found. The source does not require a winner, a penalty, or a real reading. A return can show the poster being rewritten by adults while the children’s chant keeps the old words, making the gap between official language and play visible. Keep the word Hot theatrical, keep the numbers imaginary, and let an existing child or therapist consumer decide whether the game needs a boundary reminder. The game is about inherited fear becoming a rule-shaped toy, not about teaching radiation.
