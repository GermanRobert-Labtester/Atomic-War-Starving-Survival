# EXPANSION CW67-05 — The Rhyme at the Mess-Hall Door

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 67: Children’s Stories at the Threshold

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.

## 1. Expansion thesis

Counting Out Ash is a local children’s folklore record in bunker_children_folklore_batch_2.json. Children chant the rhyme while lining up for meals, using the final child pointed to on “again” to decide who goes first through the mess-hall door. Its lines name the hatch, latch, filter, dose, and falling ash. The record says the rhyme exactly matches the bunker’s emergency drill sequence, suggesting the children learned it by overhearing adult safety briefings and turned it into a game. This plan expands the rhyme as memory carried by rhythm, keeping the meal queue, drill sequence, and child play distinct.

## 2. Story question

When children use a meal-line rhyme to decide who goes first and its numbers echo an adult emergency drill, what does a game inherit when the rhythm remembers more than the players know?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the folklore ID and exact prose. It does not establish the drill’s current wording, the children’s ages, who supervises the line, whether going first is desirable, or whether the rhyme is still used after any briefing changes. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing folklore, child, mess hall, hatch, filter, dose, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local prose: "Chanted by children lining up for meals to decide who gets to go first through the mess hall door: 'One, two, buckle the hatch. / Three, four, check the latch. / Five, six, the filter's fixed. / Seven, eight, the dose is right. / Nine, ten, the ash falls again.' The last child pointed to on 'again' is the one who goes first. The rhyme has been observed to exactly match the bunker's emergency drill sequence, suggesting the children learned it from overhearing the adult safety briefings and turned it into a game." The fuller reading lets the rhyme carry a double life. In the meal line it is a way to distribute a small privilege or delay; in the adult world it echoes a sequence attached to emergency preparation. The children may not know which words came first. A candidate passage may show an adult hearing the rhyme and recognizing the briefing inside it, or a child changing one line while insisting the beat must remain. The source supports transmission, imitation, and the strange way safety language becomes ordinary. It does not establish drill instructions, filter maintenance, dose thresholds, or a queue mechanic. Keep the rhyme as cultural evidence, not a procedure. The last word points to a child for the game, while the adult sequence remains current only through its existing owner. Let the meal door be a place where memory and appetite meet without making play responsible for safety. The rhyme can stay in the meal line where hunger gives every turn a little weight. Children may not know that the sequence echoes a drill, but adults can hear the resemblance and feel the responsibility of having let safety language become a selection game. A scene may show a child pointing to the final again while another child accepts the result without treating it as a merit. The hatch, latch, filter, dose, and ash words can remain strange in the children’s mouths. Their rhythm is the cultural fact. A later return may let one line change after a new briefing, then show an older child defending the old version because it belongs to the line’s history. The queue should not become a contest with rewards, and the final child should not be marked as lucky or unlucky beyond going first through the door. If a staff member interrupts, the reason can be care rather than punishment: the adult remembers what the words originally meant and wants the distinction restored. The rhyme then becomes a small document of transmission. It proves that children listen, that they simplify, and that they make ordinary waiting bearable with borrowed danger. Keep the emergency sequence outside the plan’s authority and let the mess-hall doorway hold appetite, memory, and a little fairness together.
