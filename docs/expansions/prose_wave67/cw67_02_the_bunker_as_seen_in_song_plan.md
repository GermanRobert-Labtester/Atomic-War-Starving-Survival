# EXPANSION CW67-02 — The Bunker as Seen in Song

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 67: Children’s Stories at the Threshold

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** art_b2_020 — The Bunker Is My Body.
**Tone:** grounded, human, restrained, material, and careful about what a damaged record can prove.
**Scope:** game content only; no production code, JSON data, route, quest, flag, simulation, or save changes.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The Bunker Is My Body is an authored artwork record in childrens_artwork_batch_2.json. A resident child, age ten, makes an abstract drawing inspired by the hymn: bunker walls become skin in flesh-tone colour, ventilation ducts become blue veins, the generator becomes a red heart with pulse lines, and the hatch becomes an open mouth. The child says they drew what they see in their head when they sing it. The Therapist calls it a perfect visual representation of the bunker-as-body metaphor, and the page is pinned to the dormitory wall. This plan expands the image as communal metaphor, keeping the drawing distinct from literal anatomy or a new system model.

## 2. Story question

When a child draws bunker walls as skin, ducts as veins, the generator as a red heart, and the hatch as a mouth after singing a hymn, what does metaphor reveal when a whole shelter becomes one body?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The cited record establishes the artwork ID, artist label, title, medium, day, tags, and exact description. It does not establish the hymn’s complete text, the child’s name, the Therapist’s larger assessment, the dormitory response, or whether every resident shares the metaphor. The prose preserves those unknowns through existing artwork, child, hymn, therapist, bunker, dormitory, journal, or narrative consumers. Verbatim local record: "An abstract drawing inspired by the hymn. The bunker walls are drawn as skin (with a flesh-tone colour). The ventilation ducts are veins (blue). The generator is a heart (red, with pulse lines). The hatch is a mouth (open). The child says: We sing the song and I drew what I see in my head when I sing it. The Therapist says the drawing is a perfect visual representation of the bunker-as-body metaphor. It has been pinned to the dormitory wall." The fuller reading lets metaphor connect rooms without claiming the bunker is literally alive. Skin can mean boundary, veins can mean movement, a heart can mean dependence, and a mouth can mean opening or speech. The child’s song-born vision is a personal translation of shared language. The Therapist’s perfect representation is praise with a professional frame, not a diagnosis of the child or a universal key to the hymn. A candidate passage may show residents arguing over whether the generator is a heart or a stomach, then returning to ordinary work without resolving the image. A return can let the drawing fade at the dormitory wall while the hymn keeps changing in different mouths. The source supports shared symbolism and embodied belonging. It does not establish a body simulation, health state, power metaphor mechanic, or therapy route. Keep skin, veins, heart, and mouth as drawn metaphors, and let the bunker remain a place inhabited by people rather than a person that can be treated. The abstract page can be read in the dormitory without requiring every sleeper to accept its body language. One resident may see the red generator as a heart, another may see it as a loud machine, and both can wake when the lights change. The child’s hymn-made vision is a way of joining systems that are normally maintained by different people. Skin makes the walls protective; veins make air and water relational; the open mouth gives the hatch a threshold that can speak. These are images, not permissions to treat the bunker as a patient. A candidate passage may let the Therapist ask which part of the body feels tired, then stop before the question becomes an assessment. A singer may change one word of the hymn and discover that the drawing still holds. The dormitory wall can make the picture part of daily life, but it cannot make the metaphor universal. A return can show dust on the pulse lines or a child adding a small ear to the mouth, revealing how communal symbols keep changing. The strongest consequence is recognition: people see that a shelter can be described through a body without becoming one. Keep the red, blue, flesh-tone, and open dark shapes as the child made them. Let disagreement breathe around the image.
