# EXPANSION CW60-03 — The Three Brass Knees

## A prose-first ecology-and-migration story collection about people, memory, and what the record cannot settle.

### Prose Wave 60: Thresholds, Marks, and Listening

## Batch brief

**Content type:** original game-content proposal with scene, diegetic-record, conversation, and conditional-return drafts.
**Content bank:** 48 distinct beat pairings, each with four alternative authored forms (192 candidate passages).
**Current local anchor:** graffiti_vent_walker_drawing — Three Brass Knees on the Duct.

## 1. Expansion thesis

Three Brass Knees on the Duct is a local graffiti posting triggered by friction_vent_sleeping_dispute, available from day 8 with weight 0.8. It records a chimney-soot drawing on a galvanized intake casing: three segmented legs, an asbestos hood, ears like turbine blades, and shaky child script saying HE LISTENS AT NIGHT. This plan treats the creature as a shared figure for sleeplessness and uncertainty. It preserves the fear while refusing to explain the vent, the material, or the creature as a playable threat.

## 2. Story question

When a soot creature with three segmented legs, an asbestos hood, turbine ears, and a warning that it listens at night appears on an intake casing, how can a frightened drawing give children company without becoming a hazard lesson?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The posting record establishes the title, exact text, triggerWorldFlag, minDay, and weight. The prose preserves the image and uses only existing graffiti, vent, sleeping dispute, journal, or narrative consumers. The fuller reading keeps the soot creature on the side of imagination and shared fear. The drawing can make a sleepless child feel less alone because the thing that listens has finally been given a face, legs, and ears. It can also make the room harder to sleep in because a shape on the intake casing remains after the lamp is lowered. That contradiction is the content. The plan should not settle whether the child believes in the walker, whether another child invented the sentence, or whether an adult has mistaken a stain for a creature. Those questions can be spoken in a scene and left unanswered. The asbestos hood and turbine ears are vivid because they mix remembered danger with machine language; they should remain images rather than invitations to explain materials or airflow.
