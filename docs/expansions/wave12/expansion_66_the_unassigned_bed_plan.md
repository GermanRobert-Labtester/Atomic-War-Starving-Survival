# EXPANSION 66 — THE UNASSIGNED BED
## A Sealed Triage Annex Story Plan
### Wave 12: The Quiet Threshold

**Primary location:** Sealed Triage Annex, Old Medical Quarter  
**Location ID:** loc_sealed_triage_annex  
**Document type:** prose-first game-content expansion proposal  
**Target:** at least 120,000 characters of authored narrative and game content  
**Story question:** What does an empty bed mean when the person who was expected to use it chose to leave?  
**Status:** draft plan; current medical and save authorities remain unchanged

---

## 1. Premise

A route fragment leads the player to the Sealed Triage Annex, a decontamination and overflow care station beneath a highway underpass. Its intake ledgers were never removed. The first legible page shows a bed marked “held,” then “empty,” then “transfer unknown.” Years later, that sequence became a story that staff reserved a place for someone important and turned other people away.

A handwritten slip changes the reading. It says the person assigned to the bed asked to leave with the relative who had carried their coat. The slip does not name either person. The official ledger never records the reason for the departure. A porter remembers a coat, a clerk remembers a quiet conversation, and a runner remembers being told not to close the intake line until the two people had reached the service stair.

The player is asked to recover the ledger and copy the annex’s route marks for an existing journal or expedition report. The player is not asked to assign priority, diagnose the unnamed person, decide whether the staff were right, or make a present-day medical choice. The story concerns the difference between an empty place, a withheld place, and a person who is allowed to leave.

Its resolution gives the record a careful label: “bed held; person chose transfer with companion; clinical details not preserved.” The name remains withheld. The annex stays under the current medical, expedition, and radiation authorities.

## 2. The short version

The Old Medical Quarter’s sealed annex was built to take overflow patients and to separate intake from the older hospital wing. A scorched approach sheet points to a service entrance. An engineering page marks two negative-pressure rooms. A torn routing overlay only makes sense when placed over both other fragments.

Inside, the player finds the old bed chart and a carbon intake stub. The chart records one bed held for a person with no stable identifier. A later hand wrote “empty” at shift change. Another hand wrote “did not present.” The same words are copied into an after-action summary, where the unassigned bed becomes evidence in an argument about who was allowed through the doors.

The records are partial, not malicious. The person did arrive. They asked that a companion stay beside them. They did not want their name written on a public copy. The staff held the bed while the companion went to find a family contact. Before the next shift, both people chose to leave by the service stair. The porter saw them go but did not sign the medical log.

The player can preserve the story as an anonymized record or leave the identity field blank without adding an explanatory note. Both outcomes protect the person’s choice. The annex’s documents remain with their original gaps. Nobody gets a retroactive diagnosis, no one is punished, and the rumor is corrected only to the extent the sources support.

## 3. What this plan adds

This plan provides:
- an expedition request and arrival sequence;
- three existing map-fragment interpretations;
- room prose for the intake bay, negative-pressure rooms, records shelf, and service stair;
- authored intake cards, handoff notes, a shift summary, and two witness accounts;
- optional conversations with a former porter, a former clerk, and an adult who knew the unnamed visitor;
- a player choice about how to describe a departure without disclosing a name;
- two truthful archive outcomes and a short epilogue.

## 4. Non-goals

This is not a new clinical feature package. It does not add:
- a second triage queue, medical intake owner, reservation ledger, diagnosis catalog, or admission state;
- a pathogen, radiation illness, exposure scenario, treatment recipe, or medical outcome;
- a patient-name privacy framework for the whole game;
- a mass-casualty mini-game, bedside management loop, or hospital simulator;
- new medical supplies, loot modifiers, medicine scarcity, or settlement-health changes;
- a “correct care” score, morality rating, or relationship bonus;
- authority for the player to reopen the annex or access current treatment rooms.

The story is a historical content visit. Any existing medical data and rules remain under their current owners. A future implementation may use current readable documents, dialogue, journal, and expedition content only after checking their schemas and path consumers.

## 5. Evidence and site fit

The current location catalog defines loc_sealed_triage_annex as an emergency decontamination and overflow trauma station in a fortified highway underpass. It lists surgical instruments, field stretchers, and sealed medical supply crates beneath fallout dust and provides current danger, travel, and radiation values. The expedition catalog has a dedicated entry with a hospital loot table and ordinary medical item categories. The world map includes the node and routes to the Holdfast and Merchant Caravanserai.

The Old Medical Quarter entry in damaged_map_zones.json names the annex and supplies three fragments:
- **Ambulance Approach:** a scorched street plan with a service entrance behind the older wing and “decon first” in the margin.
- **Utility Basement:** a water-bloomed engineering sheet showing an isolated stairwell and two negative-pressure rooms, with “keep clear — intake route.”
- **Casualty Routing:** a torn overlay with greasy folds; its colored arrows only make sense laid over the other two fragments.

These facts establish an authored exploration route and space for records. They do not establish that any patient remains, that the facility is clinically usable, or that the player can safely cross a contamination boundary. The plan never calls the annex sterile, safe, active, or medically stocked for current treatment.

## 6. Theme: an empty space is not an explanation

A bed is visible. A name is not. A line in a ledger is all that remains between the two. The player sees how paperwork can preserve an action while losing its reason, especially when different workers write for different readers.

The story’s repeated objects are:
- an empty bed with a folded blanket;
- a paper name band left unmarked;
- a coat draped over a chair;
- a route arrow that points to the service stair;
- a shift line crossed out and rewritten;
- a small slip that says “came together; left together.”

These objects should not signal a mystery that must be solved by uncovering someone’s full identity. Their purpose is to let the player understand the decision while respecting the person who asked not to be named.

## 7. Main cast

### 7.1 Iri Dane — former intake clerk

Iri worked the front desk at the annex for two shifts before transferring to a shelter clinic. They remember the unnamed visitor because the person asked to keep the companion close and spoke calmly. Iri recorded the request on a private working slip, not the public bed chart. The slip was later filed in the wrong envelope.

Iri’s flaw is that they trust the short form too much. They expected “held” to communicate the situation to the next shift. It instead looked like a reserved bed. Their arc is to acknowledge that the ledger did not preserve enough context, without claiming that a longer note would have guaranteed a better decision.

### 7.2 Ansel Roe — porter

Ansel carried blankets, water, and folded stretchers between the intake bay and the service stair. They remember the visitor by the coat: a dark wool garment with one repaired cuff. Ansel escorted two adults to the exit after the visitor asked to leave. They did not know whether the person had formally been admitted.

Ansel is direct about the limits of the memory. They can identify the sequence and the coat, but not the visitor’s name or medical condition. Their story is not a heroic rescue. They opened the door and carried the coat.

### 7.3 Sera Vonn — after-action scribe

Sera compiled a short summary for an operations binder after the annex closed. She copied the terms “empty” and “did not present” from the bed chart. She did not know about the private request slip. Years later, she hears that her summary was used to claim the staff withheld a bed.

Sera is not a villain. She made a concise copy of the record she had. She is willing to correct its wording once she sees the handoff slip, but she does not rewrite the original report.

### 7.4 Mave Orr — relative of the unnamed visitor

Mave is an older resident in a settlement near the Holdfast. They are not the patient. They carried the visitor’s coat and waited with them at intake. They remember that the person wanted to leave together. Mave does not provide the name, because they were asked not to pass it along.

Mave’s role makes privacy an active choice rather than an authorial mystery. The player may ask whether the visitor survived; Mave says they do not know where the person went afterward. The account does not promise a reunion.

## 10. Opening request at the Holdfast infirmary

A folded route sheet has arrived at the infirmary with the words “old annex — intake books” written on its outer face. Tavi Ren has set it on the table beside a closed first-aid case. The case is not part of the request; it is there because Tavi was sorting supplies.

Tavi says the annex’s records may help reconcile a story repeated by two route crews: someone claims a bed was kept empty while another person was turned away. The crews disagree about whether that happened. The player is asked to bring back the original ledger pages and the route overlay, if reachable.

Tavi is careful:
> Tavi: I am not asking you to decide whether the staff made the right medical choice. We do not have the patient history, and we are not treating anyone there.
>
> Player: Then why recover the ledger?
>
> Tavi: Because people are using one word from it to accuse someone who cannot answer.
>
> Player: Was someone turned away?
>
> Tavi: I do not know. The sheet says a bed was empty. It does not say why.

The player may ask if the visit is dangerous. Tavi refers them to current expedition and radiation guidance. No dialogue invents a live biological threat or bypasses the current dispatch decision.

## 11. Arrival at the Old Medical Quarter

The route descends under the highway. The concrete has a charcoal wash from old fires and gray dust from the road above. The annex entrance is not grand. It is a reinforced service door set behind a collapsed ambulance bay.

The first map fragment shows an arrow behind the older wing. The player can read “decon first” beside the service entrance. A second fragment shows a stairwell separated from the rooms. The third overlay contains arrows whose colors have faded; the journal text identifies each line by label as well as color.

The route is described using current expedition and map authority. The story does not add a timed approach, contamination gauge, or stealth test. If the door is inaccessible under present game state, the quest remains a document-recovery proposal until the owner confirms a real route.

## 14. The intake corridor

The corridor is narrow enough that a stretcher could pass only when the bench is folded up. The bench has two hinge marks and a strap to keep it against the wall. A pencil label on the strap reads “clear before transfer.” The paint beneath it is darker, suggesting the strap was used often.

A second strip, stuck to the opposite wall, says:

The two instructions do not contradict. They apply to different spaces. A person waiting by the first sign would still leave the stair clear. A character should point this out if the player calls it an impossible layout.

## 17. Bed chart: the line everyone copied

>

A later summary has copied only:

The player can see how the rumor grew without being told that the scribe was careless. “Held” is a normal status field. “Unassigned” is a final status field. A copy that starts and ends on those words can look like someone protected an empty bed. The missing context is not hidden behind a difficult roll; it is on nearby papers.

## 18. What the chart does and does not say

The bed chart supports a small number of statements:
- one bed was held for a time;
- the form has no name;
- the bed was empty at one count;
- the record labels it unassigned at shift close.

It does not support:
- that a patient was denied care;
- that a bed was reserved for a person with status;
- that a patient was admitted;
- that the person was medically cleared;
- that the annex was full or empty;
- that the staff knew where the visitor went afterward.

Tavi reviews this distinction in dialogue if the player asks for a medical interpretation. They say the chart records bed state and shift handoff. “The patient’s choice is on another page,” Tavi says. “The chart cannot carry it unless someone writes it there.”

If the player asks whether the blanket belonged to the unnamed visitor, Mave says “I cannot say.” That response stays fixed. The object may evoke a possible connection, but the narrative does not promote it to proof.

>

>

The slip is signed with Iri’s initials. It does not give the visitor’s name, the companion’s name, or a diagnosis. It records a request and what Iri planned to do with the bed. The phrase “bed held” was intended to tell the next clerk not to release the space while the companion was away. Iri did not expect it to be read as a reserved bed after the handoff context was separated.

If the player asks why they did not write “patient requested privacy,” Iri says the slip was on the desk, and they did not want the private name traveling with it. They used the shortest wording they thought would be understood by the next shift. The player may see this as an avoidable mistake, but the story does not need to label it negligence.
