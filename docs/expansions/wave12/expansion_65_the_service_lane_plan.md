# EXPANSION 65 — THE SERVICE LANE
## A Quarantine Barn Story Plan
### Wave 12: The Quiet Threshold

**Primary location:** Quarantine Barn, Pasture Valley  
**Location ID:** loc_quarantine_barn  
**Document type:** prose-first game-content expansion proposal  
**Target:** at least 120,000 characters, primarily authored story content  
**Story question:** When a boundary was marked for safety, who was allowed to decide when it could open again?  
**Status:** proposed content, not implementation approval

## 1. The player-facing premise

A pair of map fragments leads the player to a timber quarantine barn outside the Pasture Valley farm lanes. A herd ledger says one service gate remained available for water and feed. A second hand has marked the same lane “service only,” then crossed out the word “only” years later. The player is asked to recover the old veterinary ledger and a working route sketch, but the surviving records tell different stories about a family that kept bringing feed through the restricted lane.

At first, the marks look like evidence of a rule being broken. The full account is less clean. One caretaker kept the lane closed to people and open to supplies; a farm worker sometimes carried water through it after the keeper went to sleep; and a third person wrote “open” on a day when the gate was physically unlatched but the animals remained penned. Each person used “open” to mean a different thing.

The player’s task is not to judge the old quarantine or determine an infection status. It is to put the working route back into context so present-day travelers do not mistake a service lane for a public shortcut, and so the people who maintained the barn are not remembered as either cruel or careless. The story concludes with a new, ordinary route note: who may use the lane, what the map does not establish, and why the closure mark remains on the main gate.

## 2. Short pitch

The barn stands beyond a low ridge where the road leaves the pasture fields. Its roof is intact. The lime-washed stalls are dry in some places and dusted with ash in others. Two disinfectant basins sit beneath a cracked roof vent. A service lane bends around the main yard and reaches a narrow side gate. The old map says the lane bypasses the yard. A later hand has written “service only.”

The player arrives looking for the veterinary ledger and a route sketch after a caravan scout reports that the barn’s main doors might offer shelter from rain. Asha Kel, a pasture worker at the nearest settlement, asks the player to make clear whether the side lane is still passable. The current map already provides the route and hazard authority; the story concerns the meanings people attached to its gates.

Inside, the player finds six seasons of short notes rather than a complete history. One caretaker records feed placed in the outer trough without entry. A farm worker records carrying water in after the line froze. The veterinary ledger records a stall moved to keep one animal away from the rest. None of those statements proves that the lane was open to everyone.

A surviving local, Dena Mor, remembers using the lane with permission. Another, Harl Senn, remembers being stopped at the gate and waiting until morning. Each memory is accurate from where it stood. The player can return with a route card that marks the barn’s boundary without erasing the work done inside it.

The story uses animal care as setting, not as a new disease or herd-management system. Its emotional center is a shared gate that had to mean more than one thing: containment, access, labor, and, eventually, an ordinary way to bring clean water to a thirsty animal.

## 3. Scope and non-goals

This proposal adds authored location scenes, expedition dialogue, readable notes, witness conversations, one map-fragment interpretation, and a short return outcome. It uses the existing location, expedition, map, encounter, journal, and dialogue owners after a promotion audit confirms their current contracts.

It does not add:
- an infection, herd health, animal quarantine, or vaccination system;
- a gate-operating mechanic or a new travel graph;
- a disease clock, exposure meter, outbreak simulation, or medical treatment loop;
- ownership or access control over pasture routes;
- a new veterinary profession, animal relationship, or livestock ledger;
- a special resource called “clean feed” or “quarantine supplies”;
- a route bonus, expedition penalty, or map unlock based on moral score;
- a claim that present-day animals are infected or that the barn remains contaminated.

The player reads a historical boundary and produces a better route note. The present-day route stays under existing world-map and expedition data.

## 5. Theme: the gate had more than one side

The story returns to the barn’s side gate, a narrow frame with a simple latch. From the yard, it kept animals from the farm lane. From the lane, it kept the yard from becoming a through-road. From the trough, it was the only opening close enough for a hose. The same object divided people, protected work, and let essential tasks continue.

Characters should refer to what they actually saw: a boot beside the latch, a bucket left on the dry side, a hoof print ending at the lime line, a note that says “before dawn.” They do not explain “quarantine ethics” to the player. Their disagreement is about a gate, a bucket, the feed route, and a time when the keeper was away.

No scene should imply that everyone in the valley agreed on the procedure. The barn’s records were written by different people, at different times, for different readers. No one had authority to describe the whole valley.

## 6. Main characters

### 6.1 Dena Mor — current pasture worker

Dena tends a small pasture plot and maintains the public watering stand. She learned the service-lane route from a relative who used to deliver sacks to the barn. She has never claimed that she was a staff member or that she had permission to enter every stall. Her specific memory concerns the outer trough and the side gate.

She wants the player to mark the route accurately because a recent rain made the low road hard to cross. She does not want the barn turned into a shortcut that brings strangers through the yard. Dena’s challenge is that she uses “the lane was open” to mean “I could complete my delivery.” She knows that wording could sound broader than what she remembers.

### 6.2 Harl Senn — former relief driver

Harl brought feed and clean water to the pasture during the early crisis. He was once turned back at the main gate and waited in the road until a caretaker carried the sacks through the side opening. He never saw the stall interior. He thinks the map’s “service only” note was written to stop arguments at the entrance.

Harl is not angry at the caretaker. He is frustrated that future travelers will read “service only” as a simple label and not know who counted as service. His memory helps the player see how a policy can be clear to its author and ambiguous to the person standing outside.

### 6.3 Oren Taal — former barn keeper, absent

Oren managed the barn on the day rota. His writing survives in the ledger, on feed tags, and in one short letter. He kept water and supplies moving while trying to limit foot traffic through the stalls. He is never described as a medical expert. His decisions were practical and imperfect.

Oren’s flaw is that he wrote labels for workers who already knew the routine. Years later, those labels outlived the routine. His arc, delivered through records, is not to be vindicated; it is to have his care understood without making his decisions universal rules.

### 6.4 Iva Sen — irrigation runner

Iva carried water to the fields before the barn was repurposed, and she kept doing so after the lane received the “service only” mark. She remembers the phrase as a direction about where to walk, not a ban on all visitors. She cannot say whether the caretaker intended that meaning.

Iva is the only witness who remembers the chalk mark being added. She says it was placed at knee height so a person carrying a yoke could see it. That detail supports the story’s physical staging but does not identify who wrote it.

### 6.5 Tavi Ren — animal handler, one ledger entry

Tavi appears through one concise note and, optionally, through a later conversation at the water stand. Tavi moved a stall divider after one animal repeatedly pressed against the wall. The ledger records the movement but not the animal’s condition. Tavi does not claim a cure or diagnosis.

Tavi’s role is to remind the player that a stall assignment records space and work, not a full animal history. The character’s lines are observational and short.

### 6.6 Pell Ardo — young map apprentice

Pell is learning to update route cards. They want a clear symbol for a gate that is passable but not a public route. They are not foolish for wanting a simple legend; they have to draw the map small enough to carry. Pell gives the player a practical audience for the final note.

## 9. Opening scene at the pasture stand

The water stand is a low wooden frame beside the field path. A metal cup hangs from a nail. Asha Kel has tied a clean strip of cloth over the spout to keep grit out. She removes the cup before speaking, wipes it, and sets it upside down on the bench.

She asks for the barn ledger because the pasture crew is updating a route card after rain cut a narrow channel across the lower road. The barn’s side lane might be the drier approach, but the map says “service only.” Asha does not know whether that meant “workers only,” “deliveries only,” or simply “do not take the herd through here.”

Dena Mor is kneeling beside a watering trough, checking the small crack at its base. She says a relative used to bring feed up the side lane. Harl Senn, standing by a cart with one wheel wrapped in cord, says he was once told to wait outside that same gate. Neither one calls the other wrong.

**Asha:** “We need a route that can be read by someone who has never met us.”

**Dena:** “The lane carried water.”

**Harl:** “It also stopped me at the gate.”

**Dena:** “You were bringing a cart through the yard.”

**Harl:** “I was bringing feed.”

Asha asks the player to bring back the ledger, note what the lane physically connects, and avoid turning a remembered exception into a general invitation. The request does not ask the player to decide who may cross the route today. It asks for a useful map description.

### 9.1 Optional dialogue

>
> Dena: The lime was on the walls before I ever went near it.
>
> Harl: The rule I saw was at the gate. It did not say what was in the stalls.

**Player:** “Who had permission?”

> Dena: The keeper knew the people who carried feed.
>
> Harl: I knew how to carry feed. He still told me to wait.
>
> Asha: That is why we should describe the route, not write permission for a person we cannot ask.

**Player:** “Could we use it now?”

> Asha: We can look at it and record what is passable today.
>
> Dena: I would not take a loaded cart down the lower side after rain.
>
> Harl: Then put that on the route card, too.

The conversation places present conditions beside historical language without turning the player into a present-day gatekeeper.

## 10. The approach

The trail follows a shallow contour above the pasture. A weathered post carries two nails and no sign. One nail is bright where a strip of cord was recently removed. The other has turned the color of the post around it.

The barn roof appears through a stand of alder. The side lane is visible as a strip of hard earth between two low ditches. It meets the outer yard at a narrow gate and turns again toward a water cistern. The main gate is broader, but a fallen rail blocks half its width.

The first approach description should be concise enough to appear in movement:

> The barn sits with its long wall facing the slope. Lime still shows around the lower boards where rain has not reached. The side lane skirts the open yard and ends at a narrow gate. A trough stands outside it, dry now, with a clean circle in the dust where a cup once rested.

If the player inspects the ground, there are hoof marks inside the yard, wheel marks outside it, and one narrow line where a bucket dragged. The marks are old and cannot establish who used the lane. If the player asks Dena, she says the dragging line could be from any winter before the road washed out.

## 11. The outer trough

The trough is built from a split feed barrel. One end sits on a flat stone; the other has sunk slightly. A narrow iron band holds a repair patch in place. The repair is later than the original wood but older than the current road wash.

A faded card, tied beneath the band, reads:

> WATER HERE
>
> Do not move the trough inside the fence.
>
> Clean the cup after use.
>
> If the line is frozen, tell the keeper.

No signature or date remains. This card is the clearest evidence that people could receive water without entering the yard. It does not say whether they could use the side gate for other tasks.

If the player asks whether the trough was meant for animals or people, Dena says both kinds used it, but not the same cup. She has no record of who cleaned it. Harl remembers filling a trough once but says he cannot swear this is the same barrel.

The scene uses ordinary interaction: the player may wipe dust from the card, copy its words, and return it to the nail. No new contamination or water-quality mechanic is added.

## 12. Entry bay

The side gate opens inward a handspan and stops against a short chain. The chain is not locked. A wooden block has been placed beneath the gate to keep its edge from sinking into the mud. The side bay contains two empty pails, a scoop with a cracked handle, and a coat peg with a repaired loop.

A lime line crosses the floor just inside the gate. One end has been washed away. On the wall, a slate reads:

> OUTER SIDE: leave bundles
>
> INNER SIDE: keeper moves them
>
> If nobody answers, wait by the trough

Asha’s request asked the player to describe the route. The entry bay provides enough physical evidence to say that goods could be handed through without a visitor entering the stalls. It does not show whether the gate was ever fully open or which specific deliveries used this procedure.

Pell’s map fragment sketches the lane as a line between the outer trough and the side bay. A later route mark extends the line through the yard, but its date is missing. The player can tell Pell that the line should stop at the bay, or write “continuation uncertain.” Both are valid because the map does not establish a public path through the barn.


## 13. Main yard

The main yard is narrower than the map sketch suggests. The fence was built to hold a small group of animals, not to make a full farm enclosure. A hinge has been replaced with a piece of flat metal. The new metal is drilled through an older post whose surface still carries a rectangular pale patch where a sign used to hang.

A low rail separates the yard from the covered stalls. Beyond it, the roof is supported by timber braces. One brace is newer than the others and bears a pencil date with only the season visible. The yard’s center is packed hard. At the edge, grass has grown through a shallow channel.

A sound description, if the area is quiet: a loose sheet on the roof makes one short scrape when the wind turns. There are no animal calls. Do not place a ghostly voice, strange breath, or implied living animal in the empty stalls. The barn is a workplace after its work stopped.

The player can inspect:
- the gate hinge, replaced from a field fence;
- the pale patch on the post, whose missing sign cannot be reconstructed;
- the brace date, only season and year fragment legible;
- the channel leading toward the cistern;
- a flat stone placed where one cart wheel repeatedly cut the mud.

Each detail may help place the barn’s use in time, but none establishes a medical status. The scene gives space to look around without turning every object into a clue.

## 14. The first ledger page

The herd ledger rests in a wall box under a piece of tin. Its cover is swollen at the corners but has not torn. A thin cord has been passed through the binding. The page on top is a daily rotation card, not a complete case record.

> NORTH STALL — one brown ewe
>
> Hold apart from east pen until keeper check.
>
> Water outside if gate remains chained.
>
> Feed may be passed to outer trough.
>
> Do not move gate block while cart is loaded.
>
> Signed: O.T.

The date is missing. The card’s reverse lists feed amounts in a column, then repeats the same quantity three times in different handwriting. A later person drew a box around “outside” and wrote “not in stall” beside it.

Asha reads “hold apart” as a stall arrangement. Dena says the phrase could have meant temporary separation while the keeper checked the animal. Harl assumes the animal was sick. No one knows. The current data names quarantine stalls and disinfectant basins, but this particular card does not identify a disease or diagnosis.

If the player asks whether the ewe was infectious, Leena? No, Asha answers: “We have no test result, no symptoms, and no date. The ledger says where the animal was kept.” The story must not invent a pathogen, a fatal outcome, or a cure.

## 15. Stalls and partitions

The stalls are shallow pens with removable dividers. A chalk number remains above each opening. One divider has been moved and secured with a pin made from a bent nail. The floor beneath it has fewer scrape marks than the surrounding boards.

The player can read an old chalk list:
- stall one: trough under window;
- stall two: no bucket on left;
- stall three: keep the divider loose;
- stall four: wash pan returned to outer shelf.

The list is not a treatment plan. It is a work reminder, partly about how a person can reach each stall. The phrase “keep the divider loose” may reflect an animal’s movement, ventilation, or ease of cleaning. The record does not say. A later visitor added a question mark after it.

At stall three, a small scratch line runs along the lower board. It is too low for an adult hand and could have been made by a tool or hoof. No character declares it a child’s mark or a particular animal’s. The player can record it as wear, if they choose.

### 15.1 Tavi’s note

## 16. The wash basin room

The wash basin room is behind a half-door. Two basins sit on a shelf, each with a separate wooden handle. A wall card says “outer tools” above one basin and “inside work” above the other. The lettering is not color-coded; each label is cut into the wood as well as painted.

The lower shelf carries a short-handled brush, a tin scoop, and a roll of cloth tied with a knot that can be opened with wet fingers. No liquids remain. A circular stain at the corner could be soap or lime wash, but it has no odor. The player may inspect it; no check identifies it as a medical residue.

An instruction on the inside of the cupboard:
> Rinse the basin. Do not pour used water through the yard channel.
>
> If the rinse freezes, stop. Tell the keeper.
>
> No one has to carry a full pail alone.

The final sentence has been added in a different hand. The note makes the room more human without declaring a formal policy. It may have been written by a worker who knew the pails were heavy. The player can ask Dena if she recognizes the hand. She says no, but she uses the same instruction now at the pasture stand.

The room’s interaction should be simple: read the labels, notice that the two basins were assigned different sides of work, and continue. Do not introduce sterilization crafting or a sanitation skill.

## 17. The service lane from inside

A narrow door leads from the bay to the lane. Its sill has been repaired with two short boards. A scratch in the lower plank marks the height of water that pooled there before the repair. On the frame, an old hook holds a small wooden tag.

> LANE OPEN FOR CARRY
>
> KEEP MAIN GATE SHUT
>
> Ask before bringing a cart through

The tag is undated. One corner has been rounded by repeated handling. The phrase “open for carry” is the first explicit indication that people used the lane while the main gate stayed closed. It still does not say which people were authorized or whether “carry” referred only to supplies.

### 17.1 Conversation with Dena

> Dena: My aunt carried the sack to the side gate. The keeper took it from her. That is how it worked.
>
> Player: Did she enter the barn?
>
> Dena: No.
>
> Player: Then what did “the lane was open” mean?
>
> Dena: It meant she could get the sack there. I never said the whole yard was open.
>
> Player: A map reader might.
>
> Dena: Then the map needs another line.

If the player has not read the tag, Dena’s answer is less exact:
> Dena: She went by the trough. I remember her coming back with the strap empty. Maybe she left it outside. I was short enough that the fence blocked most of my view.

Dena does not recite facts she could not have seen as a child. If the player asks which year, she says she does not remember the year. The scene protects the scale of a family memory without making it omniscient.

This action is a present-day observation, not a permanent state change. It establishes that the gate can swing and that its latch currently misses. It does not establish that the lane is safe after rain or that a loaded cart can pass.

## 21. Optional event: the loose roof sheet

## 24. Map fragment 2: Irrigation Run

The irrigation map has been folded so often that the lane lines are darker than the field boundaries. The service lane is inked over in a second hand. A small arrow points to the outer trough. The words “service only” occupy the margin.

## 25. Map fragment interpretation choice

Pell offers three draft captions:

**A.** “Quarantine Barn access lane: service use only.”

**B.** “Outer lane reached trough and side bay. Historical delivery use shown; yard access not established.”

**C.** “Barn closed.”

A is concise but hides who counted as service. B is longer and precise. C is too broad and repeats the old closure mark without explaining the outer lane. The player can select B or write “lane passable; present latch does not catch” after physically testing the gate.

Pell says the map needs both a title and a boundary. If the player chooses A, Pell asks whether a future reader might think the lane itself is closed. If the player chooses C, Dena says “That was never the whole lane.” The player may revise without penalty. The exercise shows the difference between an old rule and a current observation.

## 62. Reading the barn’s quiet

## 80. Tone test: what the scene must leave unsaid

The story is stronger if the player is not told:
- what illness, if any, affected the herd;
- whether a particular child in the old note was Dena;
- whether the second feed sack reached a stall;
- who wrote every unsigned tag;
- whether a single person set the “service only” rule;
- why the side gate became unreliable;
- whether all visitors received the same answer;
