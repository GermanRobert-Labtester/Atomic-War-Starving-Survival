# CONTENT EXPANSION CW32-01 — The Name Page Stays Torn

## A classroom return where a blank name, a red rectangle, and one curious student resist becoming evidence.

### Prose Wave 32: The Paper People Leave Behind

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 32 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (128 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_school_gymnasium` — School Gymnasium.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The gymnasium still carries the marks of two uses that do not fit neatly together: a school with nine pupils and the first-winter shelter whose cot rows left stains in the floor. A half-filled workbook has its name page torn away. Its exercises move from directions to a family’s daily water question, a drawing of a shelter, and a future the student does not fill in. Wren attends the school and wants to know what things were for, but nothing in the records says she wrote this book. The expansion gives Wren and the player room to read carefully without turning another child’s blank into a puzzle to solve. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

What can a child learn from a page when the name is gone and the answer is still blank?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The discoverable workbook is attributed only to an unnamed child student; its name page is torn, its era approximate, and its exercise answers incomplete. ## 5. Human center

The human center is a reader who wants to help and discovers that a confident answer can take ownership of someone else’s unfinished page. Wren asks practical questions because explanations are how she encounters the world. The unnamed student left blanks for reasons the file cannot recover. A respectful scene lets the player say “I don’t know” without making ignorance the ending; the page can still be read, preserved, and shared with its limits intact. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

Wren’s proposed dialogue stays direct and age-appropriate: short questions, concrete object names, no adult essay about history. The workbook voice is reproduced only through brief references to its exercises and blank fields; the plan does not invent the child’s handwriting beyond the source description. A later reader uses precise attribution. The narrator notices the floor and paper instead of explaining what the reader should feel. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 8. Player agency

The player may inspect the workbook, ask Wren what an object was for, offer a clearly labeled guess, or leave the blank unanswered. They may choose to preserve the source wording rather than fill its fields. These are prose choices unless an existing owner already supports a consequence. The player cannot rename the student, declare that Wren authored the page, or complete the exercises on the child’s behalf. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Disturbance — the torn name page

The workbook arrives without its student’s name, but the surviving exercises still carry a point of view.

### 2. Discovery — the shelter drawing

A red rectangle and smaller shapes invite interpretation while refusing to identify a household.

### 3. Interpretation — Wren’s questions

Wren asks what the objects might mean; her curiosity does not become authorship.

### 4. Complication — two histories in one room

The school and first-winter shelter coexist in the location record; the plan does not make one erase the other.

### 5. Choice — answer, guess, or leave blank

The player decides how to talk about uncertainty, without writing over the source.

### 6. Return — the preserved blank

A later reader finds that a careful copy can keep an unanswered line readable.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
