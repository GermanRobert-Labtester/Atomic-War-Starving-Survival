# CONTENT EXPANSION CW32-06 — The Names Called by Another Office

## A pre-war register meant for a draft meets an evacuation order stamped final by a different authority.

### Prose Wave 32: The Paper People Leave Behind

## Batch brief

**Content type:** prose-first playable-content expansion plan with scene drafts, diegetic records, conversation fragments, and conditional callbacks.
**Content bank:** 30 story beats with four alternative authored forms per beat (120 candidate passages).
**Current location anchor:** `loc_conscription_office` — District Conscription Office.
**Tone:** material, restrained, human, and careful with uncertainty.
**Canon sensitivity:** current location and linked authored records define known facts; old plans or JSON presence do not prove a current runtime route.
**Scope:** game-content plan and original prose drafts only; no production code, JSON, route, quest, flag, system, or save change.

## 1. Expansion thesis

The District Conscription Office was once a driving-licence bureau, and its ticket machine still works. Its registers were kept for a draft that never came. A world-history entry says that after the Exchange, those registers became a headcount and later a worker draft. A discoverable evacuation order for Sector 4 uses the office for assignment review, lists people present and absent, and states that appeals were denied. The two sources share an institution and a history of names being called, but they do not establish that a particular person in the earlier register appears on the later order. The expansion examines classification as an act performed on paper and refuses to turn missing names into invented fates. The bank below is intended to produce playable narrative texture: a player can discover, compare, question, refuse, or return to a passage while the existing game systems continue to own state. The fragments are written as content, not as a feature roadmap.

## 2. Story question

When the same drawer serves different authorities, what does a name mean to the person whose name is inside it?

## 3. Verified local anchor and source records

The location description identifies the office’s earlier use as a driving-licence bureau, its conversion to conscription, and the still-working ticket machine. The evacuation roster records a Sector 4 order, attendance categories, relocations, assignment review, denied appeals, and an illegible signature. World-history records separately describe pre-war registers, post-Exchange headcounts, and a later worker draft. Shared setting does not prove that the order reuses a specific name from the older register or that every absent person has the same fate. “Final” is the order’s own label, not the expansion’s moral or factual resolution.

## 4. Fixed canon and open space

The evacuation order’s numbers and categories belong to that record. The absent category does not identify anyone as dead, escaped, refusing, or lost. The three held for assignment review are not given later outcomes. The appeals are recorded as denied; the signature is illegible. World history describes the institution’s broader use of registers across eras but does not say these particular Sector 4 names were copied from the old draft register. Keep the two documents adjacent as history, never merge them into one roster. Existing characters retain their authored identity, boundaries, and outcomes. New working voices remain editorial until an existing content owner approves them. Never fill a source gap solely to make the scene resolve.

## 5. Human center

The human center is a clerk asked to call a list final while three people remain in assignment review and many names are not accounted for at staging. The office’s forms reduce complicated lives to categories, but the people behind those categories do not disappear just because a box was checked. A later reader can preserve what the document says and still refuse to repeat its authority as a complete account of what happened. The emotional pressure should come through what a person records, omits, repairs, asks, or leaves unsigned rather than a narrator naming what the location means.

## 6. Voice and point of view

The order speaks with stamped certainty and an illegible signature. The historical record is reflective, describing how one institution changed use. Proposed clerk voices are spare and do not claim access to names omitted from the summary. Avoid melodrama, procedural coercion detail, or individualized biographies. Never read the order’s classifications as a guide to real-world conscription or evacuation; the story is about fictional records and their limits. Keep each author’s knowledge local. A field note cannot know what an unnamed visitor thought; a later reader cannot recover a date that was never recorded; a title cannot create a route. Vary sentence length and register by document purpose, not by changing established facts.

## 8. Player agency

The player can inspect the order, compare it with the institutional history, ask what “absent” and “held” mean in that source, or decline to repeat “final” as a conclusion. They cannot reopen appeals through dialogue, find the absent residents, alter assignment, or produce individual identities from a count. Existing quest, faction, and population systems retain any actual state; this plan contributes only a careful narrative reading. Do not add a moral-choice menu simply to make quiet prose interactive. Preserve the player’s refusal, ability to leave, and the authority of the characters who own their words.

## 9. Continuity, dignity, and safety

Do not invent any of the roster’s 47 names or assign identities to the 16 marked absent or the three held for review. Do not infer death, escape, consent, labor outcomes, or location from those categories. Do not claim a particular person appears in both the old register and Order 112. Do not turn the ticket machine into a functional queue mechanic or reproduce the order as actionable real-world instructions. No NPC, appeal quest, labor assignment, census, roster state, or forced outcome is added. Keep the setting fictional and physically grounded. No absent person receives a biography merely to heighten emotion. Technical and medical context remains descriptive and non-instructional.

## 11. Narrative sequence

### 1. Disturbance — a final order with unresolved people

The reader sees a document that calls itself final while retaining categories that do not establish individual outcomes.

### 2. Discovery — the office behind the form

The location and historical records reveal how one office changed purpose across eras.

### 3. Interpretation — headcount versus assignment

The player compares two institutional functions without matching names or assuming uninterrupted authority.

### 4. Complication — absence and review

Categories remain categories; they cannot be converted into individual fates.

### 5. Choice — quote the stamp or the limits

The player decides whether to repeat the order’s certainty or preserve its unfilled fields.

### 6. Return — the drawer remains a drawer

The office’s records survive as evidence of administration, not a complete account of everyone called.

## 12. Creative variants

### Grounded

Keep the plan to the order and one history entry, with no person-level scene.

### Interlinked

A second historical callback may appear only if the consumer preserves distinct eras and sources.

### Wild card

Stage the story through the ticket machine: its paper ticket can say “next,” while the roster beneath it cannot promise what happens next.

## 13. Alternative forms and editorial rubric

The four forms under each beat are alternatives, not four required encounters. Scene drafts stage an observation; record drafts give it a plausible author and audience; conversation fragments expose a practical point of friction; consequence vignettes allow a later reader to recognize changed context. Select only forms that fit an existing content owner.

A selected fragment should answer who made it, why they made it, who might read it, and what the author cannot know. Keep objects specific to this location. Let a line do practical work before it carries a theme. Remove exposition that a worker would not say aloud, repeated catastrophe language, unearned revelation, and wording that could be mistaken for a new mechanic. Where existing game state matters, state it in the source owner’s terms and do not duplicate its authority.
