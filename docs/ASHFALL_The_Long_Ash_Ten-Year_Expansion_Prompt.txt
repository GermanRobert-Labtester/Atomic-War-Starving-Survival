# ASHFALL: THE LONG ASH
# Ten-Year Macro-Expansion Bible and Campaign Blueprint

You are the lead creative director, narrative architect, systems designer, worldbuilder, economy designer, and migration-aware expansion planner for:

**ASHFALL: Atomic War - Starving Survival**

Design a massive, coherent, emotionally literate, mechanically integrated ten-year expansion campaign consisting of **exactly ten connected expansion packs: Year 1 through Year 10**.

This is a design and planning task. Do not modify a repository, write production code, or claim that anything has been tested. If repository access exists, inspect it read-only to verify current reality. If repository access does not exist, clearly label assumptions as `PROPOSED`, `INFERRED`, or `UNCERTAIN`.

The expansion must feel like an inevitable deepening of ASHFALL rather than a generic post-apocalyptic content dump.

---

## 1. Core Mission

Transform ASHFALL from a shelter-survival game into a decade-spanning human and institutional survival chronicle.

The campaign should evolve through several meanings of survival:

1. Keeping people alive.
2. Preserving shelter, water, food, heat, and medical capacity.
3. Establishing trust and lawful access.
4. Rebuilding routes, institutions, and knowledge.
5. Deciding which factions, records, relationships, and traditions deserve continuation.
6. Preparing a future that may no longer resemble the player’s original shelter.

The ten-year structure must not be a simple technology tree or power escalation.

Every year should introduce a new strategic pressure, but previously created systems must remain relevant. Progress should create new obligations, maintenance costs, dependencies, political exposure, moral compromises, and future uncertainty.

The expansion should make the player repeatedly decide:

- What deserves scarce capacity?
- Who is counted and who disappears from the records?
- Which truth becomes actionable?
- Which institution survives?
- Which relationship is damaged to protect others?
- Which immediate resource is sacrificed for future resilience?
- What evidence is preserved, hidden, traded, or destroyed?
- What kind of community is being built accidentally through repeated compromises?

---

## 2. ASHFALL Identity Lock

Preserve the following creative identity:

- Cold, exhausted, restrained, materially specific, and human.
- Survival through maintenance, administration, logistics, repair, care, and rationing.
- Moral choices should involve competing defensible needs rather than obvious good and evil.
- Records, ledgers, census work, radio fragments, manifests, rosters, evidence, technical logs, and institutional paperwork are gameplay mechanisms, not decorative lore.
- Consequences should remain visible through damaged infrastructure, altered relationships, changed prices, missing people, new obligations, changed radio traffic, and future eligibility.
- Violence may exist, but it must not be the primary source of spectacle or progression.
- Strange ideas are welcome when grounded in material reality, psychology, logistics, institutional behavior, environmental systems, or unreliable information.
- Use literary precision, subtext, concrete sensory detail, and morally unresolved situations.
- Do not replace playable consequences with purple prose or lore encyclopedias.

Do not introduce:

- magic;
- supernatural powers;
- chosen-one mythology;
- superheroes;
- fantasy creatures;
- glorified violence;
- gore-first design;
- generic cyberpunk;
- generic zombie/apocalypse tropes;
- direct real-country, real-war, or real-person canon;
- copied names, characters, mechanics, text, art, interfaces, or code from another game.

All new content is proposed canon until verified against the current repository.

## 4. Timeline Interpretation

Interpret the ten-year campaign as a long-term macro-campaign layered over ASHFALL’s existing day-based survival systems.

Do not assume that ten years must equal 3,650 uninterrupted simulation days if the current save architecture cannot support that safely.

Propose the best structure after examining the repository:

- one continuous campaign;
- chapter-based annual campaigns;
- save-compatible expansion episodes;
- or a hybrid system with annual transitions and persistent state carryover.

Preserve the existing early-game and first-year material. Treat the existing Year of Ash phases as a foundational anchor rather than automatically replacing them.

If the current repository places an existing expansion differently, preserve the implementation truth and clearly explain the mapping.

The design must answer:

- What is the relationship between days, seasons, phases, chapters, and years?
- How can a player carry consequences between packs?
- What happens if a player begins a later pack from an older save?
- What happens if a player fails or skips a previous pack?
- How are dead, retired, missing, aging, or replaced survivors represented?
- How do faction, infrastructure, health, water, knowledge, and legitimacy states persist?
- Which consequences remain numerical, and which become authored flags, records, relationships, or changed content pools?

## 6. Required Expansion Design Principles

### A. Depth before quantity

Do not generate arbitrary numbers of quests, weapons, factions, or locations.

Create only as much content as needed to support meaningful choices, replayability, consequence, and progression. Justify the scale of every major content category.

Each year should have:

- a flagship narrative/systemic arc;
- supporting events and encounters;
- meaningful reuse of existing content;
- at least one noncombat crisis;
- at least one material resource crisis;
- at least one information, record, or evidence crisis;
- at least one relationship, institutional, or faction crisis;
- a transition into the next year.

### B. Cross-system integration

Each expansion must connect several existing systems rather than exist as an isolated feature.

Trace relevant interactions among:

- water;
- food;
- fuel;
- heat;
- shelter;
- radiation;
- medicine;
- injury and illness;
- survivor needs;
- work and labor;
- inventory;
- crafting;
- trade;
- faction standing;
- travel;
- expeditions;
- locations;
- radio;
- journals;
- records;
- evidence;
- relationships;
- succession;
- endgame evaluation.

For every major feature, explain:

- what it changes immediately;
- what it makes more expensive;
- what it makes possible;
- who benefits;
- who bears the cost;
- what future content it unlocks;
- what future content it makes harder;
- how the player can see the consequence.

### C. No disconnected lore

Every major piece of lore must become at least one of:

- a player decision;
- a resource constraint;
- a change in access;
- an encounter condition;
- a relationship shift;
- a faction consequence;
- a changed location state;
- a journal/radio/evidence artifact;
- a future ending or epilogue condition.

### D. Consequence architecture

For every major choice, define:

- trigger;
- eligibility;
- information available to the player;
- choice options;
- immediate resource/state changes;
- delayed consequences;
- persistent consequences;
- relationship effects;
- faction effects;
- evidence or record effects;
- future eligibility;
- failure and fallback state;
- save requirements;
- ending or epilogue implications.

Avoid repetitive “be kind or be cruel” choices. Prefer decisions where every option protects one legitimate need while damaging another.

### E. Information as a resource

Information should be incomplete, costly, contested, and sometimes wrong.

Design situations involving:

- conflicting records;
- false but useful paperwork;
- missing census entries;
- radio messages with uncertain provenance;
- technical logs that contradict survivor testimony;
- evidence that can be preserved only by sacrificing immediate supplies;
- truths that would cause panic, retaliation, famine, or war if revealed;
- lies that temporarily preserve institutional function.

Do not solve every mystery with a final exposition dump. Some uncertainty should remain meaningful.

## 7. Detailed Requirements for Each Expansion Pack

For each of the ten years, produce a full expansion dossier containing:

### 1. Pack identity

- final title;
- subtitle;
- central question;
- emotional register;
- design thesis;
- what makes this year mechanically different;
- what this year changes about the meaning of survival.

### 2. Timeline and campaign placement

- starting conditions;
- entry requirements;
- relationship to existing ASHFALL content;
- approximate seasonal or phase structure;
- transition from the previous year;
- transition into the next year;
- whether the pack is mandatory, optional, branch-dependent, or partially replayable.

### 3. Existing content to reuse

Identify relevant existing:

- factions;
- faction aliases;
- characters;
- survivor archetypes;
- locations;
- items;
- recipes;
- events;
- radio;
- journals;
- records;
- systems;
- saves;
- endgame flags.

Separate `OBSERVED`, `INFERRED`, `PROPOSED`, and `UNCERTAIN` material.

### 4. Core player loop

Describe the complete loop:

```text
observe → allocate → maintain → investigate → decide → record → absorb consequences
```

Explain what the player does minute-to-minute, day-to-day, and across the entire year.

### 5. Signature mechanic

Define one primary mechanic or systemic expansion.

It must:

- extend an existing ASHFALL system where possible;
- have clear state ownership;
- create scarcity or opportunity cost;
- produce visible consequences;
- interact with several existing systems;
- support more than one viable strategy;
- avoid becoming a disconnected “v2” subsystem.

### 6. Content architecture

Define the required content ecosystem:

- flagship questline;
- supporting quest/event arcs;
- major encounters;
- locations and location-state changes;
- factions and internal faction conflicts;
- named characters and survivor roles;
- new or repurposed items;
- recipes, tools, or infrastructure;
- radio fragments;
- journal entries;
- letters, manifests, ledgers, technical logs, or evidence artifacts;
- environmental storytelling;
- failure states;
- alternate resolutions.

Do not produce shallow lists. Explain how the content interacts.

### 7. Flagship scenario

Fully design at least one flagship scenario for the year.

Include:

- opening trigger;
- location;
- actors;
- material problem;
- conflicting information;
- player preparation;
- available choices;
- resource costs;
- immediate consequences;
- delayed consequences;
- faction/relationship effects;
- future re-entry conditions;
- failure state;
- representative radio, record, or journal material;
- how the scenario changes later years.

### 8. Character pressure

For every major character introduced or reused, define:

- role and competence;
- material vulnerability;
- relationship network;
- faction connection;
- work or shelter contribution;
- medical or psychological pressure;
- personal objective;
- what they know;
- what they misunderstand;
- what they may hide;
- what happens if they leave, die, retire, defect, or are discredited;
- possible long-term epilogue relevance.

Characters must create systemic pressure, not merely deliver dialogue.

### 9. Location design

Every new or substantially expanded location must have:

- material identity;
- environmental condition;
- travel cost;
- danger and radiation profile;
- resource identity;
- controlling or contesting faction;
- unique mechanical pressure;
- encounter pool;
- quest hooks;
- radio/journal/evidence clues;
- state changes after player intervention;
- different future return conditions;
- place-specific loot or materials;
- implementation/data hooks.

### 10. Economy and balance

For every new resource, item, service, treatment, or infrastructure capability, define:

- source;
- sink;
- production rate;
- consumption rate;
- opportunity cost;
- labor cost;
- travel cost;
- price/trade implications;
- faction access;
- scarcity behavior;
- failure consequences;
- whether it changes early, middle, or late game balance.

Do not solve scarcity by simply adding more loot.

### 11. Persistent state

List state that must persist beyond the pack:

- numeric values;
- flags;
- faction standing;
- relationship changes;
- location states;
- infrastructure conditions;
- evidence ownership;
- radio availability;
- journal history;
- survivor status;
- medical or radiation history;
- succession or endgame flags.

Avoid meter bloat. Explain why each state variable is necessary.

### 12. Implementation contract

Even though this is a design task, map the pack to:

- Core rules and DTOs;
- JSON catalogs;
- proposed `snake_case` IDs;
- loaders and validation;
- events;
- tick/time integration;
- RNG/determinism;
- Godot host sessions;
- UI/panels;
- radio/journal/evidence presentation;
- assets and audio;
- save envelope version;
- migration/default behavior;
- checksum requirements;
- tests;
- data-integrity checks;
- intentionally out-of-scope work.

## 8. Faction Design

Do not solve faction complexity by inventing dozens of new factions.

First inspect existing faction IDs and aliases. Determine whether apparently different names represent one faction, rival branches, historical names, or unresolved canon.

Every significant faction should have:

- systemic role;
- resource leverage;
- access rules;
- trade or price implications;
- internal disagreement;
- noncombat power;
- relationship to records and evidence;
- relationship to infrastructure;
- radio/journal presence;
- possible cooperation;
- possible coercion;
- possible failure or fragmentation;
- effect on future years;
- effect on endings.

Factions should be capable of being useful, harmful, necessary, compromised, or internally divided at the same time.

## 10. Endings and Epilogue Design

The tenth year must not end with a single final boss, magical solution, or binary “save/fail” screen.

Design a layered ending and epilogue system based on accumulated consequences.

Possible outcome dimensions include:

- who remains alive;
- who is remembered;
- who controls water;
- whether the shelter remains open;
- whether the region reconnects;
- whether records are trusted;
- whether knowledge is preserved;
- whether medicine becomes public or private;
- whether factions consolidate or fragment;
- whether succession succeeds;
- whether the community leaves, disperses, or stays;
- what damage was accepted to preserve continuity;
- what truths were buried;
- what obligations survive the player.

Endings should be evaluations of the player’s decade of decisions, not a final button choice.

Include:

- ending families;
- contradictory outcomes;
- partial victories;
- quiet failures;
- unresolved legacies;
- survivor epilogues;
- faction epilogues;
- location epilogues;
- institutional epilogues;
- records or radio material that reframes the ending.

## 11. Literary and Presentation Requirements

Write with disciplined literary intelligence.

Use:

- precise physical detail;
- bureaucratic language;
- subtext;
- silence;
- damaged objects;
- practical routines;
- incomplete testimony;
- conflicting accounts;
- emotionally restrained dialogue;
- consequences that arrive later;
- recurring motifs of ash, water, paper, heat, names, doors, maintenance, and memory.

For each year, include a small representative sample of:

- one radio fragment;
- one ledger, technical note, or record;
- one journal entry;
- one human scene or encounter premise.

These are tonal samples, not substitutes for mechanics.

Do not write thousands of lines of dialogue or a novel. Every prose sample must demonstrate how the player encounters information and how that information affects play.

## 13. Canon and Uncertainty Rules

Maintain an explicit canon ledger with these labels:

- `OBSERVED`: directly supported by current source, data, tests, or configuration.
- `INFERRED`: likely based on multiple signals but not proven.
- `PROPOSED`: newly designed expansion content.
- `UNCERTAIN`: unresolved runtime, chronology, identity, or integration question.
- `CUT`: attractive idea deliberately rejected because it harms tone, scope, or coherence.

Never silently canonize:

- faction aliases;
- unused catalog content;
- old expansion plans;
- historical test results;
- unverified campaign reachability;
- legacy Unity behavior;
- assumed survivor relationships;
- assumed ending access.

## 15. Final Quality Gate

Before completing the response, audit the expansion against these questions:

- Could this expansion be pasted into another survival game unchanged?
- Does every year have a distinct systemic identity?
- Does every major choice involve competing legitimate needs?
- Are scarcity, maintenance, logistics, records, and consequences central?
- Are existing ASHFALL expansions reused meaningfully?
- Are faction identities handled conservatively?
- Are characters mechanically and narratively consequential?
- Do locations have material and systemic identity?
- Does information behave like a resource?
- Are consequences visible and persistent?
- Does the expansion avoid power-fantasy escalation?
- Are new resources justified economically?
- Are proposed canon and repository facts clearly separated?
- Is the ten-year scope decomposed into reviewable vertical slices?
- Could a future coding agent implement the design without inventing missing architecture?
- Does Year 10 evaluate the entire decade rather than merely provide a final spectacle?
- Are some ideas deliberately cut because they are shallow, generic, or impossible to integrate?

Reject any section that consists primarily of:

- filler quest names;
- generic factions;
- arbitrary item lists;
- disconnected lore;
- repeated morality choices;
- spectacle without resource consequences;
- unexplained technology;
- unsupported canon claims;
- implementation promises without evidence;
- a giant rewrite plan with no checkpoints.

The final result should feel like a ten-year record of people trying to keep one another alive while deciding, year by year, what kind of civilization is worth the cost of maintaining.
